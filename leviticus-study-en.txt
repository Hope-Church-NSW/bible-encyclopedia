Book of Leviticus
Leviticus — וַיִּקְרָא — Vayyiqrā
6
1. The Name of the Book and Its Meaning

The Hebrew name of the Book of Leviticus is וַיִּקְרָא — Vayyiqrā, and its meaning is “And he called,” which is the first word with which the book begins: “And the Lord called Moses and spoke to him from the tent of meeting, saying.” This beginning is highly important because it reveals that the book does not begin with a human initiative or with a proposal from the priests, but begins with God’s call to Moses from within the tabernacle, as though the whole message proceeds from the presence of God himself.

As for the name commonly used in Western languages, it is Leviticus, which is derived from the Greek name connected with the Levites, and especially with matters related to worship, priesthood, sacrifices, and purity. But the name “Leviticus” may lead the reader to think that the book is intended for the Levites alone, whereas the truth is that the book addresses the whole congregation of Israel and sets before it the order of life in the presence of God. Therefore, its subject is much broader than the function of the tribe of Levi.

The central idea around which the book gathers all its themes is this: How can a sinful people live in the midst of the presence of a holy God? Exodus ended with the glory of the Lord dwelling in the tent of meeting, and now Leviticus comes to explain how the people are to deal with this presence, how worship is to take place, how sacrifice is to function, how the priest is to work, how the concept of impurity is to be addressed, and how the people are to live in holiness.

2. The author and the meaning of his name

Jewish and Christian tradition attribute the Book of Leviticus to Moses, the same tradition that attributes the Pentateuch to him. Moses appears in the book as the mediator who receives the word of the Lord and conveys it to the congregation and the priests, and the first chapters begin with phrases confirming that the Lord spoke to Moses and gave him the instructions concerning sacrifices, worship, and holiness.

The name Moses in Hebrew is מֹשֶׁה — Mosheh, and in the Book of Exodus it is connected with the idea of his being drawn out of the water, when Pharaoh’s daughter said, “Because I drew him out of the water.” Although scholars discuss the linguistic origin of the name, the biblical narrative itself gives the name an explanation connected with the story of Moses’ rescue, an explanation that became part of the biblical identity of the character.

The Bible does not provide a precise calendar date for Moses’ birth or death, and therefore it is not correct to assign a specific year without stating that it is only an estimate. The fixed piece of information is that he died at the age of 120 according to Deuteronomy 34:7, and the Books of Exodus and Leviticus place his ministry in the wilderness period after the exodus from Egypt and before entry into the promised land.

3. For Whom Was the Book of Leviticus Written?

The book was directed to the congregation of Israel, which had now become connected with the tabernacle, the covenant, and worship; therefore, it is not a book for priests only. It is true that the priests and Levites occupy an important place in its content, but the laws concerning holiness, purity, festivals, food, social relationships, vows, and other matters touch the life of the whole congregation.

The book may be viewed as a guide for a people who had come out of Egypt and been freed from Pharaoh’s authority, but who now stood before an entirely different authority: the authority of the holy God. Exodus gave Israel freedom from slavery, but Leviticus explains that biblical freedom does not mean that a person does whatever he wishes, but that he lives in a right relationship with the God who redeemed him.

4. Why Was the Book Written?

If the Book of Exodus explains how God saved Israel from Egypt, then Leviticus explains how Israel is to live after salvation. Salvation created a covenant relationship, and God’s presence in the midst of the congregation created the necessity of an order that defines how to approach God and how to deal with sin, impurity, and worship.

The book wants to establish firmly in Israel’s mind that God is not an ordinary god who can be approached according to human desires, but is the Holy One. Therefore, sacrifices, priesthood, purity, festivals, and commandments all appear within one framework: the holiness of God and the necessity of the holiness of his people.

And the verse that summarizes the heart of the book is:

“You shall be holy, for I am holy”
(Leviticus 11:44).

Here, holiness is not merely a collection of external rules, but a reflection of God’s own identity.

5. The Relationship between Exodus and Leviticus

The end of Exodus and the beginning of Leviticus should not be read separately, because there is a clear theological progression between them. In Exodus, the tabernacle is constructed and the glory of the Lord fills it; then Leviticus begins within this same context, as the Lord calls Moses and speaks to him from the tent of meeting.

This means that the question posed by Exodus is:

How does God dwell among his people?

Whereas the question answered by Leviticus is:

How can the people live in the presence of this holy God?

Thus Leviticus becomes an essential part of the story of redemption, because God’s presence among the people is not merely a privilege, but carries the responsibility of holiness, worship, and obedience.

6. The General Division of the Book

The book of Leviticus can be divided into interconnected theological units, showing that it is not a collection of unrelated laws, but an integrated structure that begins with approaching God and ends with covenant life.

7. The Offerings — Leviticus 1–7

The book begins with five principal types of offerings and sacrifices that appear in the system of worship: the burnt offering, the grain offering, the peace offering, the sin offering, and the guilt offering. Each type has a different function and context; therefore, the offerings should not be reduced to a single idea, as though they all served the same purpose.

The burnt offering, for example, is associated with the offering that ascends entirely upon the altar, whereas the peace offering appears in the context of fellowship and thanksgiving, and the sin offering and guilt offering address the reality of sin, transgression, and their consequences. Through these systems, Israel learns that sin is not a minor matter that can be ignored, and that approaching God requires a genuine resolution to the problem of guilt and impurity.

Here the concept of blood appears as a central element in atonement, and Leviticus 17:11 explains that the life is in the blood and that God has given it upon the altar to make atonement for the lives. This concept would become one of the most important backgrounds needed for understanding the New Testament’s discussion of the blood of Christ.

8. The Priesthood and the Consecration of Aaron and His Sons

The book then moves to the consecration of Aaron and his sons for the priesthood, and here we see that God is the one who determines who serves before him and how they serve. The priesthood is not a human privilege that a person chooses for himself, but a calling within the covenant system.

Aaron appears as the high priest, while his sons become priests serving alongside him. The priestly system begins with sacred garments, anointing, consecration, and sacrifices, because the person who approaches the sanctuary must be consecrated for the service of God.

This introduces an important theme in biblical theology: mediation. The people do not enter every aspect of the sanctuary in the same manner; rather, specific roles exist for the priests, and the high priest would later become an essential background for understanding Hebrews’ message about Christ as the supreme High Priest.

9. Nadab and Abihu — The Danger of Approaching God Without His Command

In Leviticus 10, one of the most awe-inspiring events in the book takes place, when Nadab and Abihu, the sons of Aaron, offer unauthorized fire before the Lord, which he had not commanded them to offer; then fire goes out from before the Lord and consumes them.

The incident reveals that God’s holiness is not a theoretical idea, and that worship is not an arena in which people may do whatever they wish and then ask God to accept it. God requires that he be treated as holy by those who approach him, and this is clearly demonstrated in Moses’ response after the death of Aaron’s two sons.

The theological lesson here is of profound importance: grace does not nullify God’s holiness, and approaching God is a great privilege, but it is also a great responsibility.

10. The Clean and the Unclean — Leviticus 11–15

These chapters address a wide range of purity laws concerning foods, animals, childbirth, skin diseases, and bodily discharges. These texts require careful reading, because the concept of “uncleanness” in Leviticus does not always correspond to the concept of moral sin.

Some states of uncleanness were temporary ritual conditions that did not necessarily mean that a person had committed a sin, such as certain illnesses, discharges, or particular bodily conditions. The purpose of the purity system was to teach Israel that there is a distinction between the holy and the common, and between the clean and the unclean, and that approaching the sanctuary requires an appropriate condition according to the system God gave.

Thus we see that Leviticus cultivates in Israel a continual awareness that God’s presence among the community makes daily life itself connected with holiness.

11. The Day of Atonement — Leviticus 16

Chapter sixteen is considered the theological heart of Leviticus, because it presents the system of the Day of Atonement — יוֹם הַכִּפֻּרִים — Yom Kippur. On this day, the high priest enters the Most Holy Place in an extraordinary ritual context and offers sacrifices for himself and for the people.

On this day, two goats appear within the atonement ritual: one is offered as a sacrifice to the Lord, while the other is associated with removing the sins of the community and sending them into the wilderness. The complete image becomes a powerful expression of two interconnected ideas: atonement for sin and the removal of sin from the community.

This chapter is extremely important for understanding the message of Hebrews, because the New Testament will compare the ministry of the high priests in the tabernacle with Christ’s ministry as High Priest, explaining that Christ does not enter an earthly sanctuary with the blood of an animal, but offers himself in a definitive redemptive work.

12. The Holiness Code — Leviticus 17–22

From chapter 17 onward, the book moves to a broad collection of laws often called the Holiness Code. Here holiness moves from the altar ritual into daily life, and laws appear concerning blood, adultery, family relationships, injustice, love of one’s neighbor, priests, and sacrifices.

The recurring statement in this section is that Israel must be distinct from the nations surrounding it because the God who chose Israel is holy. Holiness is not merely physical separation, but a complete way of life concerned with the body, family, sexuality, work, money, the vulnerable, and worship.

Among the most beautiful ethical declarations in this section is:

“You shall love your neighbor as yourself”
(Leviticus 19:18).

This command would occupy a profoundly central place in Jesus’ teaching, as he would place it alongside love for God at the heart of the greatest commandment.

13. The Feasts and Seasons — Leviticus 23

Chapter twenty-three presents a list of the sacred seasons and feasts, including the Sabbath, Passover, the Feast of Unleavened Bread, the firstfruits of the harvest, the Feast of Weeks, the Feast of Trumpets, the Day of Atonement, and the Feast of Booths.

These feasts were not merely social occasions, but means of forming a collective memory connected with God’s deeds. Passover takes the people back to the Exodus, the firstfruits connect worship with the seasons of harvest, the Day of Atonement focuses on purification and atonement, and the Feast of Booths recalls the people’s life in the wilderness.

Thus time itself becomes part of worship; the people remember God not only in the sanctuary, but also remember his deeds on specific days and seasons repeated through the generations.

14. The Sabbath and the Jubilee

In Leviticus 25, the idea of the Sabbath for the land appears, followed by the year of Jubilee. This is one of the most distinctive social and economic ideas in the law. The land is not treated as an absolute human possession, because the Lord says that it belongs to him, and Israel lives in it as strangers and sojourners.

The Jubilee establishes a system that restores certain conditions of land ownership, servitude, and debt, and prevents exploitation from accumulating without end. This idea reflects the principle that God is the true owner of the land and the people.

Therefore, Leviticus does not speak only about worship inside the tabernacle, but also connects holiness with economics, ownership, and social justice.

15. Blessings and Curses — Leviticus 26

The book’s main section concludes with a declaration of the consequences of obedience and disobedience. If the people walk in God’s statutes, goodness, peace, and blessing will come; if they reject the covenant, severe consequences will follow, extending to the loss of the land and exile.

This section is extremely important for understanding Israel’s subsequent history, because when the prophets interpret exile and judgment, they return to the covenant logic that appears here. Consequently, Leviticus does not stand apart from the books of the Kings and the Prophets, but provides a theological framework that helps explain what will happen to Israel centuries later.

Yet Leviticus does not end with the idea of judgment alone, but leaves the door open to confession, return, and remembrance of the covenant, because God’s faithfulness to the covenant remains present even amid the discipline of the people.

16. Vows and Consecration — Leviticus 27

The final chapter addresses vows and the things that a person consecrates to the Lord, while regulating how they are to be handled. This brings us back to an important principle: what is devoted to God is not ordinary.

The book therefore teaches Israel that dealings with holy things should not be random or based solely on momentary emotion, but that people bear responsibility for what they offer to God. Thus the book ends as it began: with the holy God and the human being who needs to learn how to approach him and live before him.

17. The Main Figures in the Book
Moses

He is the mediator who receives God’s word and conveys it to the people, and therefore occupies a fundamental position in the structure of the book.

Aaron

He is the high priest, the central human figure in the priestly system, and becomes a symbol of priestly ministry in Israel.

Nadab and Abihu

Their story warns against treating God’s holiness lightly or approaching him in a manner he has not commanded.

Eleazar and Ithamar

Aaron’s sons who continued in the ministry after the death of Nadab and Abihu, appearing within the continuation of the priesthood.

Yet the true central figure in the book is not any of these, but the holy God who determines the system of worship and declares the conditions for life in his presence.

18. The Most Important Message Presented by Leviticus

The central message can be summarized in the statement:

The people whom God redeemed and called into the covenant must live in holiness before the holy God.

This message prevents us from reading Leviticus as a book of rigid laws. The sacrifices, priesthood, purity, festivals, holiness, and Jubilee are all parts of one question: How can sinful human beings live in the presence of God?

The answer presented by the book is that approaching God requires atonement, mediation, purity, obedience, and holiness.

19. Christ in Leviticus

Leviticus is one of the richest books of the Old Testament for understanding the work of Christ, not because every detail in it should be turned into a direct symbol, but because the system of sacrifices, priesthood, and atonement establishes the theological framework that the New Testament uses to explain the cross.

Christ appears in the light of Leviticus as the perfect sacrifice, the great high priest, and the final atonement. Therefore, the Letter to the Hebrews relies extensively on the vocabulary of the high priest, the sanctuary, blood, and atonement. Likewise, when John the Baptist points to Jesus as the Lamb of God, he places the reader within a biblical world in which the ideas of the lamb, sacrifice, and redemption were connected to the history of salvation.

From this perspective, the cross can be understood not as an isolated event that suddenly appeared in the first century, but as the culmination of a long biblical trajectory that began with the problem of sin, sacrifice, and atonement in Genesis, then took a clear form in Exodus and Leviticus, and subsequently acquired prophetic and theological dimensions until it was fulfilled in Christ.

20. The Historical Period of the Events in the Book

The events of Leviticus take place during the wilderness period after the exodus from Egypt and before entering the land of Canaan, with the tent of meeting in the vicinity of Mount Sinai as their central setting. This means that the book belongs to a relatively short period compared with Genesis, yet it presents a vast quantity of instructions that would shape Israel’s religious and social life for many centuries.

Determining the absolute year in which the events of Leviticus occurred depends on the chronological model of the exodus adopted by the researcher. If the date of the exodus differs, the approximate date of the events that took place in the wilderness differs as well. Therefore, we will retain relative chronological expressions when there is no necessity to give a specific year.

21. The Date of Composition

Jewish and Christian tradition connects the book with Moses and the wilderness period, whereas modern critical schools hold that the priestly and legal materials in Leviticus passed through stages of collection, writing, and editing before the text reached its final form. This issue is related to broader discussions concerning the formation of the Torah, and therefore one date should not be presented as settled for all scholars.

More important for theological study is that the text in its present form presents an integrated system of worship, holiness, and covenant. This system became an important foundation for understanding Israelite worship and later became a primary background for interpreting the work of Christ in the New Testament.

22. Why Is Leviticus Essential for Understanding the Bible?

The modern reader may feel that Leviticus is very distant from contemporary concerns, but it is in fact one of the most important books for understanding words such as atonement, sacrifice, blood, uncleanness, purification, priest, high priest, Holy of Holies, holiness, burnt offering, Passover, and Jubilee.

If we remove Leviticus from our understanding of the Bible, large portions of the Letter to the Hebrews and the message of Christ concerning sacrifice and atonement will become less clear. Many New Testament concepts related to holiness, blood, atonement, and approaching God are also based on a theological world that was powerfully shaped in this book.

🔑 Summary of Leviticus

Hebrew name: וַיִּקְרָא — Vayyiqrā — “And he called.”
Common name: Leviticus — associated with the Levites and the priesthood.
Traditional author: Moses.
Moses' birth: historically unknown with precision.
His death: 120 years, according to Deuteronomy 34:7.
Audience: The congregation of Israel, with specific instructions for the priests.
Central location: The tent of meeting in the wilderness.
Theme: Holiness, atonement, and worship.
Major events: The consecration of the priests, the death of Nadab and Abihu, the sacrificial system, the Day of Atonement, the festivals, and the Jubilee.
Principal figures: Moses, Aaron, Nadab, Abihu, Eleazar, and Ithamar.
Theological key: The holy God dwells among his people; therefore, his people must be holy.
