Paul's Second Letter to the Corinthians
1. The Epistle’s Name and the Meaning of the Name

It is known as the Second Epistle of the Apostle Paul to the Corinthians. It is a deeply personal and pastoral epistle that reveals Paul’s heart as a shepherd and apostle and explains the nature of Christian ministry amid pain, rejection, and weakness.

Whereas First Corinthians addressed the church’s problems, divisions, and conduct, Second Corinthians reveals more clearly the person of the apostle himself, the sufferings of ministry, the authority of the apostolic message, the nature of the new covenant, reconciliation, giving, and the power of God in weakness.

2. The Author and His Character

The author is the Apostle Paul, who mentions Timothy with him in the opening of the epistle. Paul’s character appears here in a more personal manner than in most of his epistles, as he speaks of his sorrows, the afflictions of his ministry, the rejection he received from some, and his defense of his apostleship.

Paul does not defend himself out of pride, but because the rejection of his apostleship threatened the church’s confidence in the gospel it had received from him.

3. To Whom Was the Epistle Written?

It was written to the church of God in Corinth, together with all the saints in Achaia. It comes within the context of a complex relationship between Paul and the church, following previous problems, corrections, visits, and correspondence.

The epistle reveals that part of the church had begun to recover its proper attitude toward Paul, while others continued to criticize his authority and ministry, prompting him to clarify the nature of his apostleship.

4. The Reason for Writing the Epistle

The epistle combines several purposes: encouraging believers, explaining the reason for certain actions by Paul, defending his apostleship, addressing the matter of repentance and reconciliation, and encouraging the church to complete its collection for the poor believers in Jerusalem.

Paul also confronts the “false apostles” who entered the church and presented a different model of ministry based on appearances and human strength.

5. The Time and Place of the Epistle’s Composition

The epistle was probably written around 55–56 AD, after First Corinthians, in the context of Paul’s ministry in Macedonia or while traveling through that region.

The chronological details should be treated as a historical reconstruction based on the Book of Acts and Paul’s epistles, not as dates explicitly specified by the text.

6. The Main Theme

The central theme is Christian ministry, in which God’s power is manifested amid human weakness.

The epistle redefines apostolic power: it lies not in eloquence, appearance, or human authority, but in faithfulness to Christ, enduring suffering, bearing the death of Christ, and serving others.

The thought reaches its climax in the Lord’s declaration to Paul:

“MY GRACE IS SUFFICIENT FOR YOU, FOR MY POWER IS MADE PERFECT IN WEAKNESS.”
(2 Corinthians 12: 9).

7. God, the God of All Comfort

Paul opens the epistle with a profound declaration about God:

“BLESSED BE GOD, THE FATHER OF OUR LORD JESUS CHRIST, THE FATHER OF MERCIES AND GOD OF ALL COMFORT.”
(2 Corinthians 1: 3).

Afflictions in the apostle’s life do not mean God’s absence; rather, they become an arena for experiencing His comfort, so that the servant may comfort others with the comfort he himself has received.

8. Suffering and Ministry

Paul reveals that apostolic ministry does not mean a life free from suffering. He experienced affliction, persecution, danger, and despair even of life itself.

Yet suffering takes on new meaning when it is borne in fellowship with Christ. The servant carries the death of Jesus in his body so that the life of Jesus may also be manifested in him.

Thus, suffering is transformed from a sign of failure into fellowship in Christ’s path and testimony to His power.

9. Christ Is God’s “Yes”

Paul declares that the promises of God in Christ are “Yes” and “Amen.”

Christ is the center of God’s faithfulness to the covenant and the promises; therefore, the believer’s confidence does not rest on changing circumstances, but on the person of Christ, in whom God’s promises are fulfilled.

10. Ministry and the New Covenant

One of the epistle’s most important themes is Paul’s discussion of ministers of the new covenant.

He compares the ministry of the covenant written on tablets of stone with the ministry of the Spirit, which writes God’s work in human hearts. This does not mean that the Old Covenant is evil or without glory; rather, Paul explains that the glory of the New Covenant is greater because it is connected with the work of the Spirit and with Christ.

He says:

“WHO ALSO MADE US SUFFICIENT AS MINISTERS OF A NEW COVENANT, NOT OF THE LETTER BUT OF THE SPIRIT; FOR THE LETTER KILLS, BUT THE SPIRIT GIVES LIFE.”
(2 Corinthians 3: 6).

11. The Glory of Christ

Paul connects the glory of the New Covenant with the revelation of Christ’s glory. The veil that obscured understanding in the reading of Moses is removed in Christ.

As believers behold the glory of the Lord, they are gradually transformed into His image.

Here one of the epistle’s deepest concepts of sanctification emerges: the believer is not transformed by self-effort alone, but by continually beholding the glory of the Lord and through the work of the Spirit within him.

12. The Gospel of Christ and the Veil

If the gospel is veiled, it is not because Christ is unclear, but because “the god of this age” has blinded the minds of unbelievers.

But the believer sees in Christ the image of God; therefore, the knowledge of Christ becomes a revelation of the knowledge of God’s glory.

13. Treasure in Earthen Vessels

Paul presents one of the most beautiful images of Christian ministry:

“BUT WE HAVE THIS TREASURE IN EARTHEN VESSELS, THAT THE EXCELLENCE OF THE POWER MAY BE OF GOD AND NOT OF US.”
(2 Corinthians 4: 7).

The gospel is a treasure, and the servant is an earthen vessel. Human weakness does not negate the value of the message; rather, it makes God’s power more evident.

14. Death Works in the Servant and Life in Others

Paul says that they carry in the body the dying of the Lord Jesus, so that the life of Jesus may also be manifested in their bodies.

True Christian ministry involves self-giving: the servant dies to his comfort, glory, and rights so that the life of Christ may reach others.

This makes ministry a practical extension of the logic of the cross.

15. The Outer Person and the Inner Person

Paul distinguishes between the outer person, who is perishing, and the inner person, who is being renewed day by day.

In this way, he offers a Christian perspective on suffering: the body may weaken, and circumstances may change, but the inner person can be continually renewed by the power of God.

Hope does not stop at the present, but looks toward eternal glory.

16. Our Heavenly Dwelling

Paul speaks of the present body as a temporary earthly dwelling and declares the believer’s hope in the heavenly dwelling.

This does not mean despising the body or rejecting creation, but expresses the expectation of salvation’s completion in the resurrection, when the believer puts on what is heavenly and eternal.

17. The Judgment Seat of Christ

Paul declares that all will appear before the judgment seat of Christ, so that each may receive according to what he has done in the body.

This affirms the believer’s responsibility for his life and deeds, without nullifying the basis of salvation by grace.

Grace does not mean the absence of judgment upon deeds; rather, it makes the believer’s life a response to Christ who saved him.

18. Reconciliation

One of the epistle’s most important texts is Paul’s declaration:

“THEREFORE, WE ARE AMBASSADORS FOR CHRIST, AS THOUGH GOD WERE PLEADING THROUGH US. WE IMPLORE YOU ON CHRIST’S BEHALF: BE RECONCILED TO GOD.”
(2 Corinthians 5: 20).

Reconciliation is at the heart of the gospel message: in Christ, God took the initiative to reconcile humanity to Himself and gave the church the ministry of proclaiming this reconciliation to the world.

19. “He who knew no sin”

The concept of reconciliation reaches its climax in:

“For He made Him who knew no sin to be sin for us, that we might become the righteousness of God in Him.”
(2 Corinthians 5: 21).

This is among the greatest New Testament statements concerning Christ's saving work, for it presents a profound saving exchange: the righteous Christ bore what was ours, so that we might receive what is associated with His righteousness.

20. The grace of God

Paul warns believers not to receive the grace of God in vain, because true grace produces a holy life.

Thus, the epistle does not set grace against holiness; rather, grace itself moves the believer toward holiness and obedience.

21. Paul's fatherly heart

In the epistle, Paul appears as a spiritual father who suffers for his children. He does not merely want to prevail in a theological debate; he wants to see the church walking in the truth.

The epistle reveals that true apostolic authority is not domination, but service, love, and self-giving for others.

22. The defense of apostleship

Paul confronted people who measured ministry by outward appearances: eloquence, letters, personal presence, recommendations, and external displays.

Paul, however, sets forth a different standard: faithfulness to Christ, steadfastness in the gospel, endurance of suffering, and the fruit of ministry.

For this reason, the epistle becomes a defense of a concept of ministry shaped by the cross.

23. “The power of God in weakness”

This theme reaches its climax in Paul's discussion of the “thorn in the flesh.” Paul asked the Lord three times for it to depart from him, but the Lord did not remove the weakness; instead, He gave him a greater promise:

“My grace is sufficient for you, for My strength is made perfect in weakness.”

Christian strength, therefore, does not become the absence of weakness, but the experience of the sufficiency of grace amid weakness.

24. Giving

Paul devotes an important section to collecting contributions for needy believers in Jerusalem.

He does not present giving as a religious tax, but as an act of grace, love, and fellowship among the churches.

He grounds this in the example of Christ:

“For you know the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, that though He was rich, yet for your sakes He became poor, that you through His poverty might become rich.”
(2 Corinthians 8: 9).

25. Giving and grace

Paul connects giving with the grace of God and affirms that God is able to make the believer self-sufficient so that he may abound in every good work.

Christian giving is not based on pressure, but on grace that transforms a person's relationship to money, possessions, and others.

26. Spiritual warfare

Paul declares that the weapons of our warfare are not fleshly, but are mighty through God for the pulling down of strongholds.

The battle here is not against people, but against ideas that exalt themselves against the knowledge of God.

This gives apostolic ministry a spiritual and intellectual dimension at the same time.

27. False apostles

Paul warns against those who pretend to be apostles of Christ while presenting a different gospel, a different spirit, and a different Jesus.

He reveals that the danger is not always external persecution; it may come through the distortion of the gospel from within the Christian community.

28. Paul's boasting

Paul uses the language of “boasting” ironically; if he is compelled to defend his apostleship, he boasts not merely of his achievements, but also recounts his weakness, sufferings, and dangers.

Thus Paul overturns the measure of glory: the true servant does not make his personal strength the center of his ministry, but allows grace to be displayed in his weakness.

29. Christ in Second Corinthians

Christ is the Savior and Reconciler, the image of God, the glorified Lord, the example of self-giving, and the center of the New Covenant.

The servant cannot understand his ministry apart from the crucified and risen Christ; just as Christ bore the cross for others, the apostle bears the sufferings of ministry for the body of Christ.

30. The major movement of the epistle

The epistle begins with the God of all comfort, who sustains Paul amid affliction. It then reveals that suffering does not conflict with the message, but can become a means of displaying the life of Christ in the servant. Paul then moves to explain the glory of the New Covenant and the work of the Spirit, who removes the veil and transforms the believer into the image of Christ. He further explains that the divine treasure is placed in earthen vessels so that the power of God, rather than of humanity, may be displayed. From there, he arrives at the heart of the gospel: God was in Christ reconciling the world to Himself, and gave the church the ministry of reconciliation, because Christ, who knew no sin, became sin for us so that we might become the righteousness of God in Him. Paul then explains the nature of apostolic ministry and confronts the false apostles, who measure ministry by appearance and human strength, while he presents his sufferings and weakness as evidence that true power comes from Christ. In the section on giving, grace appears in practical form: the believer who has experienced the riches of Christ learns to give generously to others. Finally, the epistle reaches its climax in the “thorn in the flesh,” where Paul learns that the grace of Christ is sufficient and that the power of God is made perfect in weakness. Thus, the epistle moves from affliction to comfort, from weakness to strength, from the cross to ministry, from reconciliation with God to reconciliation with others, and from the grace of Christ to a life of giving.

31. Links with the Old Testament

The epistle rests upon an Old Testament background, especially in its discussion of Moses, the veil, and the glory of the covenant. It uses the language of “covenant,” “Spirit,” and “glory” to explain the transition from a covenant written on tablets to a covenant that works in hearts.

The concepts of glory, righteousness, temple, holiness, people, and promises likewise extend in the epistle from their Old Testament background to their fulfillment in Christ.

32. Encyclopedic Summary

Second Corinthians reveals the aspect not easily seen in the outward appearance of ministry: the servant himself must be shaped by the cross. Paul does not present the Christian message as a path to personal glory, but as a path on which the servant bears the sufferings of Christ, experiences His comfort, and proclaims the power of His grace. At the heart of the epistle, Christ appears as the foundation of reconciliation, the Spirit as the source of transformation, grace as the power that sustains the weak, and love as the foundation of giving and ministry. Therefore, the greatest paradox in the epistle is that human weakness does not mark the end of God's work, but can become the place where God's power is displayed most clearly.
