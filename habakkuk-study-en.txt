Introduction to the Book of Habakkuk

The central idea of the book: Habakkuk differs from many prophetic books because the prophet does not stand only before the people to proclaim God's word to them; rather, he stands before God and asks him about the problem of evil. The book begins with the voice of a person who sees injustice and violence and does not understand why God allows it to continue. The question then becomes more complex when God declares that he will use the Chaldeans/Babylonians to discipline Judah, and Habakkuk asks: How does God use a nation more wicked to punish a nation less wicked? Thus, the book moves from “How long?” to “the righteous shall live by faith,” and the prophet ends not by understanding all the mysteries of God's providence, but by trusting in him even when he sees neither fruit nor harvest. Therefore, the movement of the book may be summarized as: question → perplexity → divine declaration → deeper question → waiting → faith → joy in God.

1. Name of the book and its meaning

The name of the book is Habakkuk — חֲבַקּוּק — Ḥăvaqqûq. It is a Hebrew name associated with the root חבק — ḥ-b-q, which carries the meaning “to embrace” or “to clasp.” Therefore, the name is traditionally understood to mean “embrace” or “one who embraces,” although the precise linguistic derivation of the name is debated. From a literary and spiritual perspective, the name conveys a beautiful image that may help in reading the book: the prophet begins by wrestling with difficult questions, but ends in a state of dependence on and trust in God despite unchanged circumstances.

2. Who Is Habakkuk?

The Bible provides little information about Habakkuk as a person; we do not know his father's name, his tribe, or his city. Indeed, the book begins directly with the phrase, “The oracle which Habakkuk the prophet saw,” as though the focus were not on the man's biography but on the message he bore. This makes Habakkuk a distinctive figure among the prophets, because in this book we see a very clear dialogue between the prophet and God.

3. The Year of Habakkuk's Birth and Death

The Bible does not record the year of Habakkuk's birth or the year of his death. Nor is there confirmed historical information that permits the precise dating of his life. However, the book's content helps us place his ministry in the period when Babylon/the Chaldeans were rising as a major military power, before the complete fall of Judah and Jerusalem.

4. Date of writing the book

The historical indications within the book point to the period of the Chaldeans' rise; therefore, many scholars consider it likely that Habakkuk ministered in the late seventh century before Christ, most likely in the period preceding Babylon's great invasion of Judah. This period was full of major political transformations, especially after the fall of Assyria and the rise of Babylon. Thus, the question Habakkuk raises was directly connected to the question confronting God's people: Why does God permit a violent pagan power to triumph?

5. To Whom Did Habakkuk Write?

The book does not address the nations primarily; rather, it proceeds from a crisis within God's own people. The prophet sees violence, injustice, and corruption in Judah, and wonders why the Lord does not intervene. Then the divine answer comes: the Chaldeans will come for discipline. Therefore, it may be said that the book addresses God's people living amid a moral, political, and spiritual crisis who need to learn to trust in God's justice and providence.

6. Reason for writing the book

The principal reason is its treatment of the question of evil and divine sovereignty. How does God permit injustice? Why is judgment delayed? And if He will judge injustice, why does He use a people more unjust as an instrument of discipline? The book does not offer a brief philosophical answer to every problem of evil, but it offers something deeper: it returns people to trust in God's character even when they cannot understand every detail of His work.

7. Outline of the Book of Habakkuk

The book consists of three chapters and may be divided into three principal sections. The first chapter presents Habakkuk's first complaint and God's announcement that He will use the Chaldeans; then the second chapter presents the prophet's second complaint and God's answer, at the center of which is the great statement, “But the righteous shall live by faith.” The third chapter is a prayer and hymn in which the crisis is transformed from a question about God into deep trust in God.

8. Habakkuk 1 — “How Long?”

The prophet begins with a deeply painful question:

“How long shall I cry, and You will not hear? I cry out to You of violence, and You will not save?”

This is not the prayer of an unbeliever, but the prayer of a prophet who believes in God yet cannot reconcile his faith with what he sees before his eyes. Thus, the book teaches us that an honest question before God is not necessarily a sign of the absence of faith.

9. The Problem Habakkuk Saw

The prophet mentions injustice, plunder, strife, contention, and perverted judgment. He does not live in a theoretical world, but sees a society in which justice has been paralyzed. Therefore, his first question is not, Why do natural disasters occur? Rather: Why does God allow moral and social injustice to continue?

10. God's First Answer

The Lord answers:

“Behold, I am raising up the Chaldeans, that bitter and hasty nation, which marches through the breadth of the earth, to possess dwelling places that are not theirs.”

Here comes the surprise. Habakkuk asked God to intervene against evil, but God declares that His intervention will come in a way the prophet did not expect. He will use Babylon as an instrument of judgment and discipline.

11. The Chaldeans

The Chaldeans represented the rising Babylonian power. The book describes them as a dreadful and terrifying nation, gathering captives like sand. This image makes Habakkuk's second question more difficult, because God does not use a weak and righteous power to discipline Judah, but a violent pagan power.

12. The Second Problem

After Habakkuk hears the answer, he does not say, “Now I understand.” Rather, he begins with a more difficult question:

“Why do You look on the treacherous and remain silent when the wicked swallows up one more righteous than he?”

Here we reach the heart of the book. The prophet can understand that God will hold Judah accountable, but he cannot understand how God can use Babylon, which is more violent, to execute this judgment.

13. The Holiness of God

Habakkuk declares:

“O Lord, You have ordained it for judgment; O Rock, You have established it for correction.”

Then it says:

“Your eyes are too pure to look upon evil, and You cannot look upon injustice.”

The prophet does not doubt God's holiness; rather, he begins from it in raising his question. This is a very important theological point: sometimes the question arises from faith itself, because a person knows that God is just and therefore finds it difficult to understand what he sees.

14. Standing at the Watchpost

At the beginning of the second chapter, Habakkuk says:

“I will stand at my watchpost and station myself on the rampart, and I will watch to see what He will say to me, and what I will answer concerning my complaint.”

This is a wonderful picture of spiritual waiting. The prophet is no longer merely crying out, but has become one waiting for the answer. Here begins the transition from reaction to faith, and from anxiety to expectation.

15. “Write the Vision”

The Lord says:

“Write the vision and make it plain upon tablets, that he who reads it may run.”

God desires the message to be clear and proclaimed. This indicates that revelation is not an obscure idea for an elite, but a word that must be written, preserved, and proclaimed. This principle also affirms the importance of preserving and transmitting God's word faithfully.

16. “Though It Tarry, Wait for It”

The Lord says:

“Though it tarry, wait for it; because it will surely come and will not delay.”

This statement addresses the problem of time from God's perspective. The prophet sees delay, but God sees the appointed time. Human beings desire an immediate answer, but God declares that there is an appointed time for accomplishing His purpose.

17. “But the Righteous Shall Live by Faith”

This is the most famous statement in the book:

“But the righteous shall live by faith.”

In the context of Habakkuk, faith means steadfastness and trust in God during a period in which circumstances remain difficult. The text does not say that the righteous live because they understand everything, but because they trust God even when they cannot explain everything.

18. The Significance of “Shall Live by Faith”

This statement later becomes a cornerstone in the New Testament. Paul uses it in Romans 1:17 and Galatians 3:11, and its substance is also related to Hebrews 10:38. Thus, Habakkuk's statement moves from the context of the Babylonian crisis to the heart of the Christian proclamation of life by faith.

19. Faith Is Not Denial of Reality

Habakkuk does not say that everything is well. Rather, he sees injustice, war, and invasion, and acknowledges them. Yet he reaches the point at which he can say: I do not understand everything, but I know whom I trust. This is a very important point in biblical faith, because faith is not the disregard of painful evidence, but trust in God despite its presence.

20. The Five Woes

The second chapter declares a series of woes against the oppressor. These woes reveal that Babylon itself is not beyond judgment. God used it for discipline, but it was not given permission to continue in evil. Thus, an important principle appears: God can use a nation to execute His judgment without justifying its sin.

21. The First Woe — Plunder

God condemns the one who gathers what is not his and loads himself with pledges. The empire that expands its borders by force and plunders peoples cannot retain its spoils forever. Divine justice will reverse the equation, so that a day will come when those who were plundered rise against the plunderer.

22. The Second Woe — Gain through Evil

God condemns the one who builds his house by injustice and sets his nest on high in order to place himself in a secure position. This reveals that wealth and status built upon the exploitation of others do not grant true security. True security does not come from the height of the palace, but from a right relationship with God.

23. The Third Woe — Violence

The Lord condemns the city that builds with blood and establishes itself by iniquity. Here the book returns to the same moral question: What happens when violence becomes a means of building civilization? The answer is that a structure founded on blood carries within itself judgment against itself.

24. The Fourth Woe — Humiliating Others

The book condemns the one who gives his neighbor drink through evil in order to expose his nakedness and exploit his weakness. This reflects how power can become the humiliation of others. God holds people accountable not only for killing and plunder, but also for insulting human beings and exploiting their weakness.

25. The Fifth Woe — Idolatry

The prophet declares:

“Woe to him who says to wood, ‘Awake!’ To a silent stone, ‘Arise!’”

Here the book moves from political injustice to a deeper spiritual root. The empire that worships idols made by its own hands cannot rival the living God. Then comes the great declaration:

“But the Lord is in His holy temple; let all the earth keep silence before Him.”

26. “Let All the Earth Keep Silence before Him”

This statement represents the climax of the second chapter. After all the political, military, and economic noise, God's voice comes, causing the whole earth to stand silent before Him. Thus, the book moves from the voice of empires to the sovereignty of God.

27. Habakkuk 3 — The Prayer

The third chapter differs in tone. It is a prayer and hymn that recalls God's past deeds and asks Him to revive His work in the present. The prophet begins from the memory of salvation, because remembering what God did in the past becomes the basis for confidence in what He will do in the future.

28. “In the Midst of the Years Revive Your Work”

Habakkuk says:

“O Lord, I have heard Your report and was afraid. O Lord, revive Your work in the midst of the years; in the midst of the years make it known. In wrath remember mercy.”

This is among the most beautiful prayers of the Old Testament. He does not merely ask God to remove the crisis, but asks Him to revive His work amid the crisis and to remember mercy even when the time of wrath comes.

29. Remembering God's Deeds

The prophet recalls images associated with God's coming forth and His display of power in history. This means that faith does not live on present feelings alone, but is nourished by the memory of God's previous deeds. When the future becomes obscure, the believer looks back to see God's faithfulness.

30. Fear and Hope

Habakkuk says that when he heard, his lips trembled and decay entered his bones; nevertheless, he waited for the day of distress. This reveals that faith does not mean the absence of fear. A person can be terrified by circumstances and at the same time trust in God.

31. The Climax of the Book

The book reaches one of the greatest statements of faith in the Bible:

“Though the fig tree does not blossom, nor fruit be on the vines; though the produce of the olive fail, and the fields yield no food; though the flock be cut off from the fold, and there be no cattle in the stalls, yet I will rejoice in the Lord; I will exult in the God of my salvation.”

Here Habakkuk reaches mature faith. He began by questioning the absence of God's intervention, and ends by rejoicing in God even if economic, agricultural, and political circumstances do not change.

32. “I rejoice in the LORD”

The statement does not say: I will rejoice because the harvest will return, or because the enemy will be defeated immediately. Rather, it says:

“Yet I will rejoice in the LORD; I will take joy in the God of my salvation.”

This represents a radical shift in the center of joy. Joy is no longer connected merely to what God gives, but has become connected to God himself.

33. “He makes my feet like the feet of deer”

Habakkuk concludes:

“The Lord GOD is my strength; he makes my feet like the feet of deer and makes me walk on my high places.”

Deer are able to move steadily through difficult and mountainous terrain. Therefore, the image indicates that God does not necessarily promise the prophet that he will remove the mountains, but that he will give him feet capable of walking over them.

34. Habakkuk and the Problem of Evil

This book is one of the Old Testament’s deepest treatments of the problem of evil. However, it does not attempt to provide a comprehensive philosophical explanation for all suffering. Instead, it presents three truths: God sees evil, God will judge evil, and human beings are called to faith while awaiting judgment.

35. Habakkuk and Divine Sovereignty

Even when God uses Babylon, Babylon does not become the ultimate decision-maker. God uses the nations, but he also judges them. Thus, history appears in the book as a sphere in which God acts without depriving human beings of their moral responsibility.

36. Habakkuk and Faith

Faith in Habakkuk is neither naïve faith nor psychological optimism. It is faith born through struggle, questions, and waiting. Therefore, “the righteous shall live by faith” means that a person’s relationship with God continues even when he cannot see the end of the road.

37. Habakkuk and Christ

The New Testament takes the statement “the righteous shall live by faith” and makes it an essential part of its explanation of the gospel. In Romans and Galatians, the statement is connected with the justification of a person by faith; in Hebrews, it is connected with steadfastness and not turning back. Thus, Habakkuk’s voice becomes part of the Christian proclamation of life founded on faith in God rather than reliance on self-righteousness.

38. Habakkuk and the Cross

From the perspective of the New Testament, Habakkuk’s principle appears more deeply in the cross. At its historical moment, the cross may have seemed as though evil had triumphed and Christ had been defeated, but the resurrection reveals that God was working at the heart of the scene. Thus, Habakkuk helps us understand an important biblical principle: God’s providence may appear mysterious in the moment, but this does not mean that God is absent from the event.

39. Habakkuk and the Church

The church sometimes lives in circumstances resembling Habakkuk’s question: Why does God allow evil? Why is intervention delayed? Why does injustice appear stronger? The book does not promise that every crisis will end immediately, but it provides a stronger foundation: God is good, God is just, God is Lord of history, and the righteous are called to live by faith while waiting.

40. Habakkuk and Worship

Chapter Three teaches us that worship can arise from the midst of suffering. Habakkuk does not wait until all circumstances become favorable in order to praise. Rather, he worships God while the danger is still present. Therefore, worship here is not a denial of the pain, but a declaration that God is greater than the pain.

41. The Most Important People in the Book
Habakkuk

The prophet, who represents the believer struggling with questions about evil, justice, and divine sovereignty.

The Lord

The true center of the book: the one who hears, answers, announces judgment, and gives strength.

The Chaldeans / Babylonians

The instrument God uses for discipline, and then subjects to judgment because of its own wickedness.

The People of Judah

The historical background to the prophet’s crisis, because corruption and injustice within Judah formed part of the reason for his initial complaint.

42. The Most Important Events in the Book

Habakkuk’s first complaint; the declaration of injustice within Judah; God’s announcement concerning the Chaldeans; the prophet’s astonishment that a wicked nation would be used for discipline; the second complaint; Habakkuk’s stationing himself at the watchpost; the writing of the vision; the declaration that “the righteous shall live by faith”; the five woes against the oppressor; the declaration of God’s sovereignty; Habakkuk’s prayer; and finally, the declaration of joy in God despite the absence of figs, crops, sheep, and cattle.

43. The Most Important Message Presented by the Book

Faith is not understanding everything God does, but trusting God when we do not understand him. Habakkuk began by asking, “How long?” and ended by saying, “Yet I will rejoice in the LORD; I will take joy in the God of my salvation.” Between these two questions, he learned that God sees injustice, that he will judge evil, that the delay of judgment does not mean God is absent, and that the righteous do not live merely by what they see but “by faith.” Therefore, the prophet’s greatest victory was not that the Chaldeans disappeared immediately or that the fields returned to productivity, but that his heart reached the point where he could say: Even if the fig tree does not blossom and the vines yield no fruit, God himself remains the cause of my joy.

44. The Major Movement of the Book of Habakkuk

The book can be summarized in this path:

Injustice.

Then:

A cry.

Then:

“How long?”

Then:

God’s answer.

Then:

Greater perplexity.

Then:

Waiting.

Then:

The vision.

Then:

“The righteous shall live by faith.”

Then:

Judgment upon the oppressor.

Then:

Prayer.

Then:

“In wrath remember mercy.”

Then:

“I rejoice in the LORD.”

Thus, the book moves from the mind’s crisis in the face of evil to the heart’s confidence in God.

🔑 The Sentence That Summarizes the Book of Habakkuk

“The Book of Habakkuk is a journey of faith from questioning to worship. The prophet begins by seeing injustice and crying out, “How long?” Then, when God answers him that the Chaldeans will come, his perplexity increases, and he asks how God can use a wicked nation to discipline his people. But God teaches him that history is not outside his control, that judgment has its appointed time, and that “the righteous shall live by faith.” In the end, the prophet reaches a faith that does not depend on an abundance of blessing or on the disappearance of the crisis, but is able to say, “Yet I will rejoice in the LORD; I will take joy in the God of my salvation.” Therefore, the greatest lesson of the book is not that the believer will always receive an explanation for everything that happens, but that he can trust God even when he does not understand what is happening.”
