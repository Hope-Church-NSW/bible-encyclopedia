Introduction to the Book of Joel

The central idea of the book: The Book of Joel is the book of the day of the Lord, judgment, repentance, mercy, the outpouring of the Holy Spirit, the restoration of what affliction destroyed, and finally the judgment of the nations. The book begins with a devastating natural disaster in the form of swarms of locusts that overran the land, but it does not allow the reader to stop at the disaster itself. Rather, it turns it into a spiritual question: What happens when a warning becomes a call to return to God? From there, the book moves from locusts to the day of the Lord, from devastation to fasting and repentance, and from repentance to God's promise: ‘And it shall come to pass afterward that I will pour out my Spirit on all flesh,’ and it ends with the proclamation of the Lord's presence among his people and the judgment of the nations. Thus, the book's major movement is: disaster → warning → repentance → mercy → the Holy Spirit → restoration → final judgment → the presence of God.

1. Name of the book and its meaning

The name of the book is Joel, and the Hebrew name is יוֹאֵל — Yôʾēl. It consists of the element Yo/Yahu — יוֹ, associated with the name of the Lord, and the element El — אֵל, meaning God. Thus, the name means “the Lord is God” or “Yahweh is God.” This meaning corresponds remarkably well with the book's content, because the crisis facing the people reveals their need to return to acknowledging that the Lord alone is God, and that the disaster is not explained solely through natural circumstances but must lead a person to examine his relationship with God.

2. Who Was Joel?

The book introduces the prophet as “Joel son of Pethuel,” but it does not provide much information about his personal life, family, or occupation. Therefore, our knowledge of Joel's character comes primarily from the message he carried and from the way he understood the events of his era in light of God's work. This brevity regarding personal information makes the book more focused on God's message than on the prophet's own biography.

3. Joel's Year of Birth and Death

The Bible does not record the year of Joel's birth or death, nor does it provide a specific date for his ministry. The book also does not mention the name of the king who ruled Judah when the prophecy was written; therefore, scholars have differed greatly in determining Joel's era. We must therefore be precise: the prophet's date is uncertain, while the content of the prophecy itself is clear, and the various estimates should not be turned into definitive dates.

4. Date of writing the book

Scholars differ in dating the Book of Joel. Some place it in an early period after the division of the kingdom, while others regard it as one of the postexilic books. The disagreement results from the absence of the king's name and of direct historical references that would help determine the period precisely. Yet regardless of the proposed date, the central axis remains constant: God's people face a crisis, and God uses it as a call to repentance, then announces a greater promise concerning the Spirit, restoration, and the day of the Lord.

5. For whom was the book written?

Joel addresses Judah and Jerusalem primarily, speaking to the elders, the priests, and the people, because the crisis affected the land, society, and worship together. The prophet does not treat the crisis as merely an economic problem, although the locusts destroyed the crops; rather, he sees it as an opportunity for the people to gather before God in fasting and prayer. Therefore, the book's true audience is the covenant community that needs to return to the God of the covenant.

6. Reason for writing the book

Joel aims to interpret the locust disaster as a warning calling the people to awaken and return to God. Then the prophet lifts the people's eyes from the present disaster to “the day of the Lord,” so that they may realize that what is happening before them can be a miniature representation of a greater judgment. But the book does not use fear to end in despair; instead, it opens the way to mercy, repentance, the outpouring of the Holy Spirit, and the restoration of what the disaster destroyed.

7. The Structure of the Book of Joel

The book can be divided into four main sections. Joel 1 describes the locust disaster and the devastation that struck the land; then Joel 2:1–17 moves to the warning of the day of the Lord and the call for the people to fast and repent; Joel 2:18–32 presents God's response through mercy and restoration and the promise of the outpouring of the Spirit; finally, Joel 3 announces the judgment of the nations and the Lord's dwelling in Zion. Thus, the book progresses from an earthly disaster to an eschatological event, from repentance to the Holy Spirit, and from restoration to final judgment.

8. Joel 1 — The Locust Disaster

The book opens with a terrifying picture of swarms of locusts that consumed what was in the land, leaving the crops, vineyards, and trees in a state of ruin. The locusts were not merely a minor agricultural problem but a disaster threatening food, the economy, and worship, because the absence of crops meant the absence of some of the materials used in offerings and sacrifices. Joel therefore connects the economic crisis with worship and spiritual life, making the disaster a call to return to God.

9. “Hear This, You Elders”

The prophet begins by calling the elders and the people to listen, because what happened is not something that should pass without interpretation. He then asks whether anything like this had occurred in the days of their ancestors, as though he wanted to make the disaster an event that had to be preserved in memory. Here the importance of spiritual memory emerges: the people who learn to interpret their events before God can turn a crisis into a lesson for future generations.

10. Locusts in the Bible

Locusts sometimes appear in the Bible as part of creation, but they also appear in the context of judgment, as in the plagues of Egypt in the Book of Exodus and in the covenant warnings in the Book of Deuteronomy. Joel's use of locusts therefore evokes an entire biblical memory and causes the reader to ask: Are we facing only a natural disaster, or a divine warning? The prophet does not leave the matter at a natural explanation but uses the disaster to lead the people to return and pray.

11. The Disaster's Effect on Worship

Joel says that the grain offering and the drink offering have been cut off from the house of the Lord because the fields have become desolate. This is a very important theological point, because the disaster affected not only the economy but also disrupted the worship system. Thus, the book shows that daily life and worship are not separate spheres, and that the ruin of the land can become a spiritual crisis when the people are unable to offer to God what they had customarily offered.

12. The Call to the Priests

The prophet calls the priests to consecrate themselves, fast, and gather the people, because the crisis requires a communal response. Repentance here is not merely an individual decision within the heart but a collective movement of the people before God. This reveals the role of spiritual leadership in times of crisis: the task is not only to interpret what is happening politically or economically but to lead people into prayer, self-examination, and a return to God.

13. Joel 2 — “The Day of the Lord”

The book moves from the locusts to the concept of the day of the Lord, one of the most important themes among the prophets. In the Bible, the day of the Lord is not merely a specified calendar day but an expression for God's decisive intervention in history for judgment and salvation. Joel therefore uses the present disaster as a warning that prepares the people to face a day greater than anything they have witnessed.

14. The Trumpet in Zion

The prophet says:

“Blow a trumpet in Zion; consecrate a fast; call a solemn assembly.”

The trumpet here is the sound of warning and mobilization, announcing that the matter can no longer be met with indifference. Zion, which was associated with God's presence and worship, becomes the place where the warning is proclaimed, as though God were saying to his people: Wake up before the great day comes.

15. “Return to Me with All Your Heart”

This is the heart of Joel's message:

“Yet even now, declares the Lord, return to me with all your heart, with fasting, with weeping, and with mourning.”

What is required is not merely a change in outward appearance, because the prophet also says:

“And rend your hearts and not your garments, and return to the Lord your God.”

This is one of the book's most important spiritual messages: true repentance begins in the heart, whereas tearing one's garments, fasting, or maintaining a religious appearance without inward change does not achieve the purpose God desires.

16. “He Is Gracious and Merciful”

Joel gives the foundation for repentance:

“For he is gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love, and he relents over disaster.”

The people return not because God is harsh and wants to destroy them, but because they know that God is merciful and opens the door of return. This language recalls God's self-revelation in Exodus 34:6, and Joel therefore connects repentance with the attributes of God that the people have known since the Old Testament. Repentance is possible because God is merciful.

17. Does the Lord Relent from Disaster?

The phrase “he relents over disaster” does not mean that God made a mistake and then discovered his error. Rather, it reflects biblical language describing God's withdrawal from executing judgment when return and repentance occur, in accordance with his covenant relationship with humanity. This is consistent with the prophets' teaching about the possibility of returning before judgment is completed. The prophetic warning often implicitly carries an opportunity: if a person returns, the door of mercy is still open.

18. God's Response

After the call to repentance, the scene changes clearly, as the Lord declares his zeal for his land and his compassion for his people. Then the promise of restoration begins: grain, wine, and oil return; the rain returns; and the years that the locusts consumed are restored. Here mercy becomes more than the removal of punishment, because it becomes the rebuilding of what was shattered.

19. “I Will Restore to You the Years”

One of the book's most beautiful promises is:

“I will restore to you the years that the swarming locust has eaten.”

This does not mean that God erases history or makes the suffering seem as though it never happened. Rather, he declares that he is able to work in a person's life after devastation so that the past will not have the final word. This is a profound spiritual principle: a person may lose years because of sin, suffering, or circumstances, but God's power to restore is greater than devastation's power to determine the future.

20. The Early and Latter Rains

Restoration is connected with the rain the land needs, because the ancient agricultural society depended heavily on seasonal rainfall. Joel uses this agricultural framework to proclaim that God restores fertility to the land after devastation. Thus, rain becomes a sign of blessing, restoration, and life after drought.

21. The Outpouring of the Holy Spirit

The book reaches one of the greatest prophecies of the Old Testament:

“And it shall come to pass afterward, that I will pour out my Spirit on all flesh; your sons and your daughters shall prophesy, your old men shall dream dreams, and your young men shall see visions.”

This prophecy transforms the scope of the gift in a remarkable way, because the Spirit is not here limited to a restricted prophetic class but extends to sons and daughters, old men and young men, male servants and female servants. Joel 2 therefore became a pivotal text for understanding the promise of the Holy Spirit in the New Testament.

22. Joel and the New Testament

On the day of Pentecost, when the disciples were filled with the Holy Spirit and spoke in tongues, Peter stood and interpreted the event through Joel, saying:

“But this is what was uttered through the prophet Joel.”

This is a very central point in biblical theology, because the Book of Acts 2 sees the event of Pentecost as an essential fulfillment of Joel's promise. Therefore, a study of Joel cannot be complete without studying Acts 2:14–21, because the New Testament itself places the Book of Joel at the heart of its interpretation of the event of the coming of the Holy Spirit.

23. “On All Flesh”

One of the prophecy's deepest elements is that the Spirit is poured out “on all flesh.” The expression does not necessarily mean that every person without exception will become a prophet; rather, it points to the unprecedented expansion of the sphere of the spiritual gift. Revelation is no longer connected only with a particular place, class, gender, or age, but there is a broad opening to the gift of the Spirit. Joel is therefore strongly connected with the universality of the Spirit's work in the New Testament.

24. Sons and Daughters

Joel says that sons and daughters will prophesy, which is very important in studying the place of women in prophetic and spiritual ministry in the Bible. The text declares that the gift of the Spirit is not limited to males but also includes women. Peter uses this text on the day of Pentecost, confirming that the promise was an essential part of interpreting the new era inaugurated by the coming of the Holy Spirit.

25. Old Men and Young Men

The text mentions old men who dream and young men who see visions, as though the promise crossed every stage of life. The Spirit's work is not monopolized by a particular age group; God can work in a person regardless of age. This picture reveals that the kingdom of God is not the project of a single generation but a work that includes families and successive generations.

26. Male Servants and Female Servants

Even male servants and female servants are included within the scope of the promise:

“Even on the male and female servants in those days I will pour out my Spirit.”

This addition is very deliberate, because it expands the promise to those with the lowest social status in the ancient community. The Holy Spirit is not given on the basis of social status, wealth, or authority but according to God's gift and grace.

27. Signs in Heaven and on Earth

Joel speaks of wonders in heaven and on earth: blood and fire and columns of smoke; then the sun is turned to darkness and the moon to blood before the great and awesome day of the Lord comes. These images are connected with eschatological prophetic language that also appears in other passages of the Bible. They should therefore be treated within the language of prophetic visions and the day of the Lord, leaving details that the text does not specify without unnecessary certainty.

28. “Everyone Who Calls on the Name of the Lord Will Be Saved”

Joel says:

“And it shall come to pass that everyone who calls on the name of the Lord shall be saved. For in Mount Zion and in Jerusalem there shall be those who escape, as the Lord has said, and among the survivors shall be those whom the Lord calls.”

This text is extremely important because it moves from speaking about judgment to opening the door of salvation. In the New Testament, this text is used directly in Romans 10:13 in the context of proclaiming salvation. Joel therefore becomes an important part of biblical theology concerning calling on the name of the Lord and salvation.

29. Joel 3 — The Valley of Jehoshaphat

The book moves in the third chapter to the judgment of the nations, and it mentions the Valley of Jehoshaphat. The name is connected with the Hebrew phrase meaning "Yahweh judges," and therefore the name itself carries a theological meaning. Nor should we treat it merely as a geographical name, because the text also uses it as an image of the place where the nations gather before God's judgment.

30. The Judgment of the Nations

After God announces the restoration of his people, he turns to the nations that wronged, sold, and exploited his people. This reveals that the restoration of God's people does not mean the absence of justice; rather, it comes with the announcement that God will hold accountable the powers that practiced evil. Here the book's trajectory is complete: God does not forget his people, and he does not allow injustice to have the final word.

31. Swords into Plowshares and Plowshares into Swords

In Joel 3 we find an image opposite to what appears in Isaiah 2 and Micah 4, where instruments of war are transformed into agricultural tools. But Joel, in the context of judgment, uses the reverse direction:

“Beat your sickles into swords and your sickles into spears; let the weak say: I am mighty.”

The image here depicts the gathering of the nations for war under divine judgment, then declares that the final battle is not outside God’s authority. Thus the book uses the language of war to proclaim the end of the nations’ rebellion before God’s judgment.

32. “The Lord Is a Refuge for His People”

After the announcement of judgment comes a promise of great importance:

“And the Lord will be a refuge for His people and a fortress for the children of Israel.”

This phrase brings us back to the heart of the book: the God who allowed discipline is Himself the refuge in the end. The goal is not for humanity to live in constant fear of God, but to learn that the Lord is the true place of safety.

33. “The Lord Dwells in Zion”

The book concludes with an image of the divine presence:

“And the Lord will be dwelling in Zion.”

This conclusion harmonizes with many of the prophetic books, where restoration reaches its goal when God is in the midst of His people. The restoration of the land, crops, and economy is not the true end; the end is the restoration of relationship and the divine presence.

34. The Most Important People in the Book
Joel

The prophet who proclaimed the message of repentance, the Day of the Lord, the Spirit, and restoration.

Pethuel

The father of Joel, and the only person explicitly mentioned as the prophet’s father.

The priests

They were an essential part of the leadership of the people in fasting, prayer, and the call to repentance.

The elders

They were addressed as the leaders of the community and the bearers of its memory and experience.

The children of Israel

The primary audience of the message, and the community that passed through the crisis of the locusts and was then called to repentance and restoration.

The nations

They appear at the end of the book as the subject of judgment before the Lord.

35. The Most Important Events in the Book

The invasion of the land by the locusts, the devastation of the crops, vineyards, and trees, the cessation of the grain offering and the drink offering, the call of the priests to fasting, the announcement of the nearness of the Day of the Lord, the call to return with all the heart, the promise of mercy, the restoration of the land and crops, the promise of rain, compensation for the years that the locusts ate, the pouring out of the Spirit on all flesh, the heavenly and earthly prophecies connected with the Day of the Lord, the announcement of deliverance for whoever calls on the name of the Lord, then the judgment of the nations and the Lord’s dwelling in Zion.

36. The Most Important Message the Book Presents

God is able to turn disaster into a call to repentance, repentance into restoration, and restoration into life by the Spirit; therefore, a person should not read crises as mere meaningless ruin. God calls His people to tear the heart, not the garments, and to return to Him; then He declares that His final purpose is not to destroy His people but to restore them, pour out His Spirit upon them, and make them live in His presence.

37. What Does the Book of Joel Indicate to Us?

The book indicates, first, that crises can become a spiritual warning, without this meaning that every specific disaster is a direct punishment for a specific sin. It indicates, second, that true repentance is not a religious appearance but the return of the heart to God. It indicates, third, that God is able to restore what the years of ruin have corrupted, yet it also declares that the greater restoration is the pouring out of the Spirit and life in God’s presence. Finally, the book lifts our gaze to the Day of the Lord, where God’s justice becomes clear and the salvation of those who call on His name appears.

38. Joel and Christ

Joel is connected with Christ in an indirect and important way through the kingdom of God, salvation, the pouring out of the Holy Spirit, and the Day of the Lord. In the New Testament, the promise of Joel 2 is directly connected with the event of Pentecost that occurred after Christ’s ascension, when Peter declared that what was happening was the fulfillment of what Joel had said. Thus Christ is the one who opens the way, in the apostolic proclamation, to the stage in which the Spirit is poured out upon believers, and Joel’s promise becomes part of the story of the salvation accomplished in Christ.

39. Joel and the Holy Spirit

If there is one text that the researcher should memorize in order to understand the Book of Joel, it is:

“And it shall come to pass afterward that I will pour out My Spirit on all flesh.”

The Spirit in Joel is not a secondary idea, but represents the major turning point from the mere restoration of the land to the spiritual restoration of the human person. For this reason, Joel 2 is directly connected with Acts 2, and forms one of the most important bridges between the promise of the Old Testament and the experience of the early church on the Day of Pentecost.

40. Joel and the Day of the Lord

The “Day of the Lord” in Joel carries two intertwined dimensions: it is connected with the historical judgment experienced by the people, and at the same time it transcends it toward the final day of judgment. Therefore, it should not be reduced to one event only, because the language of the prophets often makes earlier historical events shadows or preludes to God’s final works. Thus the locusts become a warning image that leads to a greater vision of God’s judgment and salvation at the end of history.

41. Joel, the Resurrection, and Judgment

The book does not provide a detailed doctrinal explanation of the doctrine of the resurrection as we find in Daniel 12, but it sets a strong framework for the final judgment of the nations and the Day of the Lord. When this is brought together with the New Testament proclamation concerning the resurrection and judgment, Joel becomes part of the larger picture that affirms that history has a moral end, and that God will hold evil accountable and declare the salvation of His people.

42. The Great Movement of the Book of Joel

The book can be summarized in this path:

Locusts.

Then:

Ruin.

Then:

Warning.

Then:

The day of the Lord.

Then:

Fasting.

Then:

Tearing the heart.

Then:

Return.

Then:

Mercy.

Then:

Restoring the years.

Then:

Rain.

Then:

Pouring out the Spirit.

Then:

Salvation.

Then:

Judgment of the nations.

Then:

“The Lord is dwelling in Zion.”

Thus the book begins with a dry land eaten by locusts and ends with a land in which the presence of the Lord dwells; between the beginning and the end we find repentance, mercy, the Spirit, and restoration.

📚 Primary References for Study

The foundational biblical texts: the Book of Joel 1–3, with a study of Exodus 10 to understand the locusts in the plagues of Egypt, Deuteronomy 28 to understand the locusts within the covenant curses, and Isaiah 2, 13, and 34, Jeremiah 25, Ezekiel 30, and Amos 5 to understand the concept of the Day of the Lord in the prophetic tradition. In the New Testament, special reference should be made to Acts 2:14–21 and Romans 10:13, because Peter and Paul use texts from Joel in proclaiming salvation and the work of the Holy Spirit.

🔑 The sentence that summarizes the Book of Joel

“The Book of Joel begins with a land eaten by locusts, but it does not allow a person to see the ruin without hearing the voice of God; the locusts become a warning, the warning leads to the Day of the Lord, the Day of the Lord leads to tearing the heart and returning, returning opens the door of mercy, mercy restores what the years have eaten, and then it goes beyond the restoration of the land to a greater promise: ‘I will pour out My Spirit on all flesh,’ until the book reaches the salvation of the one who calls on the name of the Lord, the judgment of the nations, and the Lord’s dwelling in Zion. Thus the story of ruin is transformed into a story of restoration, the dry land is transformed into a land that knows the presence of God, and the voice of the whole book becomes one call: Return to the Lord with all your hearts.”
