Introduction to the Book of Jeremiah

The central idea of the book: The Book of Jeremiah is the book of warning about judgment, exposing the corruption of the heart, proclaiming the new covenant, and hope amid ruin. Jeremiah stands in one of the most turbulent stages of Judah’s history, when Jerusalem was threatened by Babylon, and the people practiced worship while their hearts were far from God. Therefore the book does not merely present a prediction of the fall of a city, but reveals how a people who bear God’s name can reach collapse when worship becomes an external form and faith is separated from obedience. Nevertheless, the book does not end with judgment, because amid tears and ruin God declares a new promise: “I will make a new covenant with the house of Israel and with the house of Judah.”

1. Name of the book and its meaning

The Arabic name is Jeremiah, and the Hebrew name is יִרְמְיָהוּ — Yirmeyahu. It is a name connected with the name of the Lord in its latter part, while the first part is connected with the Hebrew root that bears the meaning of raising, appointing, or establishing, according to the proposed linguistic analysis. Therefore there is more than one interpretive formulation of the meaning of the name, among the most common of which is a meaning close to “the Lord raises” or “the Lord establishes,” with linguistic disagreement among scholars in determining the precise derivation. More important theologically is that the name Jeremiah itself is connected with the name of the Lord, which accords with the nature of his ministry, which was not based on his personal opinion, but on the word of God that he carried to a generation that was fiercely resistant.

2. Who is Jeremiah?

Jeremiah is “Jeremiah the son of Hilkiah, of the priests who were in Anathoth in the land of Benjamin”; that is, he came from a priestly background and from the region of Anathoth near Jerusalem. This is very important for understanding his character, because he had close knowledge of the system of worship and the temple. Therefore his criticisms of religious corruption came from within a genuine knowledge of worship, not from outside it. Nevertheless, Jeremiah did not become a comfortable prophet for the religious institution; rather, he became a voice confronting the priests, prophets, kings, and people when worship was separated from obedience.

3. The Location of Anathoth

Anathoth was a priestly city in the land of Benjamin and was geographically connected with the region of Jerusalem. Therefore Jeremiah was close to the religious and political center of the kingdom. This location made him close to the events taking place around the temple, the palace, and the religious leadership, but at the same time he came from an environment outside the immediate center of Jerusalem. It is striking that Anathoth itself will appear later in the book when Jeremiah buys the field there, so that the place is transformed from merely the prophet’s homeland into a symbol of hope in the restoration of the land after the exile.

4. The Calling of Jeremiah

Jeremiah’s calling is distinguished by the fact that it came at an early age. Therefore he said to the Lord: “Ah, Lord GOD! Behold, I cannot speak, for I am a child.” But God’s answer was decisive: “Do not say, ‘I am a child,’ for you shall go to all to whom I send you, and whatever I command you, you shall speak.” Here we see that the basis of prophecy is not the human being’s natural ability in oratory, but the authority of the divine word that God places in the mouth of his servant. Therefore Jeremiah’s own life became part of his message, for he had to live through suffering, rejection, and loneliness in order to bear witness to the truth with which he was sent.

5. The Year of Jeremiah’s Birth and Death

The Bible does not provide a precise year for Jeremiah’s birth; therefore the year of his birth or death cannot be determined with certainty. Since his calling came in the thirteenth year of the reign of Josiah, around 627 B.C. according to traditional chronology, it is likely that he was a young man at that time, and his ministry continued through the reigns of Josiah, Jehoahaz, Jehoiakim, Jehoiachin, and Zedekiah. As for the end of his life, it is most likely connected with the events that followed the fall of Jerusalem, but the book does not give us a reliable date for his death. Therefore this point must remain within the realm of probability, not certainty.

6. The Time of Jeremiah’s Ministry

Jeremiah’s ministry began in the thirteenth year of Josiah and continued through the turbulent years before and after the fall of Jerusalem. This means that Jeremiah lived through the collapse of the southern kingdom almost before his eyes, witnessed the transfer of world power from Assyria to Babylon, and also witnessed the fall of Jerusalem and the temple around 586 B.C. Therefore the book carries prophetic testimony from within the crisis itself, not merely a historical interpretation that came after the events had ended.

7. To Whom Did Jeremiah Write?

The primary address was directed to Judah and Jerusalem, but the book also expands to include many kings, peoples, and nations. Jeremiah addresses the people, the king, the priests, the prophets, the elders, the army, and the inhabitants of Jerusalem, and he also announces judgments on nations such as Egypt, Moab, Ammon, Edom, Philistia, and Babylon. Therefore the book carries a clear national dimension, but at the same time it presents a broader vision affirming that the Lord is not only the God of Judah, but the sovereign Lord of history and of all nations.

8. Why Did Jeremiah Write?

Jeremiah wrote to declare that Judah’s sin had reached a stage that required judgment, and to warn the people against false reliance on the temple, political alliances, or misleading prophecies. He also declared that Babylon would be the instrument of divine discipline, and that resistance to this decree would not lead to salvation but to further ruin. Nevertheless, the aim of the message was not merely to announce death, because Jeremiah also carried the promise that God would gather his people, restore them, and establish a new covenant with them.

9. Division of the book

The Book of Jeremiah may be divided into major units that help in understanding its theological and historical movement, while noting that the arrangement of the book is not a simple chronological arrangement in all its parts. Jeremiah 1–25 focuses to a large degree on the calling of the prophet, the sins of Judah, and the warning of judgment, while Jeremiah 26–45 deals with the prophet’s conflicts with the leadership and the people, and the historical events that the crisis led toward collapse. Then Jeremiah 46–51 comes to present prophecies against the nations, while Jeremiah 52 provides a historical conclusion to the fall of Jerusalem and the exile. The major movement of the book may be seen in this form: calling → warning → rejection → judgment → fall → hope → new covenant.

10. Jeremiah 1 — The Prophet Before Birth

Among the deepest statements in the book is the Lord’s saying to Jeremiah: “Before I formed you in the womb, I knew you; and before you came out of the womb, I sanctified you. I appointed you a prophet to the nations.” This text reveals that Jeremiah’s calling did not begin at the moment he stood before the people, but was connected with God’s prior purpose for his ministry. The text does not mean that Jeremiah knew all the details of his life beforehand, but it declares that prophecy is not a personal project begun by a human being, but a divine calling that has its origin in the purpose of God.

11. The Two Visions of the Almond and the Pot

The Lord gave Jeremiah two visions at the beginning of his ministry: the first was the rod of an almond tree, and the second was a boiling pot from the north. The vision of the almond is connected with the word of the Lord, meaning that he is watching over his word to perform it, while the tilted pot from the north points to the danger coming upon Jerusalem and Judah. Thus the book begins with a very important teaching: God does not send a warning without execution, because the word spoken by the Lord is transformed in history into an event.

12. Jeremiah 2 — Forsaking the Fountain of Living Waters

The Lord reproaches his people by saying: “For my people have committed two evils: they have forsaken me, the fountain of living waters, to hew for themselves cisterns, broken cisterns that can hold no water.” The image here is very profound, because the problem was not merely adding a sin to a good life, but replacing the true source of life with human sources that cannot give life. Thus idolatry, political alliances, and confidence in human abilities all become different forms of forsaking God, and the result is that a person possesses a cistern yet remains thirsty.

13. Jeremiah 3 — Spiritual Adultery

Jeremiah uses the language of marriage to describe Israel’s relationship with God, prophetic language that has strong roots in Hosea and Isaiah. Sin is not merely a legal violation, but a betrayal of the covenant, as though the people had left a faithful husband and gone after others. This connects Jeremiah directly with the prophetic tradition that sees the relationship between God and his people as a relationship of covenant, love, and faithfulness. Therefore idolatry becomes spiritual betrayal, not merely a ritual error.

14. Jeremiah 7 — The Sermon at the Temple Gate

One of the most famous passages in the book is the sermon at the temple gate, where Jeremiah stands at the entrance of the house of the Lord and warns the people against false trust in the building. They were saying: “The temple of the Lord, the temple of the Lord, the temple of the Lord are these,” as though the existence of the temple guaranteed them protection regardless of what they did. But Jeremiah declares that God wants the amendment of ways and deeds, and that the temple cannot be turned into a fortress protecting a people who insist on disobedience.

15. Why Was the Temple Sermon Dangerous?

Jeremiah’s words were shocking because the temple was a central symbol of God’s presence in Jerusalem. Therefore the announcement of the possibility of its fall was extremely dangerous politically and religiously. But Jeremiah was not diminishing the holiness of the temple; rather, he was defending the true meaning of holiness, because a holy place cannot make a person holy if he continues in evil. This is a very important principle in every age: the building, the rite, or religious affiliation cannot be used as a substitute for repentance and obedience.

16. The Potter and the Clay

In Jeremiah 18, God takes the prophet to the potter’s house, and there he sees the potter working the clay in his hand. Then the Lord uses the scene to declare his sovereignty over peoples and nations, and that he can announce judgment and then turn back from it if the people repent, or announce good and then bring judgment if the people turn to evil. This image does not mean that the human being is without responsibility; rather, it affirms at the same time God’s sovereignty and God’s moral response to human repentance and rebellion.

17. Jeremiah 20 — A Prophet Who Suffers

Jeremiah is not a cold prophet who announces judgments without feeling their pain; rather, he reveals to us an intense inner struggle. He was subjected to beating, imprisonment, mockery, and rejection, and therefore he cried out to God and expressed his bitterness over bearing the message. Nevertheless, he says elsewhere that the word of God was in his heart like a burning fire that he tried to hold in but could not. This is one of the deepest images of prophetic ministry: suffering does not cancel the calling, and the calling does not cancel suffering.

18. Jeremiah and the False Prophets

One of Jeremiah’s greatest conflicts was with the prophets who announced false peace to the people while judgment was drawing near. They said that Jerusalem would not fall and that the yoke of Babylon would be broken quickly, while Jeremiah declared that the people must submit to God’s discipline. Here the difference between the true prophet and the false prophet appears: the true prophet does not measure his message by the extent to which people accept it, but by the extent of his faithfulness to the word of God.

19. Jeremiah 23 — The Good Shepherd

The Lord criticizes the shepherds who destroyed the people and scattered them, then declares that he will raise up good shepherds. After that comes the famous prophecy:

“Behold, the days are coming, says the Lord, when I will raise up for David a righteous Branch; he shall reign as king and prosper, and shall execute judgment and righteousness in the land.”

Here Jeremiah’s connection with the Davidic hope also appears. Political ruin does not mean that God’s promise to the house of David has ended; rather, God will raise up a king connected with righteousness and justice.

20. “The Lord Our Righteousness”

The coming king is called:

“And this is the name by which they will call him: The Lord Our Righteousness.”

This is an extremely profound theological phrase because it connects the Davidic king with the righteousness that comes from the Lord. In Christian reading, this text became an important part of the study of Christ as the righteous king, while in the original study we must first place the text in the context of Israel’s hope in the just Davidic king, and then see how the New Testament reads it in light of Christ.

21. Jeremiah 29 — The Letter to the Exiles

Among the most famous texts in the book is the letter that Jeremiah sent to the exiles in Babylon, where he tells them that they will not return after a few days as the false prophets were saying, but that they must build houses, plant, live, and seek the peace of the city to which they had been taken. Then comes the famous promise: “For I know the thoughts that I think toward you, says the Lord, thoughts of peace and not of evil, to give you an end and a hope.” This text must not be separated from its context. It is a promise to a people living under the discipline of exile; therefore hope here does not mean the absence of suffering, but means that suffering is not the final word.

22. Jeremiah 31 — The New Covenant

Here the book reaches one of its greatest theological peaks:

“Behold, the days are coming, says the Lord, when I will make a new covenant with the house of Israel and with the house of Judah.”

Then it says:

“I will put my law within them and write it on their hearts, and I will be their God, and they shall be my people.”

The fundamental problem was not a lack of the existence of the law, but the disobedience of the heart. Therefore the solution comes from within: God writes his law on the heart, so the covenant is transformed from merely an external sign into a true inward relationship.

23. Forgiveness of Sin in the New Covenant

Jeremiah completes the proclamation:

“For I will forgive their iniquity, and I will remember their sin no more.”

Here three elements are linked together:

The new heart.

The knowledge of God.

The forgiveness of sin.

These elements become very foundational in Christian theology of the New Testament. Therefore the author of the Epistle to the Hebrews quoted the text of Jeremiah 31 in his discussion of the new covenant established by Christ.

24. Jeremiah 32 — The Purchase of the Field

During the siege of Jerusalem, while the city was heading toward collapse, God commands Jeremiah to buy a field in Anathoth. At that moment the matter may seem completely illogical, because the land was about to fall under Babylon’s control. But the purchase of the field becomes an act of prophetic faith, because Jeremiah says that God will restore the people to the land, and the field will be bought again. Thus the field becomes a practical testimony that judgment is temporary with respect to the people of God, but the promise of restoration is eternal in the purpose of God.

25. The Fall of Jerusalem

The historical story reaches its climax in Jeremiah 39 and 52, where Jerusalem falls before the Babylonians. Zedekiah’s sons are killed before his eyes, then his eyes are put out and he is taken to Babylon, and the temple, the palace, and the houses of Jerusalem are burned. This is one of the most painful scenes of the Old Testament, because it represents the collapse of the political and religious order under whose shadow the people had lived for centuries.

26. Why did God allow Jerusalem to fall?

Jeremiah rejects interpreting the fall as merely Babylon’s military superiority, because for him the fundamental issue is theological before it is political. Babylon was an instrument in God’s hand to discipline Judah because of repeated disobedience, but this does not mean that Babylon itself would remain unaccountable; therefore, Jeremiah’s prophecies against Babylon also appear. Thus, the book maintains an important balance: God uses history to discipline His people, yet He remains the Lord of history and also judges the instrument He used.

27. Jeremiah’s Prophecies against the Nations

Chapters 46–51 focus on the nations, including:

Egypt.

Philistia.

Moab.

Ammon.

Edom.

Damascus.

Elam.

Babylon.

This confirms that God is not only the God of Jerusalem, but the Lord of all peoples, and that divine justice does not stop at Israel’s borders. Just as Judah could not escape judgment because of its covenant relationship, the nations too are not outside God’s authority.

28. Babylon in the Book of Jeremiah

The great theological paradox is that Babylon, which God used to discipline Judah, itself becomes the object of judgment. This reveals that God does not justify human evil simply because He used it to accomplish a particular purpose. Babylon acted according to its ambition and violence, and therefore judgment comes upon it. This principle gives us a profound picture of God’s sovereignty: God works in history without turning moral evil into good in itself.

29. The Character of Jeremiah

Jeremiah is one of the biblical figures whose inner feelings are most fully revealed, because the book allows us to see the prophet praying, crying out, weeping, questioning, and complaining. Therefore, some of his texts are sometimes called “Jeremiah’s Confessions,” passages in which the conflict between personal pain and faithfulness to the message appears. This feature makes the book very close to the Psalms, but with an important difference: Jeremiah lives through the crisis while at the same time bearing God’s word to the nation.

30. The Main Persons in the Book
Jeremiah

The prophet who bore the message of judgment and hope during Judah’s fall.

Hilkiah

Jeremiah’s father, who was one of the priests in Anathoth.

Josiah

The king during whose days Jeremiah’s ministry began, and whose reign witnessed an important religious reform.

Jehoiakim

A king whose reign was associated with resistance to Jeremiah and persecution of his message.

Jehoiachin

The king of Judah who was taken into Babylonian exile.

Zedekiah

The last king of Judah before Jerusalem’s fall, who was in a highly unstable political and religious situation.

Baruch son of Neriah

Jeremiah’s scribe and assistant, who played an important role in writing and recording the prophetic messages.

Nebuchadnezzar

The king of Babylon who led the empire that besieged and overthrew Jerusalem.

Hananiah

The false prophet who proclaimed that Babylon’s yoke would soon be broken; Jeremiah confronted him with God’s message.

32. The Most Important Message Presented by the Book

God’s people cannot replace obedience with rituals, nor can they use the temple or religious affiliation as a guarantee against judgment; yet God, even when He disciplines His people, does not annul His covenant, but opens before them the way of hope through forgiveness, the new heart, the new covenant, and return to the land.

33. What Does the Book of Jeremiah Indicate to Us?

It indicates that God is concerned with the heart and not merely with religious appearance, and that worship which does not produce obedience becomes religious deception. It declares that divine judgment is real, but it is neither arbitrary nor vindictive; rather, it is connected to God’s justice, holiness, and faithfulness to the covenant. It also reveals that true hope is not founded on political circumstances, for Jerusalem may fall and the temple may burn, yet God’s word remains. Finally, the book directs attention to the new covenant, which reaches its fulfillment in the work of Christ, where sins are forgiven and God’s law is written on the heart.

34. Jeremiah and Christ

Jeremiah occupies an important place in the study of Christ because the book brings together:

The Davidic king.

The good shepherd.

Righteousness.

The new covenant.

The forgiveness of sins.

These elements appear in concentrated form in the person of Christ in the New Testament. Therefore, the study of Jeremiah should not stop at the fall of Jerusalem, but should follow the thread that begins with the failure of the old covenant to produce inward obedience and reaches the proclamation of the new covenant in Christ.

35. Jeremiah and the New Covenant

The New Testament directly quotes Jeremiah, especially the text of:

Jeremiah 31:31–34.

This appears clearly in:

Hebrews 8.

Then the author of Hebrews returns to the text in:

Hebrews 10.

This confirms that the early church understood Jeremiah’s promise of the new covenant as an essential part of Christ’s saving work. Therefore, it may be said that Jeremiah provides one of the most important prophetic building blocks for understanding the transition from the old covenant to the new covenant.

36. Jeremiah and the Book of Deuteronomy

There is a deep relationship between Jeremiah and Deuteronomy, because both are concerned with:

Obedience.

Blessing.

Curse.

The land.

The covenant.

True worship.

Jeremiah lives out, in a sense, the result of what Deuteronomy warned against: disobedience leads to the loss of the land and exile. Therefore, the Book of Jeremiah may be read in part as a historical fulfillment of the warnings that the Law of Moses set before Israel.

37. Jeremiah and the Book of Hosea

There is also a strong echo between Jeremiah and Hosea, especially in the image of:

Spiritual adultery.

Hosea used marriage to explain Israel’s betrayal of the covenant, and Jeremiah uses similar language to reveal Judah’s betrayal. Yet Jeremiah adds a very powerful element: the need for a new covenant and a new heart. Thus, the prophetic line moves from diagnosing betrayal to proclaiming the divine remedy.

38. The Major Movement of the Book

The Book of Jeremiah may be summarized in this sequence:

The people forsake God.

↓

God sends the prophet.

↓

The people reject the message.

↓

Judgment approaches.

↓

Babylon besieges Jerusalem.

↓

The city falls.

↓

The temple burns.

↓

The people are taken into exile.

↓

God promises restoration.

↓

God promises a new covenant.

↓

Forgiveness and the new heart.

This movement makes Jeremiah one of the clearest books in its transition from judgment to hope.

🔑 The Sentence That Summarizes the Book of Jeremiah

“Jeremiah is the book of the prophet who stood between a rebellious people and a holy God; he bore the word of judgment while his heart wept for Jerusalem, and he declared that the temple could not protect a people who refused to repent, and that Babylon would become the instrument of divine discipline. Yet the fall of the city would not be the end of God’s story with his people; from the midst of exile and devastation comes the book’s greatest promise: a new covenant, a law written on the heart, forgiveness of sins, true knowledge of God, and hope that what sin has destroyed God can restore by his grace.”
