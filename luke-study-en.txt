The Gospel of Luke
1. Name of the book and its meaning

“Luke” is the name traditionally associated with the author of the third Gospel, and it is a common Greek name in the Roman world. His Gospel is considered the first part of a unified two-part work, followed by the Book of Acts, so that the first begins with what Christ did and taught, while the second continues the extension of his work through the Holy Spirit and the church.

2. The Author and His Character

Early Christian tradition attributes the Gospel to Luke, the physician whom Paul mentions in his letters. His association with Paul appears clearly in certain passages of Acts that use the “we” form, indicating that he accompanied Paul on some of his journeys. Luke does not mention his name within the Gospel, but in the preface he presents himself as a researcher who collected reports from the earliest eyewitnesses and arranged them accurately.

3. For Whom Was the Gospel Written?

Luke wrote to “Theophilus,” and his work also appears to have been directed toward a broader audience of believers, especially readers from a Gentile background. He is concerned with clarifying events and backgrounds, and he presents Christ as the Savior of the world, not merely the Savior of one national group.

4. The Reason for Writing the Gospel

Luke declares that his purpose is for the reader to know “the certainty of the things” he has been taught. Therefore, he did not write merely a collection of stories; rather, he gathered the accounts concerning Christ and arranged them into a coherent narrative. His interest in history, testimony, accuracy, and the sequence of events is evident.

5. The Time of Writing the Gospel

The Gospel does not directly specify the date of its composition. Many scholars associate its writing with the second half of the first century, before or around the time of the composition of the Book of Acts. The precise date remains debated, and therefore a specific year should not be asserted without explicit evidence.

6. The Main Theme

The principal theme of Luke is Jesus Christ the Savior, who came to seek and save the lost and to proclaim God’s salvation to all people. The Gospel highlights his compassion toward sinners, the poor, the sick, the marginalized, women, and Gentiles, while emphasizing that the salvation brought by Christ extends to the whole world.

7. Christ the Savior

Luke presents Christ as the Savior sent by God. This is evident from the birth narrative, when the angel announces that the child is “a Savior, who is Christ the Lord.” All the events of the Gospel move toward the work of salvation that reaches its climax in Christ’s death and resurrection.

8. Christ and the Holy Spirit

The Holy Spirit occupies a prominent place in Luke, beginning with the birth narratives and continuing through Christ’s baptism, temptation, and ministry. In Nazareth, Jesus declares that the Spirit of the Lord is upon him, connecting his ministry with a divine anointing to proclaim good news to the poor and to set the oppressed free.

9. Birth and Incarnation

Luke provides extensive details about the angel’s announcement to Mary, the birth of John the Baptist, the birth of Jesus, the shepherds, and the praises of Mary, Zechariah, and Simeon. From the beginning, Christ appears as the fulfillment of God’s historical purpose, while Luke connects the birth with the promises of the Old Testament.

10. Luke’s Concern for the Poor and Marginalized

One of the Gospel’s prominent features is its concern for those on the margins of social and religious life. This is evident in the stories of the Good Samaritan, the prodigal son, Zacchaeus the tax collector, the sinful woman, Lazarus the poor man, and others. This does not mean that Luke presents a social gospel separate from salvation; rather, he proclaims that God’s grace reaches people where they are and restores their dignity and relationship with God.

11. Prayer

Luke clearly highlights the prayer life of Christ, mentioning his prayer at his baptism, before choosing the Twelve, at the Transfiguration, in Gethsemane, and on the cross. Thus, prayer appears not as a secondary part of Christ’s life, but as an essential element of his relationship with the Father and of his ministry.

12. Distinctive Parables in Luke

Luke alone, or with particular distinction, records a number of important parables, such as the Good Samaritan, the Rich Fool, the Prodigal Son, the Rich Man and Lazarus, and the Pharisee and the Tax Collector. These parables reveal the heart of Christ’s message concerning mercy, repentance, forgiveness, love of neighbor, and the danger of relying on self-righteousness and wealth.

13. The Journey to Jerusalem

Luke gives special attention to Christ’s journey toward Jerusalem. The journey does not appear to be merely a geographical movement, but a theological road on which Christ deliberately moves toward the cross while teaching his disciples about the kingdom, discipleship, prayer, and mercy.

14. Christ and Various Groups

In Luke, Christ appears close to people regardless of their background. He interacts with sinners, tax collectors, women, the sick, strangers, the poor, and the rich. This concern reaches its climax in the story of Zacchaeus, where Christ declares: “For the Son of Man came to seek and to save the lost.”

15. The Cross

Luke does not present the cross as an unexpected tragedy, but as the fulfillment of God’s revealed purpose. Christ appears on the cross in an image of mercy and forgiveness, saying, “Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do,” and then entrusting his spirit to the Father in confidence.

16. The Resurrection

The resurrection comes as a declaration of Christ’s victory and the fulfillment of what he had previously told his disciples. Luke is distinguished by the story of the two disciples on the road to Emmaus, where Christ explains that what happened was not outside God’s purpose, but that “the Christ should suffer these things and enter into his glory.”

17. Major Figures in the Gospel of Luke

The story centers on Jesus Christ, alongside whom John the Baptist, Mary, Joseph, Zechariah, Elizabeth, Simeon, Anna, and the disciples come into prominence, in addition to figures such as Mary Magdalene, Zacchaeus, Martha, Mary, and others. Luke also gives a clear place to the poor, sinners, the sick, and the Gentiles as part of the scene that reveals the breadth of salvation.

18. The Gospel’s Main Message

Luke’s message is that the salvation God prepared in Christ has come into the world, and that Jesus is the Savior who seeks the lost, welcomes the repentant, and opens the way of salvation to all peoples. Therefore, the Gospel brings together individual salvation, God’s mercy, repentance, the cross, and the resurrection, then naturally prepares for the transition to the spread of the Gospel in the Book of Acts.

19. The Major Movement of Luke’s Gospel

The Gospel of Luke begins with a precise account of the events that led to the appearance of Jesus Christ. John the Baptist appears as the forerunner who prepares the way, and then Christ is born in Bethlehem, declaring that God’s promise of salvation has entered history. After his baptism and temptation, Jesus begins his ministry in the power of the Holy Spirit, proclaiming the kingdom, healing the sick, forgiving sins, and declaring God’s mercy to sinners, the poor, and the marginalized, while it gradually becomes clear that he is the Savior who came to seek and save the lost. Then the decisive journey toward Jerusalem begins, where he teaches his disciples about repentance, prayer, mercy, and discipleship, and reveals that the way to glory passes through the cross. In Jerusalem, Christ is handed over, crucified, and dies, but he rises on the third day and explains to his disciples that his sufferings and resurrection were part of God’s purpose revealed in the Scriptures. The Gospel ends at a new boundary, as the risen Christ moves from being the subject of testimony to being the source of the mission, and the disciples prepare to be his witnesses to all nations. This opens the way directly to the Book of Acts.

20. Connections with the Old Testament

Luke is clearly connected with the Old Testament, especially in the birth narratives, the praises of Mary, Zechariah, and Simeon, and the use of images and promises from the Law, the Prophets, and the Psalms. He presents Christ as the fulfillment of God’s promises to Israel, but the fulfillment of these promises does not stop with Israel; it extends to the Gentiles, as appears powerfully at the conclusion of the Gospel.

21. The Relationship between the Gospel of Luke and the Book of Acts

Luke and Acts represent one work in two parts. The first focuses on Christ’s ministry, death, and resurrection, while the second focuses on the continuation of his witness and the spread of the Gospel through the apostles and the Holy Spirit. Therefore, the end of Luke is not a closed conclusion, but a transition from Christ’s work in the flesh to the continuation of testimony to the risen Christ through the power of the Holy Spirit.

22. Encyclopedic summary

The Gospel of Luke is the Gospel of the Savior, mercy, and salvation for all people. It presents Jesus Christ as the fulfillment of God’s promises, the one anointed by the Holy Spirit, the physician who came to the sick, the shepherd who seeks the lost, the Savior who welcomes the repentant, and the Lord who deliberately walks toward the cross. Luke emphasizes that salvation is not confined to one group, class, or social background, but extends to everyone who believes in Christ, repents, and returns to God. The story ends with the resurrection and preparation for witness, making the life of Christ in the Gospel the foundation of the worldwide mission that will spread through the Book of Acts.
