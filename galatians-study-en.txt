Paul's Letter to the Galatians
1. The Epistle’s Name and the Meaning of the Name

It is known as the Epistle to the Galatians, Paul's letter in which he strongly defended the truth of the gospel, justification by faith, and the believer's freedom in Christ. The letter is associated with the region of Galatia in central Asia Minor, where Christian communities were established through Paul's ministry.

The letter is characterized by its forceful tone, because the issue it addresses is not merely a difference of opinion, but the danger of distorting the foundation of the gospel and adding human requirements to Christ's saving work.

2. The Author and His Character

The author is the apostle Paul, who presents himself from the outset as an apostle “not from men nor through man, but through Jesus Christ and God the Father.”

This opening is important for understanding the letter, because Paul is not defending himself merely for the sake of self-defense; he is defending the source of his apostolic authority and the source of the gospel he proclaims.

Here his character emerges as that of an apostle who holds firmly to the truth, even when doing so leads to direct confrontation with practices or people of status.

3. To Whom Was the Epistle Written?

It was written to the churches of Galatia, a form indicating multiple communities in the region.

The basic problem was that some teachers tried to persuade Gentile believers that faith in Christ was not enough, and that they had to come under the requirements of the law, especially circumcision, in order to be fully accepted before God.

Galatia therefore became a battleground between the gospel of grace and a gospel that depended on works of the law for justification.

4. The Reason for Writing the Epistle

Paul wrote because the believers were in danger of turning away from the gospel they had received.

He declares from the outset:

“I am astonished that you are so quickly deserting him who called you by the grace of Christ and turning to a different gospel”
(Galatians 1: 6).

The danger was not the addition of a minor practice, but a change in the basis of salvation: Is a person justified before God by faith in Christ, or by performing the works of the law?

5. Date of the Epistle

Researchers differ over the date of the letter because of differing views about what is meant by “Galatia” and the order of some events in Paul's ministry.

Some studies place it in an early period, while others place it approximately in the middle of the 50s AD.

The date should therefore be regarded as a historical probability rather than information explicitly determined by the text.

6. The Main Theme

The central theme is:

Justification by faith in Christ and freedom in the grace of the gospel, without relying on works of the law as the basis of salvation.

A person does not become righteous before God because he bears a physical mark or observes a ritual system, but by faith in Jesus Christ.

The letter therefore moves from defending the gospel, to establishing justification by faith, to explaining the function of the law, and then to Christian freedom and life by the Spirit.

7. The One Gospel

Paul begins by defending the truth of the gospel and declares that any gospel differing from the gospel he proclaimed is rejected, even if it is brought by an apostle or an angel.

This is one of the letter's strongest statements:

“But even if we or an angel from heaven should preach to you a gospel contrary to the one we preached to you, let him be accursed”
(Galatians 1: 8).

The truth of the gospel is not measured by the preacher's personality, but by his faithfulness to the revelation God made in Christ.

8. The Source of Paul's Gospel

Paul emphasizes that the gospel he proclaims was not derived from a human source, but from a revelation of Jesus Christ.

He recounts how he was formerly a persecutor of the church, and then God called him by his grace and revealed his Son in him.

Paul's own life thus becomes a testimony to the power of grace: the persecutor became an apostle of Christ.

9. Grace and Calling

Paul describes his calling as the result of God's grace, not personal merit.

This idea is fundamental to the entire letter: salvation, ministry, and calling all flow from God's grace.

In this way Paul confronts the view that makes the relationship with God depend on human merit.

10. Paul and the Apostles in Jerusalem

Paul presents his relationship with Peter, James, and John, showing that the apostles recognized the grace given to him and his ministry to the Gentiles.

Their recognition, however, did not create his apostleship; its origin was Christ's call.

This is important because Paul establishes that the gospel he proclaims is not a personal invention detached from the apostolic church.

11. Confronting Peter

The defense of the gospel reaches a personal situation when Paul confronted Peter in Antioch because of his conduct toward the Gentiles.

The issue was not merely social conduct, but carried a theological message: Are Gentile believers accepted in Christ as they are, or do they need to adopt Jewish identity in order for their acceptance to be complete?

Here it becomes clear that conduct can deny the gospel even when words are correct.

12. Justification by Faith

The letter reaches its theological heart in the declaration:

“We know that a person is not justified by works of the law but through faith in Jesus Christ, so we also have believed in Christ Jesus, in order to be justified by faith in Christ and not by works of the law”
(Galatians 2: 16).

Justification is a gift connected with Christ, not a wage a person earns because of his works.

13. “Crucified with Christ”

Paul declares:

“I have been crucified with Christ. It is no longer I who live, but Christ who lives in me”
(Galatians 2: 20).

Here Paul moves from justification to the believer's new identity.

The believer no longer lives on the basis of the old self that seeks to establish its own righteousness, but lives by faith in the Son of God, who loved him and gave himself for him.

14. Christ and the Blood of the Cross

Paul emphasizes that returning to reliance on the law after faith in Christ means emptying Christ's work of its meaning.

Therefore he says:

“I do not nullify the grace of God, for if righteousness were through the law, then Christ died for no purpose”
(Galatians 2: 21).

The cross therefore confirms that salvation cannot be a human achievement, because Christ gave himself to accomplish what human beings cannot accomplish by themselves.

15. The Holy Spirit and Faith

Paul asks the Galatians a decisive question: Did you receive the Spirit by works of the law or by hearing with faith?

The answer leads to a fundamental principle: Christian life begins by faith and continues by the Spirit; it does not begin by grace and then reach completion through reliance on the flesh.

16. Abraham and Faith

Paul returns to Abraham to demonstrate that the principle of justification by faith is not a later Christian invention.

He quotes:

“Abraham believed God, and it was counted to him as righteousness”
(Galatians 3: 6).

Paul thus places the gospel within the story of the Old Testament, explaining that God's promise to Abraham was directed toward the blessing of all nations in Christ.

17. The Law and the Promise

Paul distinguishes between the promise God gave to Abraham and the law that came later.

The law did not cancel God's promise, nor was it the final means of fulfilling it; rather, it had a specific function within the history of salvation.

Thus Christ becomes the center and fulfillment of the promise.

18. The Law as a Tutor to Christ

Paul uses the image of the "tutor" to explain the function of the Law.

For the Law reveals sin and leads man to realize his need, but it is not the Savior.

And with the coming of faith in Christ, salvation history reaches a new stage, because the believer has become a son of God through faith.

19. Sons of God in Christ

Paul declares:

“For you are all sons of God through faith in Christ Jesus”
(Galatians 3: 26).

Here the Epistle moves from "justification" to adoption.

For the believer does not merely obtain a verdict of acquittal, but enters into a filial relationship with God through Christ.

20. Neither Jew nor Greek

In Christ, the barriers that were used for spiritual discrimination collapse:

“There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free, there is neither male nor female; for you are all one in Christ Jesus”
(Galatians 3: 28).

This does not eliminate human and social differences, but it eliminates using them as a basis for distinction in acceptance before God and salvation.

21. The Son and the Heir

Paul explains that the believer is not a slave, but a son; and if a son, then an heir as well.

This revelation reaches its climax in God sending His Son and then His Spirit into the hearts of believers, so that the believer cries out:

“Abba, Father!”

For salvation in Galatians is not merely liberation from condemnation, but a transition from slavery to sonship.

22. Christian Freedom

Paul declares:

“Stand fast therefore in the liberty by which Christ has made us free, and do not be entangled again with a yoke of bondage”
(Galatians 5: 1).

Christian freedom is not a freedom to sin, but a freedom from attempting to obtain righteousness before God through the works of the Law.

23. Freedom and Love

Paul warns against turning freedom into an opportunity for the flesh, and presents the alternative:

“For you, brethren, have been called to liberty; only do not use liberty as an opportunity for the flesh, but through love serve one another”
(Galatians 5: 13).

Therefore, freedom does not end with the self, but transforms into love and service.

24. The Flesh and the Spirit

Paul presents the conflict between the works of the flesh and the fruit of the Spirit.

The works of the flesh reveal the nature of a life controlled by man apart from God, whereas the fruit of the Spirit reveals the life in which the Holy Spirit operates.

This clarifies that the Gospel does not liberate man from the Law so that he may live without holiness, but liberates him so that he may walk by the Spirit.

25. The Fruit of the Spirit

Paul presents the famous list:

“But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, longsuffering, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control”
(Galatians 5: 22–23).

It is noteworthy that he speaks of "fruit" in the singular, pointing to a single life produced by the Spirit in the believer.

26. Crucifixion with Christ

Paul affirms that those who belong to Christ have crucified the flesh with its passions and desires.

This brings us back to Galatians 2: 20: the new life is not merely an external behavioral change, but a union with Christ that transforms man's relationship with himself and with sin.

27. Love and Bearing Burdens

The Epistle moves to a direct practical application:

“Bear one another's burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ”
(Galatians 6: 2).

After Paul rejects relying on the Law for justification, he speaks of the "law of Christ" in the context of love and bearing the burdens of others.

Thus grace does not eliminate morality, but re-establishes it on love.

28. Sowing and Reaping

Paul lays down a general spiritual principle:

“Do not be deceived, God is not mocked; for whatever a man sows, that he will also reap”
(Galatians 6: 7).

The spiritual life has consequences; for man sows either to the flesh or to the Spirit, and what he sows appears in the harvest.

29. The Cross and Glory

The Epistle concludes with a most powerful declaration:

“But God forbid that I should boast except in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ, by whom the world has been crucified to me, and I to the world”
(Galatians 6: 14).

For the cross is Paul's ultimate criterion; neither circumcision, nor achievements, nor status, nor religious appearance.

30. Christ in the Epistle to the Galatians

Christ is the Savior, the Crucified One, the Son of God, the source of righteousness, the liberator of man, the center of the promise, and the Mediator who makes the believer a son and an heir.

And the entire Gospel moves around the truth that Christ's work is complete and sufficient, and that adding any other foundation for justification destroys the essence of grace.

31. Links with the Old Testament

Paul links the Epistle to the Galatians to the Old Testament in a special way through Abraham, the promise, the Law, Moses, circumcision, and the offspring.

And he does not place the New Testament in arbitrary opposition to the Old Testament, but reads the history of the Old Testament as part of God's plan pointing toward Christ.

For the promise to Abraham precedes the Law, and Christ is the offspring of the promise, and in Him the blessing reaches the Gentiles.

32. The Grand Movement of the Epistle

Galatians begins with a defense of the source of the Gospel and Paul's apostolic authority, because the churches had begun to move from the gospel of grace to another gospel that adds the works of the Law to faith in Christ. Then Paul proves that his message came from Christ, presents his relationship with the apostles, and describes his confrontation with Peter when conduct threatened the truth of the Gospel. From there, he reaches the heart of the Epistle: man is not justified by the works of the Law but by faith in Jesus Christ, because returning to the Law as a foundation for righteousness means nullifying the grace of God. Then Paul turns to Abraham to demonstrate that justification by faith was present in the story of the promise, and that the Law came with a temporary function leading to Christ. And in Christ, the believer passes from slavery to sonship, from sonship to inheritance, and from division to unity in the body of Christ. Then he declares that the freedom given by Christ is not a freedom for the flesh, but a freedom that serves others through love. Therefore, the believer transitions from reliance on the works of the Law to walking by the Spirit and displaying the fruit of the Spirit. Finally, Paul returns to the cross as the foundation of everything, refusing to boast except in the cross of Christ. Thus, the Epistle moves from the Gospel and grace, to justification, to sonship, to freedom, to life by the Spirit, and then to the cross as the center of all Christian identity.

33. Encyclopedic Summary

The Epistle to the Galatians is a powerful declaration of the freedom of the Gospel and the sufficiency of Christ's work. It confronts every attempt to turn grace into a system of merit, and proclaims that man is justified by faith in Christ, not by the works of the Law. However, Paul does not turn freedom into moral permissiveness; for the person who is liberated from reliance on the flesh enters into a life led by the Spirit in which love and the fruit of the Spirit are manifested. And from Abraham to Christ, from the Law to the promise, from slavery to sonship, and from circumcision as a physical sign to faith working through love, the Epistle leads to a single truth: Christ is the foundation of salvation, the cross is the foundation of boasting, the Spirit is the source of new life, and love is the practical expression of freedom.
