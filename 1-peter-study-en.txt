The First Letter of Peter
1. The Epistle’s Name and the Meaning of the Name

It is known as First Peter and is one of the General Epistles in the New Testament.

The name “Peter” means rock or stone, and it is the name Christ gave to Simon son of Jonah. Peter was one of the twelve and among Christ's foremost disciples, as well as a witness to pivotal events in His ministry, death, and resurrection.

The epistle presents an integrated vision of the believer's life amid suffering and persecution, linking hope in salvation, holiness of life, endurance of suffering, and the example of the suffering Christ.

2. The Author and His Character

The author opens the epistle by saying:

“Peter, an apostle of Jesus Christ, to the exiles of the Dispersion in Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia”
(1 Peter 1: 1).

He clearly presents himself as “an apostle of Jesus Christ.”

The author is Peter, who was a fisherman in Galilee at the beginning of his career, then was called by Christ to become one of His disciples and apostles.

The Gospels clearly portray the development of his character: human impulsiveness, weakness, and denial of Christ, followed by restoration and commission; after the resurrection, he became a bold witness for Christ in the early church.

This gives the epistle particular depth in its discussion of steadfastness after failure, hope amid suffering, and following Christ on the path of endurance.

3. To Whom Was the Epistle Written?

It was written to believers living in various regions of Asia Minor:

Pontus.
Galatia.
Cappadocia.
Asia.
Bithynia.

Peter describes them as “exiles of the Dispersion.”

This expression carries a profound spiritual dimension: the believer lives in the world but does not regard the world as his final homeland.

Therefore, the epistle brings together the believer's historical reality as one living within a society that may oppose him and his spiritual identity as one of God's people awaiting a heavenly inheritance.

4. The Reason for Writing the Epistle

Peter wrote to encourage believers facing suffering, rejection, persecution, and social pressure.

He does not treat suffering as a sign of God's absence, but places it within God's plan for the refining of faith.

Thus, the epistle teaches the believer:

how to live holy when the world around him is hostile, how to suffer without losing his hope, and how to follow Christ when following Him becomes costly.

5. Date of the Epistle

Christian tradition considers the epistle to have been written in the period before Peter's martyrdom in Rome, perhaps in the middle or late sixties of the first century.

Scholars differ in determining the precise date, and there are also academic discussions concerning the place of writing and some historical details.

Therefore, the date should be regarded as a historical estimate rather than a direct biblical statement.

6. The Main Theme

The epistle may be summarized as:

“Living hope and holiness amid suffering.”

The believer may suffer, but he is not without hope.

He may be rejected by the world, but he is accepted by God.

He may lose earthly things, but he awaits an inheritance that is imperishable, undefiled, and unfading.

7. New Birth and Living Hope

Peter begins his epistle with praise to God:

“Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who according to His abundant mercy has begotten us again to a living hope through the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead.”
(1 Peter 1: 3).

Christian hope is not psychological optimism, but is founded on Christ's resurrection.

8. The Heavenly Inheritance

Peter describes the inheritance as:

“imperishable and undefiled and unfading, reserved in heaven for you.”
(1 Peter 1: 4).

Here the contrast appears between changing earthly things and the inheritance preserved by God.

The believer may lose many things in the world, but he does not lose the inheritance that God preserves for him.

9. Tested Faith

Peter says:

“so that the genuineness of your faith, more precious than perishable gold, though tested by fire.”
(1 Peter 1: 7).

Suffering here resembles the fire that tests gold.

But the purpose is not to destroy faith, but to reveal its genuineness and refine it.

10. Faith Without Seeing

He says:

“Though you have not seen Him, you love Him. Though you do not see Him now, yet believing in Him, you rejoice with joy inexpressible and full of glory.”
(1 Peter 1: 8).

This is one of the characteristics of Christian faith: loving Christ and believing in Him without seeing Him physically.

11. Holiness of Life

Peter moves from salvation to conduct:

“but as He who called you is holy, you also be holy in all your conduct.”
(1 Peter 1: 15).

Grace does not abolish holiness; rather, it produces it.

12. “Be Holy, for I Am Holy”

Peter connects the believer's holiness with the nature of God:

“for it is written: Be holy, for I am holy.”
(1 Peter 1: 16).

Holiness is not merely a set of rules, but a call for the believer's life to reflect the holiness of the God who called him.

13. Redemption by the Blood of Christ

Peter arrives at a great soteriological center:

“knowing that you were redeemed, not with perishable things, silver or gold, from your futile way of life inherited from your fathers, but with precious blood, as of a lamb without blemish and without spot, the blood of Christ.”
(1 Peter 1: 18-19).

Here the background of Passover and sacrifices appears clearly.

The believer was not redeemed by gold or silver, but by the blood of Christ.

14. Christ the Lamb

Peter associates Christ with the lamb without blemish.

This opens a broad Old Testament background, especially:

The Passover lamb.
Sacrifices without blemish.
The concept of redemption by blood.

These symbols attain their fulfillment in Christ.

15. The Living Word

He says:

“And this is the word which was preached to you.”
(1 Peter 1: 25).

New birth is connected with the living and enduring word of God.

16. Spiritual Milk

The second chapter begins:

“and as newborn infants, desire the pure spiritual milk, that you may grow by it.”
(1 Peter 2: 2).

The believer should not remain a spiritual child; rather, he should grow in the knowledge of God.

17. Christ the Living Stone

Peter presents Christ in imagery connected with the Old Testament:

“coming to Him as to a living stone, rejected by men but chosen and precious in the sight of God.”
(1 Peter 2: 4).

Christ is rejected by men, but He is chosen and honored by God.

18. The Cornerstone

Peter quotes from Isaiah:

“Behold, I lay in Zion a chosen and precious cornerstone, and he who believes in Him will not be put to shame.”
(1 Peter 2: 6).

Thus, the imagery of the temple, the stone, and the corner becomes the background for understanding Christ as the foundation of the new people of God.

19. The People of God

Peter declares:

“But you are a chosen generation, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for His own possession.”
(1 Peter 2: 9).

This text has a direct background in Exodus 19, where God called Israel to be a special people for Himself.

But Peter applies this identity to the community of believers in Christ.

20. A People for Witness

Continues:

“That you may proclaim the excellencies of Him who called you out of darkness into His marvelous light”
(1 Peter 2: 9).

Election, then, is not a privilege without a mission; rather, it is a call to bear witness.

21. Strangers and Sojourners

He says:

“Beloved, I urge you, as strangers and sojourners, to abstain from fleshly lusts which wage war against the soul”
(1 Peter 2: 11).

This identity defines the believer's relationship to the world:

He lives in it, but does not belong to its evil values.

22. Good Conduct before the Nations

Peter calls for the lives of believers to be a testimony before unbelievers.

For the church bears witness not only through preaching, but also through conduct that others observe.

23. Submission to Authorities

Peter addresses the believer's relationship to civil authority, calling for submission and respect for public order.

He does not present this as a sanctification of authority, but as part of Christian witness and upright conduct in society.

24. Christ, the Example of the Sufferer

Peter reaches the center of the epistle:

“For to this you were called. For Christ also suffered for us, leaving us an example, that you should follow in His steps”
(1 Peter 2: 21).

Christ is not only the one who saved us from sin; He is also the model we follow when we suffer.

25. Christ without Sin

He says:

“Who committed no sin, nor was deceit found in His mouth”
(1 Peter 2: 22).

The suffering Christ is not a guilty person paying the price for his sin, but the righteous One who suffers for sinners.

26. Christ and the Bearing of Sins

The text arrives at the declaration of salvation:

“Who Himself bore our sins in His body on the tree, that we, having died to sins, might live for righteousness”
(1 Peter 2: 24).

Here the background of Isaiah 53 appears clearly, where the Servant of the Lord bears the iniquities of others.

27. “By His Stripes You Were Healed”

Peter says:

“By whose stripes you were healed”
(1 Peter 2: 24).

In context, the healing here is fundamentally connected with the healing of a person from sin and his restoration to righteousness, in light of the context of Christ bearing sins.

28. Christ the Shepherd

He says:

“For you were like stray sheep, but have now returned to the Shepherd and Overseer of your souls”
(1 Peter 2: 25).

Christ here is the Shepherd who restores the stray sheep.

The image is directly connected to the background of Psalm 23 and Ezekiel 34.

29. Marriage and Married Life

Peter addresses the marital relationship, focusing on inward holiness, mutual respect, and Christian witness within the home.

He affirms that a woman's value does not rest on outward adornment alone, but on the inner person of the heart.

30. “Be All of One Mind”

He calls believers to:

Unity.
Compassion.
Brotherly love.
Mercy.
Humility.

For the church is not merely a doctrinal gathering, but a community that ought to reflect the character of Christ in its relationships.

31. Do Not Repay Evil for Evil

He says:

“Not repaying evil for evil or insult for insult, but on the contrary, blessing”
(1 Peter 3: 9).

Here the heart of Christ's teaching on loving enemies and refraining from revenge appears clearly.

32. “Be Ready to Give an Answer”

He says:

“But sanctify the Lord God in your hearts, always being ready to give an answer to everyone who asks you for a reason for the hope that is in you”
(1 Peter 3: 15).

This is one of the most important texts on Christian apologetics (Apologetics) in the New Testament.

But Peter connects the defense of the faith with:

Meekness and fear.

For witness is not a dialectical victory, but a declaration of the hope found in Christ.

33. Christ and His Suffering Once

He says:

“For Christ also suffered once for sins, the righteous for the unrighteous, that He might bring us to God”
(1 Peter 3: 18).

Here the profound purpose of the cross appears:

Bringing humanity to God.

34. Noah and the Ark

Peter uses the story of Noah and the flood within his discussion of salvation and judgment.

Just as the ark, amid the waters of judgment, was a place of deliverance for those who entered it, the text presents a historical image that carries significance for salvation in Christ.

35. Christ and Judgment

Peter speaks of Christ, who died in the flesh but was made alive by the Spirit, then turns to the subject of preaching to the spirits in prison.

This is one of the most difficult passages in the epistle to interpret, and Christian interpretations have differed concerning the meaning of “the spirits in prison” and the nature of the preaching to them.

Therefore, an entire doctrine should not be built upon a single interpretation of this text, whose meaning is disputed.

36. Baptism

Peter connects the story of Noah with baptism, then explains that the intention is not merely the removal of the filth of the body, but:

“The request for a good conscience from God”
(1 Peter 3: 21).

The meaning, then, goes beyond the water itself to the work of salvation connected with the resurrection of Jesus Christ.

37. Christ at the Right Hand of God

The passage concludes:

“Who is at the right hand of God, having gone into heaven, with angels and authorities and powers being made subject to Him”
(1 Peter 3: 22).

The Christ who suffered is the same Christ who is glorified and possesses authority.

This is one of the epistle's most important contrasts:

The cross → glory.

38. Christ, the Example in Suffering

Peter urges the believer to arm himself with the mind of Christ, who suffered in the flesh.

For suffering for Christ should not lead to bitterness, but to separation from sin and consecration to God.

39. Judgment Begins with the House of God

He says:

“FOR THE TIME HAS COME FOR JUDGMENT TO BEGIN WITH THE HOUSE OF GOD”
(1 Peter 4: 17).

This does not mean that the church is immune from discipline or testing; rather, God's own people live before God's holiness and judgment.

40. Gifts and Serving Others

He says:

“AS EACH ONE HAS RECEIVED A GIFT, MINISTER IT TO ONE ANOTHER, AS GOOD STEWARDS OF THE MANIFOLD GRACE OF GOD”
(1 Peter 4: 10).

A gift is not a personal privilege, but a stewardship for serving others.

41. Love Covers a Multitude of Sins

He says:

“BUT ABOVE ALL THINGS HAVE FERVENT LOVE FOR ONE ANOTHER, FOR LOVE WILL COVER A MULTITUDE OF SINS”
(1 Peter 4: 8).

This does not mean that love nullifies Christ's atonement, but that love does not expose others or live according to the logic of revenge; rather, it seeks forgiveness and restoration.

42. “Do Not Be Surprised by the Fiery Trial”

He says:

“BELOVED, DO NOT BE SURPRISED AT THE FIERY TRIAL WHICH IS TAKING PLACE AMONG YOU TO TEST YOU, AS THOUGH SOME STRANGE THING HAPPENED TO YOU”
(1 Peter 4: 12).

Suffering because of faith is not evidence that God has abandoned the believer.

It is part of the experience of belonging to Christ.

43. Sharing in Christ's Sufferings

He says:

“BUT REJOICE INASMUCH AS YOU SHARE IN CHRIST'S SUFFERINGS, THAT YOU MAY ALSO REJOICE WITH EXCEEDING JOY AT THE REVELATION OF HIS GLORY”
(1 Peter 4: 13).

Suffering and glory are connected in the logic of the epistle.

44. Shepherding God's Flock

He addresses the elders:

“SHEPHERD THE FLOCK OF GOD WHICH IS AMONG YOU, SERVING AS OVERSEERS, NOT BY COMPULSION BUT WILLINGLY, NOR FOR DISHONEST GAIN BUT EAGERLY”
(1 Peter 5: 2).

Ministry is not a means of control or gain, but the care of God's people.

45. Christ, the Chief Shepherd

He says:

“AND WHEN THE CHIEF SHEPHERD APPEARS, YOU WILL RECEIVE THE CROWN OF GLORY THAT DOES NOT FADE AWAY”
(1 Peter 5: 4).

Here Peter places all shepherds under the authority of Christ, the Chief Shepherd.

46. Humility

He says:

“LIKewise YOU WHO ARE YOUNGER, SUBMIT YOURSELVES TO THE ELDERS. YES, ALL OF YOU BE SUBMISSIVE TO ONE ANOTHER, AND CLOTHE YOURSELVES WITH HUMILITY, FOR GOD RESISTS THE PROUD, BUT GIVES GRACE TO THE HUMBLE”
(1 Peter 5: 5).

Humility is one of the most important marks of the community that follows Christ.

47. Cast All Your Care upon Him

One of the most comforting verses in the epistle:

“CASTING ALL YOUR CARE UPON HIM, FOR HE CARES FOR YOU”
(1 Peter 5: 7).

Faith does not mean the absence of care, but entrusting care to God, who cares for the believer.

48. The Devil as a Roaring Lion

Peter warns:

“BE SOBER AND VIGILANT. FOR YOUR ADVERSARY THE DEVIL WALKS ABOUT LIKE A ROARING LION, SEEKING WHOM HE MAY DEVOUR”
(1 Peter 5: 8).

Therefore, the believer's life requires spiritual vigilance, resistance, and steadfastness.

49. “Resist Him, Firm in the Faith”

He says:

“RESIST HIM, STEADFAST IN THE FAITH, KNOWING THAT THE SAME SUFFERINGS ARE BEING EXPERIENCED BY YOUR BROTHERHOOD IN THE WORLD”
(1 Peter 5: 9).

The suffering believer is not alone; there is fellowship in suffering within the body of Christ.

50. The God of All Grace

The epistle concludes with great hope:

“AND MAY THE GOD OF ALL GRACE, WHO CALLED US TO HIS ETERNAL GLORY IN CHRIST JESUS, AFTER YOU HAVE SUFFERED A LITTLE WHILE, PERFECT, ESTABLISH, STRENGTHEN, AND SETTLE YOU”
(1 Peter 5: 10).

This verse summarizes the movement of the epistle:

Temporary suffering → grace → establishment → strength → eternal glory.

51. Christ in First Peter

Christ is the theological center of the epistle.

He appears as:

The risen Christ who established living hope.
The Lamb without blemish who redeemed us by His blood.
The living stone rejected by people and chosen by God.
The cornerstone for God's people.
The perfect example in suffering.
The bearer of sins on the tree.
The Shepherd and Overseer of souls.
The glorified Christ at the right hand of God.
The Chief Shepherd who will appear in glory.

Thus, the believer cannot separate salvation from Christ, holiness from Christ, suffering from Christ, hope from Christ, or glory from Christ.

52. Connections with the Old Testament

First Peter is exceedingly rich in the Old Testament, and its most important backgrounds include:

Exodus 19: “a royal priesthood and a holy nation.”
Isaiah 28: the cornerstone.
Isaiah 53: the suffering Servant of the Lord and the bearer of sins.
Psalm 34: seeking life and turning away from evil.
Psalm 118: the stone rejected by the builders.
Leviticus: the call of God's people to holiness.
The story of Noah and the flood.
The image of the shepherd in the Psalms and the Prophets.

Thus Peter does not present the identity of the church as an idea separate from the Old Testament, but sees believers in Christ entering into the story of God's people, holiness, election, worship, and witness.

53. The Major Movement of the Epistle

The epistle begins by proclaiming the new birth and the living hope resulting from Christ's resurrection, then moves to the heavenly inheritance and faith tested by fire. From this salvation, Peter proceeds to call believers to holiness, explaining that their redemption was accomplished through the blood of Christ, the spotless Lamb. He then moves to the identity of the church as a chosen people, a royal priesthood, and a holy nation, calling it to proclaim the excellencies of God. After that, he explains how the believer lives amid a society that does not share his faith, and how his conduct becomes a witness to Christ. The epistle reaches its climax in the suffering Christ, who committed no sin, yet bore human sins on the tree, leaving believers an example that they should follow in his steps. It then turns to marital and ecclesial relationships, the defense of hope, prayer, love, and the endurance of suffering. Finally, it addresses the elders and the church, calling them to shepherding, humility, vigilance, and resistance to the devil, and concludes with the God of all grace who, after suffering, perfects, establishes, strengthens, and enables the believer.

54. Encyclopedic Summary

First Peter is an epistle of hope and holiness amid suffering. It begins with Christ's resurrection, which gives the believer a living hope and an imperishable inheritance, then reveals that faith may pass through fire in order to be tested and to show its genuineness. From this hope arises the call to holiness, because he who called believers is holy. At the heart of the epistle appears Christ: the spotless Lamb, the living stone, the cornerstone, the suffering servant, the bearer of sins, the shepherd of souls, and the chief Shepherd. Therefore, Christian suffering is not viewed as defeat, but as a path on which the believer follows Christ while awaiting glory. The epistle affirms that the church is not a community fleeing from the world, but a people living in the world as witnesses to Christ; its weapons are holiness, good conduct, love for others, gentleness, hope, and prayer. At its conclusion, suffering is transformed from the question, “Why does this happen?” into a certainty: the God of all grace is the one who perfects, establishes, and strengthens, and eternal glory in Christ is the end.
