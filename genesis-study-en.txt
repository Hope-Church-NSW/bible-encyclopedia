Book of Genesis
5
Book of Genesis
Genesis — בְּרֵאשִׁית — Bereshit
1. The Name of the Book and Its Meaning

The Hebrew name for the Book of Genesis is בְּרֵאשִׁית (Bereshit), and its literal meaning is "In the beginning", which is the word with which the book itself begins in the Hebrew text: "In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth." This name is very important because it reveals, from the very first word, that the book does not begin with the history of Israel, but begins from God, creation, and man, then moves gradually until it reaches the family of Jacob, which will become, in the Book of Exodus, the nation of Israel.

As for the name that has come down to us in the Greek, Latin, and then Arabic languages, it is Genesis, from the Greek word γένεσις (genesis), which carries the meanings of origin, source, birth, and creation. From here came the name "Genesis", which is a very fitting name for the content of the book because it presents the origins of many things that would later become central in the Holy Bible: the origin of creation, the origin of man, the origin of sin, the origin of death, the origin of nations, the origin of languages, and the origin of the covenant people.

2. The author and the meaning of his name

Jewish and Christian tradition attributes the Book of Genesis to Moses as part of the Torah, that is, the five books that were closely associated with Moses in biblical tradition. And the Holy Bible itself does not present an explicit statement in the Book of Genesis saying "Moses wrote it", as occurs in some places with respect to other parts of the Law; therefore, we ought to distinguish between the historical tradition of Mosaic authorship and modern academic discussion regarding the sources and editorial stages through which the books of the Torah may have passed.

The name of Moses in Hebrew is מֹשֶׁה (Mosheh), and the Book of Exodus connected the name with the story of his being drawn out of the water when Pharaoh's daughter said: "Because I drew him out of the water" (Exodus 2:10). Many scholars hold that the name may have an Egyptian background, while the text presents a Hebrew interpretation of the name connected to the circumstances of the rescue of the child Moses; therefore, the matter should not be reduced to one definitive meaning without pointing to this linguistic discussion.

3. The birth and death of Moses

The Holy Bible does not provide a specific calendar date for the birth of Moses; therefore, we cannot say in a scientific manner that Moses was born in a specific year without adding that this is merely an estimate based on a particular chronological model of the Exodus. The life of Moses can be historically linked to the period of the second millennium BC, but determining the century itself depends largely on the chronological model adopted by the researcher for the date of the Exodus, whether the early or late model.

As for his death, the Scriptures give very clear information, namely that Moses was one hundred and twenty years old when he died, as Deuteronomy 34:7 mentions that his eyes were not dim nor had his vigor departed. Therefore, we can scripturally establish the age of Moses at death, but we cannot convert that into confirmed calendar years of birth and death; thus, our methodology in the encyclopedia will be not to invent dates that neither the text nor history possesses.

4. To Whom Was the Book of Genesis Written?

Genesis was written in the context of the people of Israel and the covenant world, which needed to know its religious, historical, and theological roots. The Israelite who hears the story of Moses and the exodus from Egypt first needs to know why God chose Abraham, how Isaac came to be, where Jacob came from, why Jacob's name became “Israel,” and how the children of Israel ended up in Egypt.

For this reason, Genesis may be regarded as the historical and theological foundation of Exodus, because the exodus does not in fact begin in Egypt; rather, it begins with the promises God made to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. When the reader reaches the end of Genesis, Jacob and his sons are found in Egypt, making the transition to Exodus entirely natural, because Exodus begins with almost the same names with which Genesis ends.

5. Why Was Genesis Written?

The first purpose of the book is to declare that God is the origin of creation, the source of life, and the sovereign ruler of history, rather than humanity, nature, or the gods of the nations. This declaration was especially important in the world of the ancient Near East, where the peoples surrounding Israel held diverse conceptions of creation, the gods, humanity, and nature, whereas Genesis presents God as the one who creates by his word and establishes order in the universe.

The second purpose is to explain the entry of sin, death, and corruption into the human world; therefore, the account of the fall in Genesis 3 stands at the heart of the early narrative. The third purpose is to show the beginning of God's plan of redemption through the call of Abraham and the covenant with him, so that the book moves from humanity's general problem to God's choice of a person, a seed, and a blessing that ultimately extends to the nations.

6. The Geographical Key to the Book

Genesis extends across a very broad geographical area, beginning with the region of Eden and the land associated with the first rivers, then moving to Mesopotamia, Ur and Haran, then Canaan, and finally Egypt. Thus, Genesis is not a book detached from the geography of the ancient Near East; rather, it continually moves among regions that represented important centers of civilization, politics, and trade.

Among the most important sites associated with the book are Eden, Mesopotamia, Ur, Haran, Shechem, Bethel, Hebron, Beersheba, Sodom and Gomorrah, and Egypt. Some sites can be identified with a high degree of confidence, while others, such as the geography of Eden and the locations of some cities of the plain, remain debated; therefore, these differences should appear clearly in any encyclopedic study.

7. Division of the Book

Genesis may be divided in a way that combines its literary structure and historical progression into two major sections: the primeval history of humanity in Genesis 1–11, followed by the history of the patriarchs in Genesis 12–50. This division is important because it clarifies how the book moves from humanity's universal story to the particular story of one family chosen by God to be central to the covenant plan.

First Section: The Primeval History of Humanity

Genesis 1–11

This section addresses creation, Adam and Eve, the fall, Cain and Abel, the flood, Noah, the Table of Nations, and the Tower of Babel. It provides a biblical explanation for the origins of the problems that will later appear in human history: sin, violence, death, division, and collective pride.

Second Section: The Patriarchs

Genesis 12–50

It begins with the call of Abraham and God's covenant with him, then follows Isaac, Jacob, and Jacob's sons, while Joseph occupies a large portion of the end of the book. Thus, a clear line emerges: Abraham → Isaac → Jacob → Israel → the children of Israel → Egypt.

8. Creation — Genesis 1–2

The book opens with a majestic declaration:

“In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth.”

This sentence is not merely a literary introduction; it is a theological declaration that places God before everything else. The universe is not a god, the sun is not a god, the sea is not a god, and humanity is not the absolute center of existence; rather, all are subject to the one Creator.

Genesis 1 then presents humanity as created in the image of God, while Genesis 2 adds details concerning Adam, the garden, marriage, work, and the commandment. Thus, the book provides a biblical foundation of great importance for understanding human worth, the sanctity of marriage, humanity's responsibility toward creation, and the relationship between obedience and life.

9. The Fall — Genesis 3

Evil enters the story when humanity violates God's commandment, and here one of the Bible's most important theological truths appears: humanity was not created evil; rather, evil entered human experience through disobedience. The consequences of the fall appear in the relationship with God, the relationship between man and woman, and the relationship with the ground; then death enters human history.

Yet amid the scene of judgment, the first indication of future hope also appears in the promise of the woman's seed, who will crush the serpent's head. This is the text that Christian theology through the centuries has regarded as the first gospel, or Protoevangelium. Thus, Genesis not only explains why humanity needs salvation, but from its beginning plants the first seed of the idea that God himself will intervene to address the problem of sin.

10. Cain, Abel, and the Flood

After the fall, violence quickly appears in the story of Cain and Abel, where jealousy becomes murder, and the story of the two brothers becomes an early model of the disintegration of human relationships as a result of sin. Corruption then spreads across the earth until the story of Noah and the flood arrives, presenting extensive judgment while also offering salvation for Noah's family through the ark.

The covenant with Noah appears after the flood, and the sign of the bow in the clouds becomes a reminder that God has established a covenant with creation. Thus, an important idea recurs in Genesis: judgment does not nullify God's purpose for creation; rather, God preserves his plan amid judgment.

11. The Tower of Babel

In Genesis 11, human pride reaches a collective form when humanity attempts to make a name for itself and build a city and a tower. God intervenes, confuses their languages, and scatters the groups, and this event becomes a biblical explanation for the spread of nations and languages.

The transition from Babel to Abraham is highly significant both literarily and theologically, because humanity attempted to make a name for itself in Babel, whereas God later says to Abram: “I will make your name great.” Here the book moves from humanity's project of making its own glory to God's project of bringing blessing through a person who obeys him.

12. Abraham — Genesis 12–25

Abraham is the central figure in the second half of the book, and his story begins when God calls him to leave his land, his kindred, and his father's house for the land that he will show him. God then gives him a promise consisting of interconnected elements: land, seed, and a blessing extending to the nations.

Abraham represents the great turning point in the book, because the story that began with all humanity moves to one man, then to one seed, then to a people who will bear the promise of the covenant. Abraham's life also becomes a school of faith: it contains obedience, fear, and hesitation; the waiting for the promise; the testing of the offering of Isaac; and the faith to which the New Testament bears witness in Romans, Galatians, Hebrews, and James.

13. Isaac and Jacob

Isaac is the son of promise, and his life displays the continuation of the covenant that began with Abraham, whereas Jacob's life becomes more personally complex. Jacob begins as a person with clear traits of cunning and ambition, but he enters a series of divine dealings that gradually transform him until he becomes the father of the twelve tribes.

Among the most important stages of his life are the vision at Bethel, his marriage, his work with Laban, his return to the land of Canaan, his encounter with Esau, and then his struggle at Peniel, where his new name, Israel, is associated with a new stage in his life. Thus, Jacob's story is not merely a family story, but the story of the formation of a people's identity.

14. Joseph

Joseph occupies a large portion of the end of the book. His story is distinguished in that, in its direct narrative form, it does not include repeated appearances of miracles like some other accounts, yet the hand of God is powerfully present in directing events. Joseph is sold as a slave because of his brothers' jealousy, moves to Egypt, then to prison, then rises to a position of authority, and ultimately becomes the person God uses to preserve the life of his family during the famine.

The most important theological statement in Joseph's story is that the evil intended by humanity can be used by God within his good purpose, as is clearly seen in Genesis 50:20. This does not mean that God justifies the brothers' evil; rather, it means that God's sovereignty is able to work even through painful human events and transform them into a means of accomplishing his purpose.

15. Major Characters

The major characters in the book include Adam, Eve, Noah, Abraham, Sarah, Lot, Isaac, Rebekah, Jacob, Esau, Joseph, and Joseph's brothers. Each character has a role within the overall progression, but the human characters cannot be separated from the book's central character: God himself, who begins creation, judges after the fall, preserves Noah, calls Abraham, renews the covenant, and directs history until Jacob's family arrives in Egypt.

This is a methodological point that we will maintain in all introductions to the books: we will not merely ask, “Who are the people?” but will also ask: What is the role of each character within the message of the book? For a biblical character is not merely a name in a list, but part of the book's theological argument.

16. The Most Important Message of Genesis

The central message of Genesis may be summarized in this statement:

God is the Creator of humanity, and humanity fell and came under the power of death; yet God did not abandon his purpose, but through covenant and promise began to form a people who would bear blessing to the world.

The entire book moves from a general problem to a solution that begins in a particular way. After seeing the sin of Adam and Cain, the flood, and Babel, the call of Abraham comes so that blessing may be connected with a specific seed. From here begins the line that will lead in the rest of Scripture to Israel, then to Christ, then to the extension of blessing to the nations.

17. The Theological Key to the Book

If we wish to retain Genesis in one phrase, we may say:

“The Book of Beginnings and Promise.”

It is the beginning of creation, the beginning of humanity, the beginning of marriage, the beginning of sin, the beginning of death, the beginning of the nations, the beginning of the covenant, and the beginning of Israel. Most importantly, it lays the theological seeds that will grow throughout the whole Bible.

Therefore, Genesis contains themes that will later appear on a greater scale: seed, covenant, sacrifice, land, blessing, waters, life, death, redemption, and kingdom. When we reach the end of the Bible, we find that many of these seeds sown in Genesis appear complete or in their final form in Revelation.

18. The Time of the Book's Events and the Time of Its Writing

The events of Genesis cover an immense period compared with the rest of the books. The first two chapters address the origin of creation and humanity, then the narrative moves to early human history, and afterward to the period of the patriarchs. Determining absolute dates for all these events is among the most difficult questions, because the text does not provide a complete calendrical timeline that allows every event to be assigned definitively to a Common Era or Before Common Era year.

As for the time of the book's composition, Jewish and Christian tradition associates it with Moses and the period in which Israel was formed as a covenant people, whereas modern academic schools maintain that the text of the Torah passed through long stages of composition, collection, and redaction. In our encyclopedic study, we will present both views clearly, without turning either a critical or traditional theory into an absolute fact when the evidence remains debated.

19. The Relationship of Genesis to the Rest of the Bible

It may be said that every book after Genesis stands upon the foundation laid by Genesis. Exodus presupposes Abraham and the covenant; Leviticus presupposes the exodus and the tabernacle; Numbers and Deuteronomy presuppose the promise of the land; while the history of Israel continues in light of the covenant with the patriarchs. In the New Testament, the characters and events of Genesis become part of the theological argument of Christ, Paul, Peter, Hebrews, and others.

The New Testament repeatedly returns to Adam, Noah, Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and Sarah, and the concept of “the seed of Abraham” becomes central to understanding Christ's message to the nations. Therefore, Genesis should not be studied as a book about the past only, but as the theological infrastructure of the entire biblical story.

🔑 Summary of Genesis

Name: בראשית — Bereshit — “In the beginning.”
Greek meaning: Genesis — origin/beginning/formation.
Traditional author: Moses.
Author's birth: historically unknown.
His death: 120 years old according to Deuteronomy 34:7, with no confirmed calendrical date.
Audience: the people of Israel in the covenant context.
Theme: creation, the fall, promise, covenant, and the patriarchs.
Focus: God begins the plan of redemption amid a world fallen into sin.
Characters: Adam, Noah, Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, Joseph, and others.
Key: the beginning that explains everything that follows.
Theological line: creation → fall → judgment → promise → covenant → seed → Israel → Egypt.

If God promised Abraham seed, land, and blessing, how will he make from Abraham's seed a people who live in covenant and come out of the land of Egypt?
