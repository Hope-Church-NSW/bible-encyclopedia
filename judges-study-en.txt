Book of Judges
Judges — שֹׁפְטִים — Shoftim
6
1. The Name of the Book and Its Meaning

The Hebrew name for the Book of Judges is שֹׁפְטִים — Shoftim, and its meaning is “Judges.” It is the plural of the word שֹׁפֵט — Shofet, that is, the judge, ruler, or holder of judicial and leadership authority. But the word “judge” in the biblical concept is not limited to a person who sits to adjudicate disputes, because the judges who appear in this book were generally leaders whom God raised up to save Israel from oppression and to lead the people in a time of crisis.

The name itself reveals the nature of the historical period; Israel did not have a central king ruling the whole nation, and leadership was not stabilized in a royal dynasty. Rather, the Lord would raise up a leader in a time of crisis, and then that leader’s mission would end. For this reason, the book becomes a record of a transitional period between the possession of the land in Joshua and the establishment of the monarchy in Samuel.

At the end of the book appears the phrase that summarizes the political and spiritual condition:

“In those days there was no king in Israel. Everyone did what was right in his own eyes.”

This phrase is not merely a political observation, but a theological key to the whole book.

2. The author and the meaning of his name

The book does not mention the name of its author directly, and therefore no specific person can be identified with certainty as having written all its materials.

Ancient Jewish tradition connects the book with Samuel the prophet, while many scholars hold that the book passed through stages of collection and editing, and that its present form is connected with the historical and interpretive traditions that appeared within the framework of what is called the Deuteronomistic History.

What is most important for biblical study is that the book presents history bearing a clear theological interpretation of events. It does not merely narrate Israel’s victories and defeats, but connects each stage of them with the people’s relationship to the Lord.

3. The Time of the Events of the Book

The events of Judges begin after the death of Joshua, when a large part of the land had come under Israel’s control, but peoples and regions remained that had not been fully subdued.

This point is very important, because the Book of Joshua ended with the declaration of the possession of the land, while Judges reveals that the possession was neither complete nor final in practical terms.

Thus Israel lives in a new situation: it has the land, but it is surrounded by pagan peoples, and if it mixes with them and adopts their forms of worship, the spiritual crisis begins.

4. For Whom Was the Book Written?

The book was written for the Israelite nation, and especially for the generations that came after the generation of Joshua.

The generation that knew the works of the Lord in the days of Joshua had come to an end, and a new generation arose that did not know the Lord or the work that He had done for Israel.

This sets before us the problem of transmitting faith between generations, which is one of the most important themes of the book:

A generation that knows God → a generation that learns → a generation that forgets → a generation that falls.

5. Why Was the Book of Judges Written?

The basic purpose of the book is to explain why Israel’s defeats were repeated despite the fact that God had given it the land.

The answer the book gives is not that the Lord failed, but that Israel abandoned the Lord and worshiped other gods.

Therefore, the book becomes a study of the consequences of disobedience, and at the same time a declaration of God’s repeated mercy; whenever the people cried out to the Lord, He raised up a deliverer for them.

So the message is not:

“Israel was strong and then became weak.”

But rather:

“Israel was faithful for a time and then abandoned the Lord, but the Lord continued to show mercy to His people when they returned to Him.”

6. The General Division of the Book

The Book of Judges may be divided into 3 major sections:

First: A Historical Introduction

Judges 1–2

The failure of the tribes to subdue the Canaanites, followed by an explanation of the cycle of falling.

Second: The Cycle of the Judges

Judges 3–16

Othniel, Ehud, Shamgar, Deborah and Barak, Gideon, Tola and Jair, Jephthah, Ibzan, Elon, and Abdon, then Samson.

Third: Internal Decline

Judges 17–21

Micah, Dan, the crime at Gibeah, and the civil war against the tribe of Benjamin.

This division is important because the book begins with external enemies and ends almost with internal collapse within the people of God themselves.

7. The Failure of the Tribes to Subdue the Land

The book begins by recording the continued presence of the Canaanites in many regions.

The Lord had given Israel clear instructions, but the people did not carry out the command completely.

Over time, what should have been an enemy allowed to remain turned into a source of religious and moral influence.

Here a dangerous spiritual principle appears:

A small tolerance of sin can turn into an entire system of life.

8. The Generation That Did Not Know the Lord

Among the most serious verses in the book is:

“And another generation arose after them who did not know the Lord or the work that He had done for Israel.”

This does not mean that they had not heard the name of the Lord, but that the knowledge that should have produced relationship and faithfulness was no longer present in their lives.

Here Judges is directly connected to Deuteronomy 6:

Teach your children.

If society fails to transmit faith from generation to generation, the result appears quickly.

9. The Repeated Cycle in the Book of Judges

The book presents a pattern that is clearly repeated:

1. The people abandon the Lord

↓

2. They worship other gods

↓

3. The Lord allows them to be oppressed

↓

4. The people cry out to the Lord

↓

5. The Lord raises up a judge

↓

6. The judge saves the people

↓

7. A period of rest comes

↓

8. The judge dies

↓

9. The people return to evil

Thus the cycle begins again.

These are not merely repeated events, but a biblical diagnosis of the nature of the human heart when it deals with grace without true inward change.

10. Othniel

The first judge was Othniel son of Kenaz.

When the people cried out to the LORD, he raised up Othniel to deliver them from the king of Aram.

This judge is the closest to the ideal picture in the book: the Holy Spirit came upon him, he delivered Israel, and then the land had rest.

But as time passes, the characters of the judges become more complex.

11. Ehud son of Gera

Ehud was from the tribe of Benjamin, and he was left-handed.

He made a double-edged sword and used it to kill Eglon king of Moab; then he led Israel to victory.

The story once again reveals that the LORD can use a person or a means that seems unexpected to accomplish deliverance.

After Eglon's death, the people gathered and defeated Moab, and the land had rest.

12. Shamgar

Shamgar is a briefly mentioned but fascinating figure.

The book states that he killed six hundred Philistines with an ox goad.

His presence in the record confirms that God can use different means and different people in moments of crisis.

13. Deborah

Deborah is considered one of the most prominent figures in the book.

She was a prophetess and judge of Israel, and she would sit under the palm tree of Deborah, while the people came to her for judgment.

Then she summoned Barak son of Abinoam and led him into battle against Sisera.

Here we find a different model of leadership: God uses a woman who is both a prophetess and a judge at the heart of national events.

14. Barak and Sisera

Barak led the army of Israel against the forces of Jabin king of Canaan, while Sisera was the commander of the army.

In the end, Sisera's forces were defeated, and he fled to the tent of Jael, the wife of Heber the Kenite.

There Jael killed him, and thus the battle became a complete victory.

15. The Song of Deborah

In Judges 5 we find one of the oldest poetic texts in the Bible, according to many studies.

The song attributes the victory to the LORD and reviews the participation of the various tribes.

It also mentions Jael and her role in killing Sisera.

The song is highly significant because it presents the war not merely as a military event, but interprets it as God's work in the history of his people.

16. Gideon

Gideon is one of Israel's most famous judges.

When the angel of the LORD appeared to him, he was hiding and threshing wheat in the winepress because he was afraid of the Midianites.

The angel of the LORD said to him:

"The LORD is with you, mighty warrior."

The irony is clear: the man is afraid and hiding, but God calls him by what he can become through God's strength.

17. Gideon and the Sign

Gideon asked God for signs, the most famous being the story of the fleece that was once wet while the surrounding ground was dry, and then the reverse.

The story reveals Gideon's human weakness, but it also reveals God's patience with him.

Nevertheless, the book does not present asking for signs as a general rule for spiritual life, but recounts the event within its historical context.

18. Gideon's Small Army

Gideon had a large army, but the LORD reduced it until only 300 men remained.

The reason is clear:

so that Israel would not say:

"My own hand has saved me."

This is one of the book's strongest messages:

God sometimes reduces the means so that it becomes clear that he himself is the source of deliverance.

The three hundred defeated the army of Midian.

19. Gideon's Downfall

But Gideon's story does not end with his victory.

After his death, the people made an ephod in Ophrah, and this became a snare that led them to prostitute themselves after it.

Here one of the book's important ideas emerges:

The leader whom God uses is not necessarily perfect.

God can use a person, but that does not mean that everything this person does afterward is right.

20. Abimelech

Abimelech, Gideon's son, is not a judge in the same sense; rather, he appears as a figure representing political ambition and deviation from God's purpose.

He attempts to become ruler, kills his brothers, and then his reign ends tragically.

His story reveals the danger of turning leadership into a personal project of power.

21. Jephthah

Jephthah is one of the most complex figures in the book.

He was a mighty warrior, but he was the son of a prostitute, and his brothers drove him from his father's house.

Then, when Israel faced a crisis, they returned to him and asked him to be their leader.

Jephthah defeated the Ammonites.

But afterward he made a tragic vow concerning his daughter, a story that requires careful reading because of the interpretive debate over the nature of what happened.

22. Samson

Samson is the last of the major judges in the narrative and is one of the most famous figures in the Bible.

Before his birth, the angel announced to his mother that he would be a Nazirite to God from the womb and that he would begin to deliver Israel from the hand of the Philistines.

But Samson's story reveals a profound paradox:

God gave him supernatural strength, but his heart continued to suffer from a serious weakness before lust and revenge.

23. Samson and Delilah

Delilah pressured Samson until he told her the secret of his strength.

After he told her, his hair was cut, the Philistines seized him, and his eyes were gouged out.

Here we see the downfall of the man who should have been consecrated to God.

But God does not end his story at the point of his downfall.

24. Samson's Death

At the end of his life, Samson asked the LORD to remember him and give him strength once again.

He grasped the pillars of the Philistine temple, and the building fell on him and on large numbers of Philistines.

Thus Samson died in his final act of resistance against the Philistines.

But the story carries a painful question:

What might this man's life have become if he had lived in complete faithfulness to the calling God had given him?

25. Micah and Corrupt Worship

After the main story of the judges, the book moves into a period of internal decline.

A man named Micah makes an idol, and sets up a private priest.

This reveals that the problem was no longer only in the worship of the gods of Canaan, but that the deviation began to occur within the house of Israel itself.

Worship became something made according to human desire instead of being subject to the word of God.

26. The Tribe of Dan

The men of Dan search for a new land, and take Micah's idol and the priest with them.

Here the picture becomes clearer:

Religious corruption travels with the community.

Thus the book shows that the problem is not only in the individual, but in society when error becomes accepted and inherited.

27. The Crime of Gibeah

The story arrives at one of the darkest incidents in the Bible.

A Levite man and his companion undergo a tragic incident in Gibeah, and the events end with a horrific crime that leads to the anger of the rest of the tribes.

Then a civil war occurs against Benjamin.

Here we reach the stage in which it appears that Israel's greatest enemy is no longer only the surrounding nations, but internal moral collapse.

28. The Civil War against Benjamin

The tribes gather to punish Gibeah and Benjamin because of the crime.

A bloody war breaks out that leads to the fall of large numbers of Israelites.

This is one of the most dangerous scenes in the book, because it reveals to what degree society reached division.

The book began with the danger of the Canaanites, but it ends with the danger of the Israelites against each other.

29. The Key Phrase of the Book

The phrase is repeated at the end of the book:

“In those days there was no king in Israel. Everyone did what was right in his own eyes.”

This sentence explains the political and moral decline.

But it must be noted that it does not only mean that Israel needed some human king.

For the deeper problem was that the people did not make God and his commandment the final reference for their life.

And therefore the story of the monarchy will later become a complex question: does the people want a king who submits to God, or a king who rules instead of God?

30. The Main Characters in the Book
Othniel

The first of the judges, and an early model of the leader whom the Lord raises up for deliverance.

Ehud

The leader who delivered Israel from Moab.

Deborah

The prophetess and judge who led the stage of victory over Canaan.

Barak

The military commander who cooperated with Deborah.

Gideon

The leader whom God used with his small army to defeat Midian.

Abimelech

A model of personal power and destructive ambition.

Jephthah

A leader who suffered from his past, then God used him at the time of crisis, but he made tragic decisions.

Samson

The man who was physically strong and weak before his lusts, whom God used despite his fall.

31. The Most Important Message of the Book of Judges

The message can be summarized as:

When man forgets God, deviation does not remain confined to worship, but transfers to morals, family, society, and politics; nevertheless God's mercy remains ready to respond to the cry of his people.

The book does not embellish its heroes.

It displays their weakness, their sins, and their tragedies.

This is one of the marks of the truthfulness of the biblical narrative; for the Book does not try to fabricate flawless heroes, but reveals that true salvation comes from God and not from the perfection of the human leader.

32. Christ in the Book of Judges

The book does not present direct prophecy about Christ in the way found in some of the books of the prophets, but it presents a recurring pattern that needs the final salvation.

The people fall.

Then they cry out.

Then a savior comes.

Then the savior dies or disappears.

Then the people return to falling.

This cycle creates a great theological question:

When will the savior who does not fail come?

The judges provide a temporary salvation.

As for Christ, he provides a final salvation.

The judges die.

As for Christ, he rises.

The judges save from earthly enemies.

As for Christ, he saves from sin and death.

33. Samson and Christ

There are some symbolic points of comparison between Samson and Christ, but they must not be exaggerated.

Both had their birth connected to a special divine announcement, and both had their ministry connected to a work of salvation for Israel.

But the difference is fundamental:

Samson was a man weak before sin.

As for Christ, he is without sin.

Samson died in victory over his enemies.

As for Christ, he died and rose victorious over death itself.

Therefore Samson can be studied as a limited and contradictory model, not as a complete counterpart to Christ.

34. The Cycle of the Judges and the Need for a King

The judges create a historical and theological tension that leads directly to Samuel.

If there is no stable leadership, and if every man does what is right in his own eyes, what is the solution?

The book leaves the reader at this point.

Then Samuel comes to begin the transition to:

Samuel → Saul → David → the monarchy.

Thus the Judges becomes the bridge between Joshua and the monarchy.

35. The Time of the Writing of the Book

The events narrated by the book belong to the period that followed Israel's entry into Canaan and preceded the establishment of the monarchy.

As for the date of the composition of the book in its present form, this is a subject of debate among scholars.

Many modern studies hold that the book carries a theological and literary layer connected with the Deuteronomistic traditions, especially in its interpretation of the cycle of rebellion, judgment, and salvation.

Nevertheless, the presence of historical material, names, locations, and ancient events within the book makes it important to distinguish between the date of the events and the date of the final composition of the text.

36. Where did the events of Judges take place?

The events of the book are spread across multiple regions of the land of Israel.

These include:

Ephraim — Galilee — Valley of Jezreel — Ophrah — Shechem — Gilead — Judah — Dan — Gibeah.

The geographical map in the book is transformed into a map of spiritual conflict as well.

The regions that should have been a land of worship became theaters of battles, disturbances, and divisions.

37. Why is the book of Judges so important?

Because it reveals something profound about humanity:

Grace does not mean that humanity cannot return to sin.

Israel saw great miracles.

Yet it returned to idol worship.

And it experienced salvation after salvation.

Yet it repeated disobedience.

This leads us to humanity's need not only for a savior who delivers from crisis, but for a savior who transforms the heart and breaks the power of sin.

And here the book of Judges begins to gradually direct the reader toward awaiting the king and the complete savior.

🔑 Summary of the Book of Judges

The Hebrew name: שֹׁפְטִים — Shoftim — "Judges."
Meaning: The leaders or judges whom God appointed during times of crisis.
Author: Not explicitly stated, though ancient tradition attributes it to Samuel.
Time of events: After Joshua and before the establishment of the monarchy.
Place: The land of Canaan and various regions of Israel.
Theme: Repeated falling, judgment, crying out, and salvation.
Characters: Othniel, Ehud, Deborah, Barak, Gideon, Jephthah, Samson, Abimelech.
Major events: Wars against Moab, Canaan, Midian, Ammon, and the Philistines; the fall of Samson; Micah; Dan; Gibeah; and civil war.
The theological key: "Everyone did what was right in their own eyes."

🔵 The Theological Line from Genesis to Judges
Genesis

God gives the promise.

↓

Exodus

God redeems the people.

↓

Leviticus

God sanctifies the people.

↓

Numbers

The people fail in the wilderness, and God remains faithful.

↓

Deuteronomy

God renews the covenant and calls the people to choose life.

↓

Joshua

God gives the land and fulfills the promise.

↓

Judges

The people enter the land but forget God, so they fall, and God sends temporary deliverers.

And here lies the beauty of the arrangement of the books:

Joshua proves that God is able to give the land.

Judges proves that the problem was not in God's power, but in the human heart.

And Judges leaves us facing a question that cannot be ignored:

If every human savior dies, and if the people return to sin after each deliverance, who is the savior who can end this cycle?

This question leads us directly to the book of Ruth and then to Samuel, where the story of King David begins, and through David a long scriptural line begins that leads ultimately to the Messianic king.
