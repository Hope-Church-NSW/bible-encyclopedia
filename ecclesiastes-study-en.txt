Introduction to the Book of Ecclesiastes

The central idea of the book: Ecclesiastes is one of the deepest books of the Bible in confronting humanity’s question about the meaning of life under the sun. The book does not offer superficial answers about suffering, death, work, money, pleasure, and wisdom; rather, it takes the reader on a candid intellectual journey in which a person tests everything the world can offer, then discovers that when life is separated from God it becomes “vanity and a chasing after the wind.” Therefore, the book reaches a decisive conclusion at its end: “Let us hear the conclusion of the whole matter: Fear God and keep his commandments, for this is the whole of man.”

1. Name of the book and its meaning

The Arabic name is Ecclesiastes.

As for the Hebrew name, it is:

קֹהֶלֶת — Qohelet

The word is connected to the Hebrew root קהל — qāhal, associated with the idea of:

Gathering.

Community.

Assembly.

Hence the meaning of Ecclesiastes: the person who gathers the community or speaks to it in its assembly.

The Greek translation used the word:

Ἐκκλησιαστής — Ekklēsiastēs

From it came the English name:

Ecclesiastes.

Thus, the name conveys an important idea: the author is not merely an isolated philosopher reflecting alone, but a wise person who stands before the community and presents it with the results of life’s experiences.

2. Who is Ecclesiastes?

The book begins:

“The words of Ecclesiastes, the son of David, king in Jerusalem.”

Then it says:

“I, Ecclesiastes, was king over Israel in Jerusalem.”

For this reason, the book has traditionally been associated with Solomon.

However, modern critical scholarship debates the identity of the author and the date of composition, because the Hebrew language and certain literary features of the book point to a later period, according to a number of scholars.

Therefore, in an encyclopedic study, it is important to distinguish between:

The traditional reading, which attributes the book to Solomon.

and

Modern studies, which view the figure of “Ecclesiastes” as perhaps a wise literary persona speaking in the voice of a wise king from the line of David.

This debate should not be concealed from the researcher.

3. The meaning of Solomon’s name

As we saw in the study of Proverbs, the Hebrew name:

שְׁלֹמֹה — Shelomoh

Is connected to the root:

שָׁלוֹם — Shalom

That is:

Peace.

Well-being.

Wholeness.

The connection with Solomon is very important because the book presents an entirely different picture from the image of royal wisdom found in the Book of Proverbs.

In Proverbs, we see wisdom as a way of life.

Ecclesiastes, however, asks:

What happens when a person possesses wisdom and every resource of life, then discovers that death awaits everyone?

4. Solomon’s year of birth and death

The Bible does not record Solomon’s date of birth.

His reign is generally estimated to have occurred in the tenth century BC, approximately:

970–931 BC.

However, it must be noted that these dates concern the historical Solomon and are not direct evidence for the date of composition of Ecclesiastes.

If we adopt the traditional view that Solomon was the author, the book would be associated with a later stage of his life.

If, however, we adopt the modern views that the book was written in a later period, discussion of the “year of the composition of Ecclesiastes” would be entirely different.

5. The date of composition

This is one of the most important issues concerning Ecclesiastes.

The traditional view sees the book as associated with Solomon and therefore places it in the tenth century BC.

However, a large number of modern studies date the book to a later period, often during the Persian or early Hellenistic period, because of the characteristics of its Hebrew language, vocabulary, and intellectual structure.

There is no single date on which all scholars agree.

Therefore, it is best for the encyclopedia to say:

The date of composition of Ecclesiastes is debated. Ancient Jewish and Christian tradition associated it with Solomon, whereas many contemporary scholars date its final form to the postexilic period.

6. For whom was the book written?

Ecclesiastes addresses the person who is trying to understand:

Life.

Work.

Wealth.

Wisdom.

Death.

Time.

Authority.

Pleasure.

Justice.

It addresses especially the person who thinks that possessing more things will give life meaning.

For this reason, Ecclesiastes is highly relevant to modern people.

For the question it raises is not:

How can I possess more?

But rather:

If you possessed everything, what remains?

7. The Reason for Writing the Book

The purpose of the book can be seen in a series of tests.

Ecclesiastes tests:

Wisdom.

Pleasure.

Wealth.

Work.

Glory.

Achievement.

Then all these things are placed before a fact:

Death.

And the conclusion is not that life is without value.

But that man cannot find the ultimate meaning of life in created things alone.

8. 'Vanity of Vanities'

This phrase is the most famous phrase in the book:

'Vanity of vanities,' said Ecclesiastes, 'vanity of vanities; all is vanity.'

The Hebrew word here:

הֶבֶל — Hevel

And it means literally:

Vapor.

Breath.

Mist.

Something that quickly vanishes.

Therefore, the translation 'vanity' may make the reader think that Ecclesiastes is saying:

Nothing has value.

But the meaning is deeper.

For Ecclesiastes says that things are not permanent and are not able in themselves to grant man ultimate meaning.

9. 'Grasping the Wind'

Among the repeated expressions:

'And grasping the wind.'

And the image is very striking.

Imagine a man trying to grasp the air in his hand.

He can feel it.

But he cannot possess it.

And thus Ecclesiastes says about many of man's achievements:

You can work.

And build.

And gather.

And succeed.

But if you make these things the ultimate meaning of existence, you will discover in the end that you tried to grasp something that cannot be possessed.

10. 'Under the Sun'

The phrase:

'Under the sun'

Is among the most repeated phrases in the book.

And it describes life as man sees it within the world, time, and death.

For Ecclesiastes does not deny the existence of God.

Rather, the opposite.

But many of his observations focus on what happens when man looks at life from the perspective of the limited, temporal world.

And here appears the difference between:

Life under the sun

and

Life in the light of God and eternity.

11. The Structure of the Book

Ecclesiastes can be divided thematically into:

Ecclesiastes 1

The problem of life and the cycle of the world.

Ecclesiastes 2

Testing of pleasure, wisdom, and work.

Ecclesiastes 3

Time and the seasons of life.

Ecclesiastes 4

Oppression, loneliness, work, and competition.

Ecclesiastes 5

Worship, money, and wealth.

Ecclesiastes 6

The inability of wealth to satisfy man.

Ecclesiastes 7

Wisdom, discipline, and the limits of man.

Ecclesiastes 8

Authority, justice, and death.

Ecclesiastes 9

Death and man's portion in life.

Ecclesiastes 10

Wisdom, folly, and leadership.

Ecclesiastes 11

Risk, work, and youth.

Ecclesiastes 12

Remember the Creator, old age, death, and the conclusion of the book.

12. Wisdom in Ecclesiastes

The beautiful paradox is that Ecclesiastes does not attack wisdom.

Rather, it says that wisdom is better than folly.

But it discovers that wisdom alone cannot overcome:

Death.

For the wise man dies.

And the fool dies.

And the rich man dies.

And the poor man dies.

The king dies.

The worker dies.

Then wisdom is great.

But it is not the ultimate savior of humankind.

13. Pleasure

Ecclesiastes examines pleasure in a serious manner.

He says that he has not denied his heart anything.

Test:

Laughter.

Wine.

Houses.

Gardens.

Slaves.

Gold and silver.

Music.

Women.

Success.

Then he reaches a shocking conclusion:

Even when a person has much, he can still remain empty inside.

For pleasure can make a person happy for a time.

But it cannot give him eternal meaning.

14. Work

Ecclesiastes does not say that work is evil.

Rather, he declares that work is a gift from God.

But the problem appears when work becomes:

a person’s identity.

his worth.

the reason for his existence.

For a person may work all his life and then die, leaving all that he has built to someone else.

And here the question arises:

Who owns the fruit of your labor after your death?

15. Money

Ecclesiastes confronts a very ancient delusion that still exists today:

If I possess more, I will become happy.

But he declares that:

He who loves silver will not be satisfied with silver.

For the problem is not in money itself.

But in the attempt to use money to satisfy a spiritual need that money cannot satisfy.

16. Injustice

The book does not present a romantic picture of the world.

Ecclesiastes sees:

injustice.

the oppressed.

tears.

oppressive power.

people who find no one to comfort them.

And this makes Ecclesiastes very close to the book of Job.

But the question is different.

Job asks:

Why does the righteous suffer?

And Ecclesiastes asks more broadly:

How can the world be full of injustice, death, and vanity, and yet a person live before God?

17. "For everything there is a season"

Chapter three presents one of the most famous texts in the Bible:

"For everything there is a season, and a time for every matter under the heavens."

Then he mentions:

a time to be born.

a time to die.

a time to plant.

a time to uproot.

a time to weep.

a time to laugh.

a time to mourn.

a time to dance.

And here Ecclesiastes teaches humanity that life is not a single moment.

But changing seasons under God’s sovereignty.

18. Time

Human beings live within time.

But God is not a captive of time.

For this reason the book says:

"He has made everything beautiful in its time, and also set eternity in their hearts."

This is one of the deepest ideas of the book.

For human beings live in time.

But within him he bears a sense of what is:

eternal.

For this reason temporal things are not sufficient to satisfy him ultimately.

19. Eternity in the human heart

This idea explains a large part of humanity’s anxiety.

Human beings want:

something eternal.

But the world in which he lives is:

Changing.

Things change.

The body grows old.

Work comes to an end.

Wealth changes hands.

Generations succeed one another.

But within the human being there is a longing for:

Eternity.

Hence the need for God becomes evident.

20. Death

Death is one of the major themes of Ecclesiastes.

Ecclesiastes is not afraid to address it.

Rather, he places it at the center of his philosophy of life.

Death:

Comes to the rich and the poor.

To the wise and the fool.

To the king and the laborer.

To the young and the old.

This makes death in Ecclesiastes a corrective to humanity’s illusion about itself.

21. Why Does Ecclesiastes Mention Death So Often?

Because when a person forgets death, he lives as though he:

Will remain forever.

He may build his entire life upon:

Wealth.

Glory.

The body.

Work.

But when he remembers death, he reorders his priorities.

Therefore, Ecclesiastes does not use death merely to frighten.

Rather, he uses it to teach a person:

How to live now.

22. Joy in Ecclesiastes

Here there is a very important paradox.

Despite Ecclesiastes’ frequent discussion of death and vanity, it does not call us to despair.

Rather, the following recur throughout it:

Eating.

Drinking.

Joy.

Enjoying the fruit of one’s labor.

But with one essential condition:

That a person regard these things as gifts from God.

Ecclesiastes does not say:

Abandon life.

But it says:

Enjoy God’s gifts, but do not make them your god.

23. God in the Book of Ecclesiastes

God is powerfully present throughout the book.

He is:

The Creator.

The Judge.

The Giver of gifts.

The Lord of time.

The One who holds authority.

Ecclesiastes does not conclude that the world is without God.

Rather, he concludes that without God as its final reference point, the world becomes impossible to understand fully.

24. The Conclusion of the Book

After all the questions comes the conclusion:

“Let us hear the conclusion of the whole matter: Fear God and keep his commandments, for this is the whole duty of man.”

This sentence reinterprets the entire book.

Ecclesiastes was not trying to drive humanity toward:

Nihilism.

Rather, he was demolishing:

Illusions.

The illusion of money.

The illusion of fame.

The illusion of pleasure.

The illusion of work.

The illusion of human wisdom.

The illusion of controlling the future.

Then he returns the human being to:

God.

25. Judgment

The book also concludes with an extremely important idea:

“For God will bring every deed into judgment, including every hidden thing, whether it is good or evil.”

Here we find that life is not without accountability.

Injustice may appear to triumph now.

Evil may appear to go unpunished.

But Ecclesiastes declares that:

There is divine judgment.

This gives moral life a meaning that transcends what we see under the sun.

26. The Main Characters in the Book

Ecclesiastes differs from the historical books.

It contains no large cast of characters moving within a narrative.

But the main intellectual characters are:

Ecclesiastes

The wise man who tests life.

Man

The person who searches for the meaning of his existence.

God

The Creator, the Owner of judgment, and the Source of gifts.

The wise man

Represents the human being who uses reason and experience.

The foolish man

Represents the human being who lives without discernment.

The King

Represents power, wealth, and human ability.

27. The Most Important Events in the Book

Because Ecclesiastes is a wisdom book and not a historical book, the events in it are intellectual tests and personal experiences.

And among the most important of them are:

The test of wisdom.

The test of pleasure.

The test of wealth.

The test of work.

Meditation on death.

Witnessing injustice.

Meditation on time.

Reflection on old age.

The call to joy as a gift from God.

The return to the fear of God at the conclusion of the book.

28. Chapter Two — Testing life with all its possibilities.

Chapter Two is among the most important keys to the book.

For Ecclesiastes does not speak of pleasure theoretically.

Rather, it says that he tested it.

He possessed.

He built.

He planted.

He gathered.

He magnified.

He enjoyed.

Then he asked:

What remained?

And the result:

If these things are the final goal, then they are:

Vanity and grasping at the wind.

29. Chapter Three — Time in the Hand of God.

After man tests his life, it moves to time.

Man cannot control:

The birth.

Death.

The seasons.

The changes.

And therefore wisdom is that man should live in the time that God gave him, instead of trying to possess the future.

30. Chapter Four — Man alone.

Ecclesiastes discovers the problem of solitude.

He sees a man who works and gathers, but he finds no one to share with him.

And therefore this idea comes:

Two are better than one.

And this places human relationships in confrontation with the worship of achievement.

For not all success is genuine if it leads to the isolation of man from others.

31. Chapter Five — The House of God and Money.

The book moves to worship.

And here it presents an important warning:

Do not enter the house of God with empty words.

For worship needs:

Reverence.

Listening.

Fulfillment of vows.

Respect for the holiness of God.

Then it moves directly to money.

As if the author is saying:

Man can be:

Outwardly pious

and

Inwardly enslaved to money.

32. Chapter Seven — Wisdom and Reality.

Chapter Seven presents strong proverbs and meditations.

And among them is that man may learn more from:

A house of mourning

than from:

A house of feasting.

Because pain can teach man truths that he does not learn in times of comfort.

And this idea connects Ecclesiastes to the Book of Job and all wisdom literature.

33. Chapter Eleven — Live and work.

Ecclesiastes does not want to make man passive.

But it says:

Cast your bread upon the waters.

That is, man should:

Work.

Plant.

Take wise risks.

He does not wait for ideal circumstances.

For our inability to know the future does not mean that we stop living.

34. Chapter Twelve — Remember the Creator

The book reaches its climax:

“So remember your Creator in the days of your youth.”

Notice that the text does not say:

Wait until you grow old, and then think about God.

But rather:

Begin with God from youth.

Because youth does not guarantee the future.

And a human being does not know how long he will live.

35. The Description of Old Age

Chapter 12 presents profound poetic images of old age.

The following decline:

The strength of the body.

The senses.

The abilities.

Then death comes.

And the dust returns to the earth.

And the spirit returns to God who gave it.

Thus Ecclesiastes concludes the human earthly journey with a powerful reminder of humanity’s origin and destiny.

36. What Does the Book of Ecclesiastes Indicate to Us?

It indicates that:

Success does not equal meaning.

Wealth does not equal satisfaction.

Pleasure does not equal eternal happiness.

Knowledge does not defeat death.

Work does not grant a human being immortality.

Authority does not grant a human being control over the future.

But:

God gives life its ultimate meaning.

37. The Relationship between Ecclesiastes and Proverbs

There is a beautiful relationship between the two books.

Proverbs says:

Wisdom is the way of life.

And Ecclesiastes says:

Wisdom is great, but by itself it cannot solve the problem of death.

Proverbs teaches:

How a human being lives wisely.

And Ecclesiastes asks:

Why does a human being live at all?

Therefore, Ecclesiastes does not nullify Proverbs.

Rather, it takes wisdom one step deeper.

38. The Relationship between Ecclesiastes and Job

The books of Job and Ecclesiastes share major questions:

Why does a human being suffer?

Why does evil appear to be victorious?

What is the meaning of life?

What happens after death?

But each book addresses the question from a different angle.

Job confronts the problem of suffering.

Ecclesiastes confronts the problem of meaning.

And in the end, both return the human being to:

God.

39. The Relationship between Ecclesiastes and the New Testament

The New Testament does not quote Ecclesiastes as a book about despair.

Rather, its ideas become clearer when we set them before the gospel.

Ecclesiastes says that:

Death brings life under the sun to an end.

And the gospel declares:

The resurrection of Christ.

Ecclesiastes sees the limitation of temporal life.

And Christ declares:

Eternal life.

Ecclesiastes reveals the world’s inability to give the human being ultimate meaning.

And the gospel presents:

Christ as the way, the truth, and the life.

40. The Profound Christian Message of the Book

Ecclesiastes can be read Christianly as a dismantling of every substitute for God.

If you make:

Money your god → you will not be satisfied.

Pleasure your god → you will not be satisfied.

Work your god → you will not be satisfied.

Knowledge your god → it will not satisfy you.

Glory your god → it will not satisfy you.

Because the human being was created for something greater than the world.

Here the voice of Ecclesiastes agrees with the declaration of Christ:

“For what will a man profit if he gains the whole world and loses his own soul?”

41. The Most Important Message the Book Presents

Do not search for ultimate meaning in things that die, because everything under the sun changes and passes away; make God the center of your life, receive His gifts with joy, live wisely, and remember that your life will stand before judgment.

42. What Do We Learn from the Book of Ecclesiastes?

That the question of the meaning of life is a biblical question, not a philosophy foreign to faith.

That God allows Scripture to confront difficult questions without superficial answers.

That a human being can possess much and still remain empty.

That death reveals the limits of every human achievement.

That true joy is a gift from God, not an idol we worship.

That work is important, but it is not God.

That money is a means, not the meaning of life.

That wisdom is better than folly, but it is not salvation.

That the future is in God's hand.

That judgment gives moral life meaning.

That humanity needs God more than it needs any other achievement.

🔑 The sentence that summarizes Ecclesiastes

“Ecclesiastes does not say that life is without value; rather, it reveals that life becomes devoid of ultimate meaning when a person seeks eternity in temporal things. Therefore, it dismantles the illusions of wisdom, money, pleasure, work, and glory, then restores humanity to the true center of its existence: God, from whom the gift comes, before whom judgment takes place, and in whom alone humanity finds the meaning that transcends the limits of the sun and death.”
