Paul's First Letter to the Corinthians
1. The Epistle’s Name and the Meaning of the Name

It is known as the First Epistle of the Apostle Paul to the Corinthians. It is the first of two preserved epistles in the New Testament that Paul sent to the church in Corinth. The epistle realistically reveals the problems of the early church when the Christian faith entered a wealthy, multicultural society, addressing divisions, jealousy, morality, worship, spiritual gifts, the resurrection, and the unity of the body of Christ.

2. The Author and His Character

The author is the Apostle Paul, who introduces himself at the opening of the epistle as “an apostle of Jesus Christ, called by the will of God.” His apostolic character is clearly evident; he combines theological instruction with pastoral guidance, confronting error firmly, yet doing so with the aim of restoring the church to the holiness and unity of Christ.

Paul is particularly associated with Corinth because he founded the church there during his ministry, as recorded in the Book of Acts.

3. To Whom Was the Epistle Written?

It was written to the church of God in Corinth and to the believers called to be saints in Christ. Corinth was an important commercial and cultural city, strategically situated between the Greek mainland, causing the church to live amid a diverse society filled with moral, intellectual, and religious challenges.

For this reason, the epistle reflects the conflict between the holiness of the gospel and the culture of the surrounding world.

4. The Reason for Writing the Epistle

Paul wrote the epistle because of a large number of problems that had arisen in the church, including factionalism centered on leaders, immoral sexual practices, legal disputes, questions concerning marriage and celibacy, food sacrificed to idols, the order of worship, spiritual gifts, and misunderstandings about the resurrection.

Paul also received a report about the condition of the church, and the believers presented him with a number of questions. The epistle therefore came as a comprehensive pastoral and theological response.

5. The Time and Place of the Epistle’s Composition

Paul is thought to have written the epistle around 55 AD, approximately, while he was in Ephesus during his third missionary journey. This dating is connected with Paul’s statements about his residence and ministry in Ephesus, as well as his references to his plan to pass through Macedonia and then travel to Corinth.

This date is a historical estimate, not a date explicitly stated in the text.

6. The Main Theme

The central theme is how the church should live as the holy body of Christ amid a world full of divisions and temptations.

The epistle does not merely address the problems separately; it repeatedly returns to a fundamental question:

How should one who belongs to Christ live as a member of the body of Christ?

For this reason, nearly all the epistle’s themes are connected to three central concerns: Christ, the church, and holiness.

7. Christ and Wisdom

Paul begins by addressing the divisions resulting from attachment to ministers and places Christ at the center: Paul is not Christ, nor is Apollos, nor Cephas.

He then confronts the concept of human wisdom by proclaiming Christ crucified. What the world regards as weakness is, in God’s plan, wisdom and power.

The matter reaches its climax in his statement:

“For I determined not to know anything among you except Jesus Christ and him crucified”
(1 Corinthians 2: 2).

8. Divisions in the Church

The church was divided over the ministers’ identities. Some said they belonged to Paul, others to Apollos, and others to Cephas.

Paul corrects this way of thinking by returning the church to its true foundation: Christ is the foundation, while the ministers are merely servants whom God uses.

Thus, Christian unity is not agreement around the personality of a leader, but union around the one Christ.

9. The Church as the Body of Christ

Paul presents the church as the body of Christ. Believers are not isolated individuals, but complementary members within one body.

This truth becomes the basis for understanding spiritual gifts; differences in gifts do not mean differences in worth, because every member has a function within the body.

10. The Church as God’s Temple

Paul declares that the community of believers is God’s temple and that the Spirit of God dwells in them.

This declaration gives the holiness of the church profound significance; the Christian faith does not regard the believer’s body or the community of believers as an ordinary place, but as a dwelling place of the Spirit of God.

Therefore, corrupting the body or the church through sin is not merely a personal matter; it affects the holiness of God who dwells in his people.

11. Sexual Sin

Paul deals firmly with a serious moral situation within the church and reveals that tolerating sin does not represent genuine love.

Holiness here is not merely an individual concern; known and unrepentant sin can affect the testimony of the entire church.

This section presents the concept of church discipline, which is not intended as revenge, but to protect the holiness of the community and restore the sinner.

12. The Body and Holiness

Paul also addresses the idea that the body is separate from spiritual life. He affirms that the believer’s body belongs to Christ and is a temple of the Holy Spirit.

Therefore, bodily holiness becomes part of Christian discipleship rather than a marginal issue.

Paul reaches a powerful principle:

“So glorify God in your body and in your spirit, which belong to God”
(1 Corinthians 6: 20).

13. Marriage and Celibacy

Paul addresses the church’s questions concerning marriage and celibacy. He affirms the holiness of marriage while also explaining the value of celibacy as a gift that a believer may use in a particular way in the service of the Lord.

He does not present either option as a general rule for everyone, but relates the matter to calling, gifting, and circumstances.

14. Christian Freedom

One of the important issues in the epistle is the subject of Christian freedom, especially regarding eating food sacrificed to idols.

Paul establishes a vitally important principle: freedom should not become a stumbling block to a brother.

The mature Christian does not ask only, “Is this permitted for me?” but also asks, “Does the use of my freedom build up my brother?”

15. Paul and Ministry

Paul presents his apostolic life as a model of sacrifice for the sake of the gospel. He possesses rights as an apostle, but he relinquishes some of his rights so that he will not make the gospel a stumbling block.

One of the important principles in the epistle appears here: true Christian freedom is not the ability to claim every right, but the ability to surrender those rights for the sake of Christ and others.

16. Israel in the Wilderness

Paul returns to the events of Israel in the Old Testament, especially the exodus and the wilderness, to present them as a warning to the church.

Those who came out of Egypt experienced the works of God; nevertheless, many fell because of disobedience and idolatry. Therefore, the believer should not rely on spiritual privileges while living in sin.

Thus, Israel’s history becomes a spiritual lesson for the church.

17. The Lord’s Supper

Paul addresses the church’s misuse of the Lord’s Supper, where the meal had become an occasion for discrimination and selfishness instead of fellowship and love.

Paul directly connects the supper with the work of Christ:

“For I received from the Lord what I also delivered to you: the Lord Jesus, on the night when he was betrayed, took bread...”
(1 Corinthians 11: 23).

Thus, the Lord’s Supper becomes a memorial of the cross, a proclamation of Christ’s death, and a hope in his coming.

18. Spiritual Gifts

Paul devotes three important chapters to spiritual gifts. He affirms that the gifts are many, but the Spirit is one, the Lord is one, and God is one.

The purpose of the gift is not to exalt the individual, but to build up the body of Christ.

Here Paul corrects a fundamental problem: when a gift becomes a means of personal distinction, it loses its biblical purpose.

19. Love — The More Excellent Way

In the midst of his discussion of gifts, Paul presents the chapter on love, one of the most famous passages in the New Testament.

Love is not an additional gift; rather, it is the spirit that must govern the use of every gift.

A person may speak in tongues, know mysteries, and possess strong faith, but without love he is nothing.

For this reason, love becomes the standard of genuine spiritual maturity.

20. The Resurrection

Paul reaches a theological climax in chapter fifteen, where he defends the reality of Christ’s resurrection and the resurrection of believers.

The resurrection is not a secondary doctrine, because denying it destroys the foundation of Christian preaching, faith, forgiveness, and hope.

He affirms that Christ truly rose and that his resurrection is the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep, that is, the foundation and guarantee of the resurrection of those who belong to him.

21. Adam and Christ

Paul connects Christ and Adam through the theme of death and life. For just as death came through a human being, so also the resurrection comes through a human being, namely Christ.

Here Christ appears as the last Adam, who opens before humanity the way of life and resurrection.

22. “Christ the Firstfruits”

The declaration that Christ is the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep carries profound theological significance. Christ’s resurrection is not an event separate from believers; rather, it is the beginning of the harvest that will be completed in the resurrection of those who belong to Christ.

Thus, the empty tomb becomes the foundation of Christian hope in the final victory over death.

23. Victory over Death

The message reaches its climax in Paul’s declaration:

“Where, O death, is your sting? Where, O Hades, is your victory?”

The resurrection overturns the relationship with death; death is no longer the final word, because Christ has triumphed, and believers await the resurrection of the bodies.

24. Resurrection and Practical Life

Paul does not use the doctrine of the resurrection to satisfy theological curiosity, but connects it directly with work and steadfastness.

If Christ has been raised, and if the believer’s work in the Lord is not in vain, then the believer’s present life has eternal value.

Therefore, he concludes this section with a call to steadfastness and work:

“Therefore, my beloved brothers, be steadfast, immovable, always abounding in the work of the Lord, knowing that your labor is not in vain in the Lord”
(1 Corinthians 15: 58).

25. Christ in the Epistle

Christ is the center of the entire epistle; he is the wisdom of God and the power of God, the foundation of the church, the Lord of the Supper, the head of the body, the bridegroom whom the church awaits, and the crucified and risen Christ.

Paul does not present Christ merely as a doctrinal subject, but as the foundation for the reordering of everything: thought, ethics, relationships, worship, gifts, and hope.

26. The Holy Spirit in the Epistle

The Holy Spirit reveals the wisdom of God, dwells in believers, makes the church God’s temple, distributes gifts, and works in building up the one body.

Thus, spiritual gifts appear in the context of the Spirit’s work in the body, not as personal privileges or signs of spiritual superiority.

27. The Epistle’s Grand Movement

The epistle begins with a church rich in gifts but suffering from division. Paul redirects its attention to Christ crucified as the wisdom of God and the power of God, then reveals that partisan allegiance to ministers destroys the unity of the body and that the church is God’s temple. He subsequently confronts moral sins, disputes, and the misuse of freedom, affirming that the believer’s body belongs to Christ and that freedom must be subject to love and edification. He then returns to Israel’s history to warn the church against idolatry and apostasy, and organizes its understanding of marriage, celibacy, food, and conscience before correcting its practices of worship and the Lord’s Supper. After that, he explains the diversity of gifts, the unity of their source and purpose, and places love above every gift as the way that gives ministry its meaning. Finally, he arrives at the resurrection, declaring that Christ truly rose, that his resurrection is the firstfruits of the resurrection of believers, and that death will not have the final word. Thus, the epistle moves from a divided church to a church returning to Christ, from individual freedom to love and edification, and from present problems to the hope of resurrection and the coming glory.

28. Connections with the Old Testament

The epistle clearly draws on the Old Testament, especially in Paul’s discussion of Israel in the wilderness, idolatry, wisdom, resurrection, death, and the first human being, Adam.

Paul uses the events of the exodus and the wilderness as examples and warnings for the church, and he also cites Old Testament texts to establish the resurrection of the dead and God’s victory over death.

Thus, Paul does not view the church as separate from the story of the Old Testament; rather, he reads Israel’s history in light of Christ and draws spiritual lessons from it for the church.

29. The Grand Theological Message

The message of First Corinthians can be summarized in this movement:

Christ is the center → the church is his body → the body is holy → freedom is governed by love → gifts are for edification → the Supper proclaims the cross → the resurrection is the foundation of hope → the present life has eternal value.

The epistle does not address the Corinthian problems as a list of unrelated errors; rather, it reveals that most of them arise when a person moves away from the centrality of Christ and places himself, his knowledge, his gift, or his freedom at the center.

30. Encyclopedic Conclusion

First Corinthians reveals profoundly that a church may possess knowledge, gifts, and activity, and yet continually need to return to Christ crucified and risen. Paul addresses divisions, impurity, selfishness, the misuse of freedom, and disorder in worship and the exercise of gifts, but he does not offer merely administrative solutions. Instead, he brings every problem back to a theological truth: you belong to Christ, you are Christ’s body, your body is a temple of the Holy Spirit, every gift must be used to build up the body, love is the better way, and the resurrection is the foundation of hope and work. Therefore, the epistle reaches its climax in the resurrection, because the church that looks to the risen Christ can live in the present with holiness, unity, and service, knowing that its labor is not in vain in the Lord.
