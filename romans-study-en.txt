Paul's Letter to the Romans
1. The Epistle’s Name and the Meaning of the Name

It is known as Paul's Epistle to the Romans, and it is addressed to the believers in Rome, the capital of the Roman Empire. The title does not mean that the letter speaks politically about Rome; rather, it identifies the Christian community to which Paul presented his comprehensive presentation of the gospel.

This epistle occupies a central place in Christian theology because it presents in an interrelated way the themes of sin, judgment, the law, grace, justification, faith, union with Christ, the work of the Holy Spirit, and God's purpose toward Israel and the nations.

2. The Author and His Character

The author is Paul the Apostle, who introduces himself at the beginning of the letter as 'a servant of Jesus Christ, called to be an apostle, set apart for the gospel of God.' In the letter Paul appears as the apostle to the Gentiles, yet at the same time he carries a deep concern for Israel and for God's times and covenants.

Romans reveals a clear theological maturity in Paul's thought; he does not present the gospel as a doctrine separate from the Old Testament, but demonstrates that justification by faith and God's purpose for the nations are inseparable from God's promises to Abraham and the prophets and from God's work throughout the history of salvation.

3. To Whom Was the Epistle Written?

It was written to the church in Rome, a congregation that appears to have included believers from Jewish and Gentile backgrounds. This is evident in Paul's discussion of circumcision, the law, Israel, and the nations, as well as in his instructions concerning the acceptance of the weak and the strong.

This diversity made the question of unity in the church in Christ a major issue, because the gospel that justifies both the Jew and the Gentile by faith destroys the foundation of boasting and spiritual distinction between them.

4. The Reason for Writing the Epistle

Paul wrote the letter to present a clear exposition of the gospel he proclaimed, to explain the basis of salvation in Christ, and to affirm that righteousness before God does not rest on works of the law, but on faith in Christ.

The letter was also connected to Paul's plan to go to Rome and then to Spain; therefore he wanted the church to become acquainted with his message and teaching and to share in his future ministry.

5. The Time and Place of the Epistle’s Composition

Romans is generally thought to have been written by Paul at the end of his third missionary journey, usually from Corinth, before his journey to Jerusalem. This conclusion is based on the letter's own references and on information concerning Paul's movements at this stage.

No explicit date is given in the book itself, so dating it is treated as a historical inference based on the sequence of Paul's ministry rather than a direct biblical statement.

6. The Main Theme

The central theme is the gospel of Christ as the proclamation of God's righteousness and the salvation of humanity by faith.

Paul sets the key of the letter in the words:

"For I am not ashamed of the gospel of Christ, for it is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes: to the Jew first and also to the Greek. For in it the righteousness of God is revealed from faith to faith, as it is written: 'But the righteous shall live by faith.'"
(Romans 1:16–17).

From this point the letter moves from the problem of human sin to the proclamation of God's righteousness, and then to the new life lived by the believer.

7. The Sin of the Nations

Paul begins by exposing the state of humanity apart from God. Although the nations may know something of God through creation, they did not glorify Him as they should, and they turned from worshiping the Creator to worshiping the creature.

Thus Paul proves that the problem of humanity is not merely a lack of knowledge, but a rebellion of the heart against God and a replacing of truth with falsehood. Sin affects thought, worship, and conduct alike.

8. The Sin of the Jews

After condemning the nations, Paul turns his attention to the Jews, who may be inclined to rely on the law, circumcision, and covenant membership.

Yet possession of the law is not enough for justification; the law reveals God's will and human sin, but it does not grant the sinner righteousness simply by knowing it.

Thus Paul reaches the decisive conclusion: both Jew and Gentile are under sin, and both need God's grace.

9. Justification by Faith

After Paul closes every path to human boasting, he announces the appearance of God's righteousness in Christ. The human being is justified by faith, not on the basis of works of the law.

Abraham serves as the major witness to this; Scripture says that Abraham believed God, and it was counted to him as righteousness. Thus Paul proves that the principle of faith is not the abolition of the Old Testament, but is rooted in the story of salvation itself.

10. Christ and the Atonement

The death of Christ occupies a central place in Paul's proclamation of salvation. In Christ God reveals His righteousness, provides atonement, and justifies the sinner who believes.

Here God does not appear indifferent to sin, nor is humanity able to deal with its sin by itself; rather, the cross appears as the place where God's justice and mercy meet.

11. Adam and Christ

In Romans 5 Paul contrasts Adam and Christ. Through Adam sin and death entered the world, while in Christ grace, righteousness, and life appear.

This expands the understanding of salvation from an individual test to a reality connected to all humanity: Adam represents fallen humanity, and Christ represents the beginning of the new creation and the people of life.

12. Union with Christ

Salvation does not end with the forgiveness of sins. In Romans 6 Paul explains that the believer is united with Christ in His death and resurrection; therefore he should not continue to live under the dominion of sin.

Grace is not permission to continue in sin, but the power that transfers a person into a new life.

13. The Law

Paul addresses the believer's relation to the law, affirming that the law is holy and good, but the problem is not in the law itself but in the sinful human being who is unable to achieve righteousness.

Thus Paul does not present the law as evil or as a means of justifying humanity, but as part of God's revelation that exposes sin and leads to an awareness of the need for salvation.

14. The Struggle of the Human Being and Sin

Romans 7 reveals the depth of the problem of a person who knows what is right but finds in the sinful nature resistance to what he knows is good. This struggle reaches its climax in the cry of the needy person for salvation.

Then Romans 8 comes as a powerful answer: true liberation does not come from the human person's own strength, but from the work of God's Spirit in the believer.

15. The Holy Spirit

The Holy Spirit occupies a prominent place in Romans 8. The Spirit indwells the believer, frees him from the dominion of sin and death, leads him as a child of God, bears witness to his sonship, and helps him in his weakness.

Thus the letter moves from justification before God to the life led by the Spirit of God.

16. Adoption and Sonship

The work of salvation is not limited to removing judgment, but brings the believer into a son-like relationship with God. Therefore he can call God 'Abba, Father,' and become an heir with Christ.

Salvation in Romans is not merely a movement from sinner to innocent, but from slave to sin to son of God and heir with Him.

17. God's Purpose toward Israel

In Romans 9–11 Paul moves to a deeply significant question: what about Israel, the covenant people, in light of the rejection of many by Christ?

He affirms that the word of God has not failed, and that God is working according to His purpose in history. He also explains the relationship between Israel and the nations and uses the image of the olive tree to show that the nations entered by faith, so there is no place for boasting or arrogance.

The discussion ends by proclaiming the greatness of God's wisdom and mercy.

18. Salvation for Jews and Gentiles

One of the most important declarations of Romans is that the gospel does not create two ways of salvation, one for the Jews and another for the Gentiles, but proclaims one Savior, one faith, and one grace.

Thus the church becomes a community gathering those who were far off and those who were near around Christ, not around ethnic or religious privilege.

19. Christian Life

After the deep theological construction, the practical dimension begins in Romans 12. The believer who has experienced the mercy of God is called to present his body as a 'living sacrifice' and to be transformed by the renewing of his mind.

The new life is manifested in the use of gifts, love, service, humility, bearing with one another, and overcoming evil with good.

20. Love

Christian ethics in the letter reach their essence in love. The believer does not live for himself, nor does he use his freedom to cause his brother to stumble, but seeks what is for peace and edification.

For this reason Paul says, "Love is the fulfillment of the law"; because the law that exposed humanity's duty toward God and neighbor finds its practical realization in love.

21. Authority and Daily Life

Paul also addresses the believer's relationship to civil authorities and calls for submission to the public order as long as it does not conflict with obedience to God.

Then he returns to the heart of Christian life: love for one's neighbor, not judging others in disputed matters, accepting the weak, and building unity in the community.

22. The Weak and the Strong

Romans 14–15 addresses differences among believers in matters such as food and days. Paul affirms that Christian freedom should not become a cause of stumbling for another believer.

Here an important dimension of the theology of the letter appears: spiritual maturity is not measured only by what I can do, but by what I can lay aside for the sake of my brother.

23. Paul's Ministry

At the end of the letter Paul explains his apostolic calling to the Gentiles, speaks of his ministry from Jerusalem and the surrounding regions to broad areas, and declares his desire to go to Spain.

Thus Romans is not a merely abstract theological treatise, but is connected to a worldwide missionary message; the gospel Paul proclaims is a gospel that wants to reach the nations.

24. Christ in Romans

Christ is presented as the Son of God, the Lord, the descendant of David according to the flesh, the Son of God in power according to the Spirit of holiness by the resurrection, the Redeemer, in whom justification, reconciliation, and life are found.

Union with Christ is the heart of the transition from death to life; in Adam the old humanity appears, and in Christ the new humanity appears.

25. The Great Movement of the Letter

Romans begins with the proclamation of the gospel of Christ and the power of God for salvation, then reveals that the nations are under sin and that the Jews also cannot rely on the law, so all humanity stands in need of God's mercy. After that Paul declares that God's righteousness was revealed in Christ, that justification comes by faith, and he appeals to Abraham to show that this principle is rooted in Scripture. Then he moves from justification to reconciliation and union with Christ, explaining that those who died with Christ rise with Him to a new life, and that the law reveals sin but cannot free a person from its dominion. In Romans 8 the letter reaches its most glorious peak in the proclamation of life in the Spirit, adoption, hope, and that nothing can separate the believer from the love of God that is in Christ Jesus. Then Paul returns to Israel and the nations, explaining that God's purpose has not failed and that His mercy extends to all, and that there is no place for human boasting before God's grace. Finally, doctrine becomes life: presenting the body as a living sacrifice, renewing the mind, love, service, submission to responsibility, accepting the other, and building peace within the body of Christ. Thus the whole letter moves from sin to salvation, from salvation to life, and from life to love and service.

26. Links with the Old Testament

Romans is one of the New Testament letters most closely connected to the Old Testament. Paul appeals to Abraham, David, Moses, Isaiah, Hosea, the Psalms, and others to show that the gospel does not represent a break with God's previous revelation.

Abraham appears especially as a model of faith, while prophetic texts help interpret God's purpose toward Israel and the nations. Thus Paul reads the Old Testament in light of Christ and sees that the history of Israel and God's appointed times are moving toward the fulfillment of God's saving purpose.

27. The Great Theological Epistle

The theology of Romans can be summarized in an interconnected sequence:

Man is a sinner → the Law exposes sin → man cannot justify himself → God reveals His righteousness in Christ → man is justified by faith → he is united with Christ → he receives new life by the Spirit → he enters into sonship and hope → he lives by love and service.

This shows that Paul does not separate doctrine from life; the gospel that justifies man is the same gospel that transforms his life.

28. The Encyclopedic Summary

The Epistle to the Romans is the most comprehensive presentation in Paul's letters of the essence of the gospel; it exposes the tragedy of man under sin, then declares God's initiative in Christ, whereby the righteousness of God is revealed and reconciliation and redemption are accomplished. Through justification by faith, man moves to union with Christ and new life by the Spirit, then to sonship, hope, and love. At the same time, the epistle addresses the mystery of Israel's relationship with the nations and affirms God's faithfulness to His promises, before turning in its second half to a practical call to a life worthy of grace. Romans can therefore be viewed as an integrated theological journey that begins with **"All have sinned"**, passes through justification by faith, and arrives at "nothing shall be able to separate us from the love of God which is in Christ Jesus our Lord."
