The First Letter of John
1. The Epistle’s Name and the Meaning of the Name

It is known as the First Epistle of John, and it is the first of the three epistles traditionally attributed to the apostle John. The name “John” means “the Lord is gracious” or “the Lord shows mercy.” The epistle is distinctive in that it does not begin with the customary apostolic formula containing the name of the writer and the recipient; instead, it enters directly into its subject by announcing the Word who was from the beginning and the apostolic testimony concerning Christ, whom the apostles heard, saw, and touched. For this reason, the epistle appears more like a profound pastoral and theological testimony intended to establish believers in the truth, protect them from false teaching, and give them assurance of eternal life.

2. The Author and His Character

The epistle is traditionally attributed to the apostle John, the son of Zebedee and one of the Twelve, the disciple whose name was closely associated with the Gospel of John. Although the epistle does not explicitly mention the writer’s name, the substantial similarity in language and concepts between it and the Gospel of John is especially clear in the themes of light and darkness, love, life, truth, knowledge of God, and abiding in Christ. The writer appears as an apostolic witness speaking from the position of one who knew Christ directly, not as a philosopher presenting a theory about Christ. Therefore, he says: “That which was from the beginning, which we have heard, which we have seen with our eyes, which we have observed, and our hands have touched, concerning the Word of life” (1 John 1: 1).

3. To Whom Was the Epistle Written?

The epistle does not mention the name of any particular church or city, and is therefore usually understood as a letter addressed to Christian communities known to the writer or to a wider circle of believers. He addresses them in an intimate pastoral manner, using expressions such as “my little children,” “beloved,” and “children.” It appears that the readers were facing deviant teaching concerning the person of Christ, the nature of sin, and the Christian life. The epistle therefore came to bring them back to the apostolic foundation: the true Christ, right faith, obedience, love, and steadfastness.

4. The Reason for Writing the Epistle

John declares a very clear purpose at the end of the epistle: “I have written these things to you who believe in the name of the Son of God, so that you may know that you have eternal life, and so that you may believe in the name of the Son of God” (1 John 5: 13). The epistle aims at assurance of salvation, but at the same time establishes practical tests of that assurance: How does the believer live? Does he keep God’s commandments? Does he love his brothers and sisters? Does he confess Jesus Christ as coming in the flesh? Does he live in the light or in darkness? Thus, John does not base assurance on feelings alone, but on true faith that produces a true life.

5. Date of the Epistle

Christian tradition and many studies suggest that the epistle was written toward the end of the first century AD, in approximately the same setting associated with John’s ministry in Asia Minor. The text itself does not specify a date of writing, so the proposed date should be treated as a historical estimate. The epistle appears to belong to a period in which the church increasingly needed to defend the apostolic faith against teachings that denied certain foundational truths concerning the incarnation of Christ.

6. The Main Theme

The central theme of the epistle is eternal life in Christ, true knowledge of God, steadfastness in the truth, love, obedience, and discernment between the Spirit of truth and the spirit of error. The movement of the epistle can be summarized in three interconnected words: the true Christ, true life, and true love. Whoever believes in Christ as the apostles proclaimed him, walks in the light, keeps God’s commandments, and loves his brothers and sisters can live in the assurance of fellowship with God.

7. The Word and Life

The epistle begins with a profound proclamation about Christ: “For the life was manifested, and we have seen it, testify to it, and proclaim to you the eternal life that was with the Father and was manifested to us” (1 John 1: 2). Christ here is not merely a human being carrying a message about life; he is the historical revelation of the eternal life that was with the Father. Therefore, the epistle begins with Christ’s eternity and ends by saying: “This is the true God and eternal life” (1 John 5: 20).

8. Apostolic Testimony

John emphasizes that the Christian faith is built on the testimony of apostles who experienced Christ in a real way: “That which we have heard and seen with our eyes, which we have observed and our hands have touched” (1 John 1: 1). This statement has great theological significance because it affirms the reality of the incarnation; Christ was not a spiritual apparition or an illusion, but entered history in a form that the apostles could hear, see, and touch.

9. Fellowship with God

John says: “What we have seen and heard we proclaim to you, so that you also may have fellowship with us. And our fellowship is with the Father and with his Son Jesus Christ” (1 John 1: 3). The purpose of apostolic revelation is not merely to convey information, but to bring the believer into genuine fellowship with the Father and the Son; and fellowship with God necessarily leads to fellowship with God’s people.

10. God Is Light

John establishes a theological foundation for Christian conduct: “And this is the message that we have heard from him and proclaim to you: God is light, and there is no darkness in him at all” (1 John 1: 5). Therefore, fellowship with God cannot be merely a verbal claim; whoever says that he is in fellowship with God while continually living in darkness contradicts the revelation of God’s nature.

11. Walking in the Light

He says:

“But if we walk in the light, as he is in the light, we have fellowship with one another, and the blood of Jesus Christ his Son cleanses us from every sin” (1 John 1: 7).

The light here is not merely knowledge, but a life exposed before God. Significantly, John brings together walking in the light and the blood of Christ; holiness is not a denial of the existence of sin, but a life in God’s light, where confession, forgiveness, and cleansing are found.

12. The Reality of Sin

John warns against denying sin, saying: “If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us” (1 John 1: 8). Christianity does not begin with the claim that a person is without sin, but with the acknowledgment of his need for salvation. Therefore, confession of sin is not a sign of weakness in faith, but part of walking in the light.

13. Confession and Forgiveness

John declares:

“If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness” (1 John 1: 9).

Here God’s faithfulness, justice, and mercy come together; forgiveness is not the ignoring of sin, but is connected with Christ’s atoning work, which John will mention afterward.

14. Christ Our Advocate

He says:

“My little children, I am writing these things to you so that you may not sin. But if anyone does sin, we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous” (1 John 2: 1).

John does not present forgiveness as an excuse for continuing in sin; he first says, “so that you may not sin,” and then declares at the same time that the believer’s fall does not mean the end of his relationship with God, because Jesus Christ the righteous is the advocate with the Father.

15. Christ the Propitiation for Sins

He adds:

“And he is the propitiation for our sins, and not for ours only, but also for the sins of the whole world” (1 John 2: 2).

Here the universal dimension of Christ’s work becomes evident; his atoning death is the basis for proclaiming the possibility of salvation for the whole world, without meaning that everyone is saved without faith.

16. Knowing God and Obedience

John establishes a practical test for knowing God: “And by this we know that we have come to know him: if we keep his commandments” (1 John 2: 3). True knowledge of God is not an intellectual claim, but a relationship that affects the will and conduct. Therefore, for John, obedience is not an alternative to grace, but the fruit of genuine knowledge of God.

17. The Commandment of Love

John says that the commandment is not merely new, but is connected with the message that believers heard from the beginning:

“Whoever says that he is in the light while hating his brother is still in darkness” (1 John 2: 9).

Here love becomes a spiritual test; it is impossible for a person to claim that he lives in God’s light while harboring continual hatred toward his brother.

18. Love of the World

John warns:

"Do not love the world or the things in the world. If anyone loves the world, the love of the Father is not in him" (1 John 2:15).

The meaning is not hatred of creation or human beings, but rejection of the value system that rebels against God; for John explains that what is in the world is associated with the lust of the flesh, the lust of the eyes, and the pride of life.

19. The Passing of the World and the Endurance of God's Will

He says:

"And the world is passing away, and its lust; but the one who does the will of God abides forever" (1 John 2:17).

Here John establishes a contrast between what is passing and what is eternal; the world with its lusts is passing away, but obedience to God has eternal value.

20. The Antichrist

John warns of the appearance of "the antichrist," saying: "Little children, it is the last hour, and as you heard that the antichrist is coming, even now many antichrists have come" (1 John 2:18). And John does not focus only on a single eschatological figure, but also speaks of a spirit or movement of denial of Christ that had already appeared in his time.

21. Departure from the Community

He says concerning some who went astray:

"They went out from us, but they were not of us; for if they had been of us, they would have remained with us" (1 John 2:19).

This reveals the danger of internal deviation; the problem is not always external persecution, but may come from people who knew the Christian community and then departed from the foundation of the faith.

22. The Anointing from the Holy One

He says:

"But you have an anointing from the Holy One, and you know all things" (1 John 2:20).

John relates the believer's ability to discern to God's work in his life; the Holy Spirit leads the believer to the truth received from the apostles, not to new teaching that contradicts the apostolic gospel.

23. Denial of the Son and the Father

John presents a decisive doctrinal test:

"Who is the liar but the one who denies that Jesus is the Christ? This is the antichrist, who denies the Father and the Son" (1 John 2:22).

Here he affirms that knowledge of God cannot be separated from a correct confession of Jesus Christ.

24. Abiding in the Son and the Father

He says:

"Whoever confesses the Son has the Father also. Whoever denies the Son does not have the Father" (1 John 2:23).

This is among the strongest statements in the epistle concerning the unity of the knowledge of the Father and the Son in salvation; there is no true knowledge of the Father while rejecting the Son.

25. Children of God

John reaches one of his greatest declarations:

"See what kind of love the Father has given us, that we should be called children of God" (1 John 3:1).

The believer's fundamental identity is not merely that of a person whose sins have been forgiven, but that he has become a child of God. This sonship produces a different life; therefore, he says that the world does not know believers because it did not know God.

26. Hope in the Appearing of Christ

He says:

"Beloved, now we are children of God, and what we will be has not yet been revealed. But we know that when He is revealed, we shall be like Him, for we shall see Him as He is" (1 John 3:2).

John's eschatological hope is not merely escape from the world, but conformity to Christ at His appearing; therefore, hope produces a pure life.

27. Purification and Hope

He says:

"And everyone who has this hope in Him purifies himself, just as He is pure" (1 John 3:3).

True hope does not lead to spiritual laziness; rather, the nearer the believer comes to anticipating Christ's appearing, the more concerned he becomes to live a pure life before Him.

28. Sin and Lawlessness

John provides a clear definition:

"Everyone who sins also commits lawlessness, and sin is lawlessness" (1 John 3:4).

Sin is not merely a human mistake or psychological weakness, but rebellion against the will of God. Therefore, a life of continual sin cannot be the normal pattern for one who claims to abide in Christ.

29. Christ's Work to Remove Sin

He says:

"And you know that He was revealed to take away our sins, and in Him there is no sin" (1 John 3:5).

Christ came not only to forgive the consequences of sin, but to confront the root and dominion of sin. Therefore, union with Christ must produce a practical separation from a sinful pattern of life.

30. Children of God and Children of the Devil

John uses strong language for spiritual discernment:

"By this the children of God and the children of the devil are revealed: whoever does not practice righteousness is not of God, nor is the one who does not love his brother" (1 John 3:10).

Here John joins righteousness and love as two clear signs of God's work in a person's life.

31. Cain and Abel

John cites the story of Cain and Abel to show that hatred can become murder. He says: "Do not be like Cain, who was of the evil one and murdered his brother" (1 John 3:12). Thus, he recalls a story from Genesis to reveal the roots of hatred and evil in the conflict between the children of God and the children of evil.

32. Love as Evidence of Passing from Death to Life

He says:

"We know that we have passed from death to life, because we love the brothers. The one who does not love his brother remains in death" (1 John 3:14).

Love here is not merely a social virtue, but a sign of the spiritual transition from death to life.

33. Love and Self-Sacrifice

John directly connects love with the cross:

"By this we have known love: that He laid down His life for us; and we ought to lay down our lives for the brothers" (1 John 3:16).

Here Christ becomes the perfect standard of love; biblical love is not words alone, but self-giving.

34. Love in Deed and Truth

He says:

"My little children, let us not love in word or in tongue, but in deed and truth" (1 John 3:18).

This is among the most practical statements in the epistle; love that does not become action is merely a claim.

35. The Heart Before God

John offers profound consolation:

"For if our heart condemns us, God is greater than our heart and knows all things" (1 John 3:20).

The conscience may be harsh, but the believer's assurance rests not only on his changing feelings, but on God's complete knowledge, mercy, and faithfulness.

36. Testing the Spirits

John turns to the subject of discernment:

"Beloved, do not believe every spirit, but test the spirits: whether they are from God. For many false prophets have gone out into the world" (1 John 4:1).

The church is not called to accept every spiritual teaching without examination; rather, it must test teaching according to the apostolic revelation concerning Christ.

37. Christ Coming in the Flesh

John gives a decisive criterion:

"By this you know the Spirit of God: every spirit that confesses that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh is from God" (1 John 4:2).

This text is of great importance in defending the reality of the incarnation, because John sees denial of Christ's coming in the flesh not as a secondary difference, but as a deviation that touches the essence of faith.

38. God Is Love

John reaches the epistle's most famous declarations:

"Beloved, let us love one another, because love is from God; and everyone who loves has been born of God and knows God. The one who does not love has not known God, because God is love" (1 John 4:7-8).

“God is love” does not mean that love is God's only attribute, but declares that love belongs essentially to God's nature, work, and relationship with His creation.

39. God's Love Was Revealed in Sending His Son

He says:

"By this the love of God was revealed in us: that God has sent His only Son into the world, so that we might live through Him" (1 John 4:9).

Divine love is not an abstract concept; it entered history in the sending of the Son, and love became associated with the incarnation, the cross, and life.

40. We Love Him Because He Loved Us First

He says:

"We love Him because He first loved us" (1 John 4:19).

This is an important principle in the theology of grace: human love for God is not the starting point, but a response to God's prior love.

41. Love of God and Love of One's Brother

John sets forth the relationship decisively:

"If anyone says, 'I love God,' and hates his brother, he is a liar. For the one who does not love his brother whom he has seen, how can he love God whom he has not seen?" (1 John 4:20).

Thus, vertical love for God and horizontal love for one's brother are not separate subjects; rather, love for God is manifested in the way the believer treats his brother.

42. Belief That Jesus Is the Christ

John declares:

“Whoever believes that Jesus is the Christ has been born of God” (1 John 5: 1).

Faith here is not merely the acknowledgment that Jesus is a historical figure, but faith in Him as the Christ whom God sent and as His Son, in whom is life.

43. Faith That Overcomes the World

He says:

“For whatever is born of God overcomes the world. And this is the victory that has overcome the world: our faith” (1 John 5: 4).

For John, victory is not political or social domination, but the believer's steadfastness in faith amid a world system hostile to God.

44. The Water, the Blood, and the Spirit

John speaks of the testimony concerning Christ, saying:

“This is He who came by water and blood: Jesus Christ. Not by water only, but by water and blood. And the Spirit is the one who bears witness, because the Spirit is the truth” (1 John 5: 6).

Throughout history, this text has been associated with various interpretations, but its central direction is clear: God provides a manifold testimony to Christ, connected with the reality of His incarnation, His saving work, and the testimony of the Holy Spirit.

45. God's Testimony Concerning His Son

He says:

“And this is the testimony: that God has given us eternal life, and this life is in His Son. He who has the Son has life; he who does not have the Son of God does not have life” (1 John 5: 11-12).

Here John reaches the heart of his message: eternal life is not merely a gift separate from Christ; life is in the Son Himself.

46. Assurance of Eternal Life

As noted previously, John declares the reason for his writing:

“I have written these things to you who believe in the name of the Son of God, that you may know that you have eternal life” (1 John 5: 13).

This makes assurance one of the principal purposes of the epistle; God does not desire the believer to live continually in uncertainty concerning his relationship with Christ, but to know the basis of his hope.

47. Prayer According to God's Will

He says:

“And this is the confidence that we have before Him: if we ask anything according to His will, He hears us” (1 John 5: 14).

For John, confidence in prayer does not mean that God is obliged to fulfill every human desire, but that the believer may approach God with confidence when his request accords with God's will.

48. Prayer for a Brother

John speaks of the believer who sees his brother sinning, and says that there is a sin not leading to death; he calls the believer to pray for him (1 John 5: 16). This reveals that Christian love is not content merely with not harming one's brother, but bears responsibility for him before God through prayer and spiritual concern.

49. We Do Not Live in Sin

He says:

“We know that everyone who is born of God does not sin; rather, the one born of God keeps himself, and the evil one does not touch him” (1 John 5: 18).

This statement must be understood in light of the first chapter, which rejects denying the existence of sin. Therefore, John does not mean that a believer cannot fall into sin, but that sin is not the continuing pattern of his life or his natural identity after being born of God.

50. The Whole World Lies in the Evil One

He says:

“We know that we are of God, and the whole world lies in the evil one” (1 John 5: 19).

Here the contrast appears between the believer's identity in God and the world system that lies under the influence of the evil one. This explains many of the preceding warnings concerning love for the world, the antichrist, and the spirit of error.

51. The Son Has Given Us Understanding

John concludes with a declaration of great importance:

“And we know that the Son of God has come and has given us understanding, that we may know Him who is true. And we are in Him who is true, in His Son Jesus Christ. This is the true God and eternal life” (1 John 5: 20).

Here all the themes of the epistle converge: the coming, knowledge, truth, the Son, and eternal life. For Christ is the one who reveals the truth, and life is found in Him.

52. The Warning Against Idols

The epistle ends with brief but exceedingly powerful words:

“Little children, keep yourselves from idols. Amen” (1 John 5: 21).

After all this discussion of knowing God, the Son, and eternal life, John concludes with a warning against idols, as though the epistle were saying that true knowledge of God must protect the believer from every substitute that seeks to take God's place in the heart.

53. Christ in First John

Christ is the center of the epistle from its first word to its final content: He is the Word of life, the eternal life that was manifested, Jesus Christ the righteous, the Advocate with the Father, the propitiation for sins, the Son sent by the Father, the Christ who came in the flesh, the Son in whom is eternal life, and the one through whom humanity knows the true God. Therefore, John's defense of Christian doctrine is not a defense of a theory, but a defense of the person of Christ; if the truth concerning Christ is distorted, salvation, fellowship, and eternal life are distorted.

54. The Holy Spirit in the Epistle

The Holy Spirit appears especially as the Spirit of truth and the witness to Christ. John says: “And by this we know that He abides in us: by the Spirit whom He has given us” (1 John 3: 24), and he also affirms that the Spirit bears witness to Christ in the fifth chapter. In the epistle, the Holy Spirit is not a source of experiences detached from the gospel, but works to establish the believer in apostolic truth and faith in Christ.

55. Connections with the Old Testament

Although the epistle does not quote the Old Testament in the extensive manner found in some of Paul's epistles, its concepts are deeply rooted in the Old Testament: God is light; holiness, justice, love, righteousness, and the distinction between the children of God and the children of evil are all themes with a clear covenantal background. John recalls the story of Cain and Abel in Genesis 4 to explain the nature of hatred. Likewise, the concept of walking in the light extends the Old Testament language of God as light and holy, while the concept of knowing God is connected with God's covenant with His people and the call to know the Lord and walk in His ways.

56. The Relationship Between First John and the Gospel of John

The relationship between First John and the Gospel of John is very close linguistically and theologically; both speak of life, light, darkness, love, truth, the world, the Son, faith, and knowing God. The Gospel begins with the declaration of the Word who was in the beginning, whereas the epistle begins with testimony concerning Him who was from the beginning. The Gospel declares that life was in the Word, whereas the epistle affirms that eternal life was manifested in Christ. Therefore, First John may be read as a pastoral and practical confirmation of many truths proclaimed by the Gospel of John.

57. The Three Tests of Christian Life

The method of the epistle may be summarized in three interconnected tests: a doctrinal test concerning right faith in Jesus Christ, especially the confession that He came in the flesh and that He is the Son of God; a moral test concerning walking in the light, obedience, and rejecting a sinful pattern of life; and a fraternal test concerning the believer's love for his brothers. These tests do not mean that a person is saved by works, but reveal that true faith produces sound doctrine, holy conduct, and genuine love.

58. The Major Movement of the Epistle

The epistle begins with Christ, who was from the beginning, whom the apostles heard, saw, and touched. It then declares that this eternal life was manifested so that believers might enter into fellowship with the Father and the Son. From there, John moves to walking in the light, confessing sin, and confidence in Christ's advocacy and propitiation; then to obedience, love, and rejecting love for the world. After that, he addresses the appearance of antichrists and the denial of the Son, and affirms the necessity of abiding in the truth and in the anointing from the Holy One. He then moves to the believer's identity as a child of God, the hope of Christ's appearing, and the necessity of purification, and sets before the church the moral test and brotherly love. In the fourth chapter, he calls for testing the spirits and rejecting the spirit of error, then declares that God is love and that God's love was manifested in the sending of His Son. Finally, he reaches faith that overcomes the world, God's testimony to the Son, eternal life, assurance, and prayer; then he concludes by declaring that the Son of God has come and given us understanding to know the truth, and with the final warning: “Keep yourselves from idols.”

59. The Most Important Theological Message

The greatest emphasis of First John is that faith in Christ cannot be separated from knowing God and new life. It is not enough for a person to say that he knows God; the effect of this knowledge must be evident in his conduct. It is not enough to speak of love; it must be practiced. It is not enough to confess the name of Christ; one must confess Christ as the church received Him from apostolic testimony. Nor is it enough to speak of eternal life; one must now live in fellowship with God and in the light of Christ.

60. Encyclopedic Summary

First John is an epistle of assurance, truth, love, and eternal life. It begins with apostolic testimony concerning Christ, who was from the beginning and was manifested in the flesh, and declares that the purpose of this testimony is to bring believers into fellowship with the Father and the Son and to grant them complete joy. It then sets forth the path of Christian life in the light, with confession of sin and confidence in Christ as Advocate and propitiation, and presents obedience and love as two marks of true knowledge of God. It strongly warns against denying Christ, false teachers, and antichrists, and affirms the necessity of testing the spirits and holding fast to apostolic teaching. At the heart of the epistle, John declares that God is love, and that this love was manifested practically when the Father sent His Son so that we might live through Him. Therefore, a person cannot claim to love God while hating his brother. The epistle concludes with assurance: the believer has eternal life in the Son, and the Son of God came to give him knowledge of the truth; in the Son are the true God and eternal life. Thus, John moves from testimony to fellowship, from fellowship to light, from light to obedience, from obedience to love, and from love to assurance, all centered on one person: Jesus Christ, the Son of God.

Primary Biblical Source

The Holy Bible — Van Dyke Translation, with primary reliance on the text of First John, relating its themes to the Gospel of John, the Book of Genesis, Psalms, Isaiah, and the remaining biblical passages concerning light, holiness, love, incarnation, propitiation, and eternal life.
