The Book of Revelation
1. Name of the book and its meaning

The book is known as the Book of Revelation, and its name is taken from the opening of it: 'The revelation of Jesus Christ, which God gave him to show to his servants what must soon take place, and he made it known by sending his angel to his servant John' (Revelation 1:1). The Greek word Apokalypsis means unveiling, manifestation, or the lifting of the veil from something that had been hidden. Therefore, the book is not focused primarily on 'mystery' so much as on the proclamation of what God desires to reveal about Jesus Christ, the church, judgment, and God's final victory.

2. The Author and His Character

The author presents himself several times by the name John, saying, 'I, John, your brother and companion in the tribulation and kingdom and patient endurance of Jesus Christ' (Revelation 1:9). Early Christian tradition attributes the book to John the Apostle, the disciple of Christ and the author of the Gospel and the Johannine Epistles. John appears here not as a distant observer of the church's sufferings, but as a partner with her in tribulation, the kingdom, and perseverance.

3. To whom was the book written?

The book was addressed directly to seven churches in Asia: Ephesus, Smyrna, Pergamum, Thyatira, Sardis, Philadelphia, and Laodicea (Revelation 1:11). These churches represented real historical communities in Asia Minor, yet at the same time they provided multiple models of the church's condition before Christ: a church that labors but has lost its first love, a church that suffers and remains steadfast, a church exposed to doctrinal and moral laxity, a church that appears alive but is spiritually dead, a church of little strength but faithful, and a church that is lukewarm and needs repentance.

4. The reason for writing the book

The purpose is to proclaim the authority of Christ and encourage the church to persevere amid tribulation, while revealing the final judgment of evil and the victory of God and Christ. For this reason, the book begins with a blessing for the one who reads, hears, and keeps: 'Blessed is the one who reads aloud and those who hear the words of the prophecy and keep what is written in it, for the time is near' (Revelation 1:3). The Revelation is not a book to satisfy curiosity about the future, but a message to transform the church's present in light of Christ's coming victory.

5. The date of composition

The majority of scholars incline to the view that the Book of Revelation was written in the latter half of the first century AD, and its date is often placed in the reign of Emperor Domitian, around 95 CE, although other views propose an earlier date. John states that he was on the island of Patmos 'for the word of God and for the testimony of Jesus Christ' (Revelation 1:9), which places the book in the context of pressure, persecution, and suffering experienced by the early church.

6. The Main Theme

The central focus of the Book of Revelation is the victory of Jesus Christ the King over the powers of evil, the fulfillment of God's purpose in history, the judgment of evil, the salvation of God's people, and the appearance of the new creation. Therefore, the book should not be read merely as a sequence of disasters; the grand story begins with the risen and glorified Christ, passes through conflict, judgment, and witness, and ends with God dwelling with humanity, death no more, the new Jerusalem, the river of the water of life, and the tree of life.

7. The Revelation of Jesus Christ

The book begins with an important theological phrase: 'the revelation of Jesus Christ' (Revelation 1:1). It can be understood in two ways: the Revelation is a declaration concerning Christ, and at the same time it is a revelation coming from Christ. Therefore, the center of the book is not the beasts, the seals, or the trumpets, but the person of Jesus Christ. All the symbols and events ultimately move toward the unveiling of his authority, glory, and victory.

8. The First and the Last

Christ says, 'I am the Alpha and the Omega, the beginning and the end, says the Lord, who is and who was and who is to come, the Almighty' (Revelation 1:8). The book repeatedly shows that history is not without purpose or direction; God is the beginning and the end, and Christ holds authority over the past, the present, and the future. Therefore, evil cannot have the final word in history.

9. Christ in the Midst of the Churches

John sees Christ 'like a son of man' in the midst of the seven lampstands (Revelation 1:13). This image connects the book to the visions of Daniel, particularly the vision of 'the Son of Man' in Daniel 7. Christ is not distant from the church here; he walks among her, sees her works, and knows her toil, faithfulness, and deviation. This makes the messages to the seven churches truly the direct words of Christ to his church.

10. Christ the Priest and King

Christ appears with a long robe and a golden sash, his hair white like wool, his eyes like blazing fire, and his voice like the sound of many waters (Revelation 1:13–15). The image gathers together divine glory, royal authority, judgment, and priesthood. Christ is not merely a Savior who suffered in the past, but the risen and glorified Lord who examines his church, judges evil, and reigns forever.

11. Christ Who Died and Rose Again

Christ says, 'I was dead, and behold, I am alive forever and ever. Amen. And I have the keys of death and Hades' (Revelation 1:18). Here death, resurrection, and authority are bound together in a single proclamation; Christ entered death but emerged victorious, and therefore death itself is now under his authority.

12. The Messages to the Seven Churches

The messages addressed to the seven churches occupy chapters 2–3, and they represent the first practical application of the Revelation. Christ praises, rebukes, calls to repentance, and offers promises to the overcomers. This reveals that knowledge of the future has no value if it does not lead to spiritual faithfulness in the present.

13. Ephesus

Christ says to the church in Ephesus, 'I have this against you, that you have abandoned the love you had at first' (Revelation 2:4). The church was strong in labor, endurance, and discernment against evil, but it had lost something essential: love. Here the Revelation declares that the health of the church is not measured by activity and service alone, but by the condition of the heart in relation to Christ.

14. Smyrna

As for Smyrna, Christ says to it, 'Be faithful unto death, and I will give you the crown of life' (Revelation 2:10). Christ does not promise this church the removal of affliction, but the promise of life in the midst of affliction and beyond it. Here the logic of the Revelation appears: faithfulness matters more than comfort, and resurrection is stronger than death.

15. Pergamum

Pergamum lived in a hostile spiritual environment, yet Christ says that some believers held fast to his name. But he rebukes the presence of the teaching of Balaam and the teaching of the Nicolaitans (Revelation 2:14–15). The message reveals the danger of tolerating false teaching when it enters the life of the church.

16. Thyatira

Thyatira is commended for love, service, faith, and patience, but Christ rebukes it because it allowed the woman whom he calls 'Jezebel' to teach and seduce the servants into sexual immorality and the eating of food offered to idols (Revelation 2:20). A church may be rich in works, but it cannot compensate for tolerance of moral or doctrinal corruption.

17. Sardis

Christ says to Sardis, 'You have the name that you are alive, and you are dead' (Revelation 3:1). This is one of the most dangerous images in the book; a community can possess a spiritual reputation before people while its true condition before Christ is entirely different. Yet Christ opens the door to repentance and commends the few who have not defiled their garments.

18. Philadelphia

Christ says, 'I have set before you an open door, and no one can shut it' (Revelation 3:8). This church was weak in human strength, yet it kept Christ's word and did not deny his name. Success in Christ's eyes is not determined by the magnitude of human power but by the church's faithfulness to what it has received from him.

19. Laodicea

The messages reach their climax in the warning against lukewarmness: 'Because you are lukewarm, and neither cold nor hot, I am about to spit you out of my mouth' (Revelation 3:16). Laodicea considered itself rich and self-sufficient, but Christ sees it as 'wretched, pitiable, poor, blind, and naked' (Revelation 3:17). The message reveals the danger of relying on wealth and status rather than on Christ.

20. God's Throne in Heaven

In chapter four the scene shifts from the churches to heaven: 'After this I looked, and behold, a door standing open in heaven' (Revelation 4:1). In the center is the throne. This transition is essential to understanding the book; earthly events may appear beyond control, yet the Revelation reveals that the throne stands and that God rules history.

21. Heavenly Worship

The living creatures and the twenty-four elders declare the holiness and glory of God, saying, 'Holy, holy, holy, the Lord God Almighty, who was and who is and who is to come' (Revelation 4:8). Here the throne and worship become the key to understanding the rest of the book.

22. The Sealed Scroll

John sees a scroll in the right hand of him who sits on the throne, sealed with seven seals, and no one is found able to open the scroll. John weeps, then hears, 'Behold, the Lion of the tribe of Judah, the root of David, has conquered, so that he can open the scroll and its seven seals' (Revelation 5:5). But the surprise is that he sees a Lamb standing as though it had been slain.

23. The Slain Lamb

The Lamb is one of the greatest symbols of the book; it is he who opens the scroll and leads history toward its goal. The twenty-four elders declare, 'For you were slain, and by your blood you ransomed people for God from every tribe and language and people and nation' (Revelation 5:9). Here redemption, kingship, and dominion are united; the one who rules history is the same one who laid down his blood to redeem his people.

24. The Seven Seals

The Lamb begins to open the seals, and a sequence of events, judgments, and disturbances appears. The famous four horses appear, then the martyrs, then cosmic upheavals. Yet the whole scene remains under the authority of the Lamb; evil is not an independent power that competes with God, but moves within the history that God rules.

25. The Martyrs

John sees the souls of those who had been killed for the word of God, and they cry out, 'How long, O Lord, holy and true, until you judge and avenge our blood on those who dwell on the earth?' (Revelation 6:10). The scene reveals that God does not forget the blood of the martyrs, and that historical injustice has a final divine answer.

26. The Fourteen Thousand and the Great Multitude

In chapter seven two complementary scenes appear: the sealed ones from the tribes of Israel, and then a great multitude that no one could number, 'from every nation and tribe and people and language' (Revelation 7:9). This reveals the universal scope of God's salvation, where multitudes from many nations stand before the throne and before the Lamb.

27. The Seven Trumpets

With chapter eight the trumpets begin, and a succession of judgments appears on the earth, the sea, the rivers, and the sky, followed by terrifying powers. Yet the book repeatedly reveals that people do not repent of their deeds. Here one of the painful themes of the Revelation appears: judgment exposes the hardness of the heart when humanity refuses repentance.

28. The Two Witnesses

The eleventh chapter presents the two witnesses who testify for God, then the beast kills them, yet God raises them up. The scene proclaims a principle that recurs throughout the book: testimony may lead to pain and death, but it does not end in defeat; resurrection is God’s answer to the persecution of His witnesses.

29. The Woman and the Dragon

John sees a great woman and a great dragon, and the dragon is 'the ancient serpent, who is called the Devil and Satan' (Revelation 12: 9). This chapter reveals the spiritual dimension of the conflict; the battle appearing on earth has a demonic dimension, but the dragon does not have the last word.

30. Michael and the Fall of the Dragon

A war breaks out in heaven, and the dragon is hurled to the earth. The heavenly voice declares: 'Now the salvation and the power and the kingdom of our God and the authority of His Messiah have come' (Revelation 12: 10). The victory is linked to the blood of the Lamb and the testimony of the believers, revealing that Christian victory does not rest on human power but on the work of Christ and the faithfulness of the witnesses.

31. The Beast

The beast rising from the sea appears in chapter thirteen, bearing political and spiritual authority opposed to God. The dragon gives it its power, throne, and authority. In the structure of the book, the beast represents the forces of human rule and authority when they become instruments of rebellion against God and persecution of His people.

32. The Second Beast

Another beast appears from the earth and works to deceive the inhabitants of the earth and compel them to worship the first beast. Here the book reveals that evil does not operate only by force, but also through deception, false religious propaganda, and the manufacture of an alternative worship.

33. The Number of the Beast 666

John says, 'Here is wisdom! Let the one who has understanding calculate the number of the beast, for it is the number of a man, and his number is six hundred and sixty-six' (Revelation 13: 18). The historical interpretations of the number have been many, and the most famous view links it to the numerical value of the name, especially in the context of the Roman Empire. But the text itself warns against dealing with the number superficially, for it says, 'Here is wisdom'; it is a symbol that requires understanding of its context.

34. The Lamb on Mount Zion

After the vision of the beast, the Lamb appears standing on Mount Zion with one hundred and forty-four thousand (Revelation 14: 1). The book thus presents a contrasting picture: in opposition to the mark of the beast and its worship, there is a people who bear the name of the Lamb and the name of His Father. The conflict is ultimately a struggle over identity, loyalty, and worship.

35. The Harvest of the Earth

In chapter fourteen, a symbolic harvest of the earth appears, followed by another harvest associated with judgment. The agricultural imagery declares that history is moving toward the moment of harvest and judgment, and that what humans sow of rebellion and evil does not remain without account.

36. The Seven Bowls

The seven bowls come in chapter sixteen as the filling up of God’s wrath in judgment. The strikes increase, but the text notes that people 'blasphemed the name of God' and did not repent. These scenes once again reveal the danger of a heart that meets God’s discipline with more blasphemy instead of repentance.

37. Babylon the Great

A woman appears called 'Babylon the Great, the mother of prostitutes and of the abominations of the earth' (Revelation 17: 5). In the book, Babylon is not merely a historical city, but a symbol of a global system that gathers corruption, wealth, power, idolatry, exploitation, and resistance to God. The choice of the name Babylon links it to ancient Babylon and to every human system that sets itself in opposition to the kingdom of God.

38. The Fall of Babylon

A mighty angel announces, 'Fallen, fallen is Babylon the Great' (Revelation 18: 2). The book reveals that empires that appear eternal are not so; the economic, political, and cultural power that opposes God will reach its end. Here the mourning of the kings of the earth and their merchants turns into a proclamation of judgment on the corrupt system.

39. The Marriage of the Lamb

After the fall of Babylon comes an opposing image: 'Let us rejoice and exult and give Him glory, for the marriage of the Lamb has come, and His bride has prepared herself' (Revelation 19: 7). Here the book moves from the fall of the adulterous city to the union of the Lamb with His bride, from the judgment of the corrupt system to the fulfillment of the relationship between Christ and His people.

40. Christ the Faithful and True Rider

John sees heaven opened and a white horse, and the One seated upon it is called 'Faithful and True,' and He judges and makes war in righteousness (Revelation 19: 11). Here Christ appears as King, Judge, and divine warrior who comes to complete judgment and secure the final victory.

41. The Word of God

John says about the Rider, 'His name is called the Word of God' (Revelation 19: 13). This carries a strong direct echo of the opening of John’s Gospel: 'In the beginning was the Word.' Thus Christ appears at the end of the Bible as He appeared at the beginning of John’s Gospel: the divine Word who declared God in history and now comes in glory and judgment.

42. The Defeat of the Beast and the False Prophet

The authority of the beast and the false prophet ends, and they are thrown into the lake of fire. This declares that every power that stood against God, no matter how great its authority seemed, has a final destiny of defeat.

43. The Defeat of Satan

Satan is thrown into the lake of fire, where eternal torment awaits (Revelation 20: 10). Thus the story of the spiritual conflict reaches its end; Satan is not an equal to God, and there is no struggle between two equal powers in the book. God is the absolute Lord, and evil has a definite end under God’s authority.

44. The Thousand Years

Chapter twenty speaks of Christ’s reign with His saints for a thousand years. Many interpretive schools have arisen around this passage, including the premillennial, postmillennial, and amillennial views, with significant differences over the nature of the thousand years and the order of events. Therefore, this matter should be presented as one of the disputed prophetic topics and not turned into a definitive biblical fact by one interpretation.

45. The Resurrection and Judgment before the Great White Throne

John sees 'a great white throne,' judges the dead according to their works, and throws death and Hades into the lake of fire (Revelation 20: 11–15). Here the issue of judgment reaches its climax; no human being, power, or empire can escape the final reckoning before God.

46. The New Heaven and the New Earth

John says, 'Then I saw a new heaven and a new earth, for the first heaven and the first earth had passed away, and the sea was no more' (Revelation 21: 1). Here history does not end in emptiness, but in the beginning of a new creation. The goal of salvation is not merely escape of souls from the earth, but the establishment of a new creation in which God dwells with His people.

47. The New Jerusalem

John sees 'the holy city, Jerusalem, coming down out of heaven from God, prepared as a bride adorned for her husband' (Revelation 21: 2). Here the images of the city, temple, and bride meet; the city is the place of God’s presence, the bride is His people, and the temple in the city is not a separate structure, because the presence of God itself fills it.

48. God Dwelling with Humanity

The story of Scripture reaches a great declaration: 'Behold, the dwelling place of God is with humanity, and He will dwell with them, and they shall be His people, and God Himself will be with them as their God' (Revelation 21: 3). Here we find the culmination of God’s purpose from the book of Genesis: God wants to dwell with humanity. The story begins in Eden and ends in a city, but in both cases it revolves around the presence of God among His people.

49. No More Death or Mourning

He says, 'And God will wipe away every tear from their eyes, and death shall be no more, and neither shall there be mourning nor crying nor pain any more, for the former things have passed away' (Revelation 21: 4). Here the book presents the final end of all that entered the world through the fall: death, sorrow, and pain.

50. The River of the Water of Life

In chapter twenty-two, John sees 'a pure river of water of life, bright as crystal, flowing from the throne of God and of the Lamb' (Revelation 22: 1). This brings us back to the images of water and life in Scripture, from Eden to the rivers of Ezekiel, and culminates in its final form: life flowing from the throne of God and the Lamb.

51. The Tree of Life

He says, 'And in the middle of its street, and on either side of the river, the tree of life producing twelve kinds of fruit' (Revelation 22: 2). This is one of the greatest links between Genesis and Revelation; in Genesis the tree of life appears in Eden, and in Revelation it appears in the new creation. Thus the history of Scripture ends with the restoration of what humanity lost through the fall.

52. No Curse Anymore

John says, 'And there shall no longer be any curse' (Revelation 22: 3). This phrase links the end to the beginning of the story of the fall in Genesis; the curse on the earth and all that was tied to the results of sin reaches its end in the new creation.

53. The Face of God

The declaration reaches its climax when it says, 'They shall see His face, and His name shall be on their foreheads' (Revelation 22: 4). This is the deepest goal of salvation: not merely the removal of pain or the attainment of life, but seeing God and living in His presence.

54. 'Behold, I Am Coming Soon'

Christ repeats at the end of the book, 'Behold, I am coming soon' (Revelation 22: 7, 12, 20). This statement reveals that Revelation is not merely a description of the past or a distant future, but a call to watchfulness, readiness, and faithfulness. The Christian lives in the present under the light of the coming of Christ.

55. 'I Am the Alpha and the Omega'

Christ concludes the revelation by saying, 'I am the Alpha and the Omega, the beginning and the end, the first and the last' (Revelation 22: 13). Thus the book returns to the theme of the beginning and the end that appeared in its opening, declaring that all history falls under the authority of Christ.

56. The Closing Prayer

The book ends with the prayer of the church: 'Amen. Come, Lord Jesus!' (Revelation 22: 20). After all the visions, judgments, beasts, trumpets, and seals, the final cry of the church is not a request to know the future, but a plea for the coming of Christ.

57. Links to the Old Testament

The book of Revelation is one of the New Testament books most saturated with images and symbols drawn from the Old Testament, especially Daniel, Ezekiel, Isaiah, Zechariah, Exodus, Psalm 2, and Psalm 110. The beasts, horns, throne, lampstands, temple, plagues, Babylon, the new Jerusalem, the river, and the tree of life all enter into a vast network of biblical symbolism. Revelation cannot be understood correctly if it is isolated from this background.

58. The Relationship to Daniel

There is a special relationship between Revelation and Daniel; the image of the Son of Man, the beasts, the horns, the kingdom, judgment, the books, and the times all find a clear echo in Daniel. Yet Revelation rereads these images in light of the death and resurrection of Christ; the One who triumphs in the end is the slaughtered Lamb.

59. The Relationship to Genesis

The Bible begins with Eden, the tree of life, the river, and the divine presence, then enters death, curse, and sin into creation. Revelation ends with the new creation, the river of the water of life, the tree of life, and the removal of the curse and death. Thus there is a major theological movement from Eden to the New Jerusalem.

60. The Relationship to Exodus and the Passover

The language of plagues, exodus, blood, deliverance, singing, and victory appears in Revelation, making the story of Exodus an important background. Most importantly, Christ appears as the Lamb, extending the concept of the Passover from the sacrificial exodus to the redemption of Christ and then to the final victory of the Lamb.

61. The Lamb and the Kingdom

The central paradox of the book is that the greatest victor appears first as a slain Lamb. The kingdom of Christ does not operate by the logic of imperial power, but by the cross, redemption, and resurrection. For this reason, Revelation turns the world’s measures upside down: the One who seems weak in the world’s eyes is the true possessor of authority, and the witnesses killed by the beast are ultimately the conquerors.

62. The Church and Testimony

John describes himself as a fellow partaker in the tribulation, and the book repeatedly speaks of testimony. The church is not called to dominate the world by force, but to be faithful to Christ even to death. From here, the witnesses in Revelation are not defeated victims, but conquerors because they stood firm in the testimony of the Lamb.

63. Worship Is the Center of the Book

Worship appears in almost every stage of Revelation: worship of God, worship of the Lamb, and worship of the beast as false worship. Therefore, the book can be understood as a struggle over who is worthy of worship. Heaven declares that God and the Lamb alone are worthy of glory, while the dragon and the beast seek to turn humanity toward worship of creation rather than the Creator.

64. Numbers and Symbols

Revelation uses many symbolic numbers, such as seven, twelve, a thousand, 144000, 666, as well as geometric, cosmic, and animal imagery. One should not deal with every number in a purely literal, arithmetic way; some numbers carry clear symbolic value within apocalyptic literature. The key principle is to interpret the symbol in light of the biblical context and the book itself, rather than building a complete doctrine on a single numerical calculation.

65. Are All Symbols Literal?

The book clearly uses symbolic visionary language, but this does not mean that everything is symbolic or that everything is literal. There are real historical churches, a real Christ, and real resurrection and judgment, yet these realities are sometimes presented through visions, images, and symbols. Therefore, interpreting the book requires distinguishing between the theological truth announced by the symbol and the symbolic form in which it is presented.

66. The Message of the Book for the Suffering Believer

Revelation says to the believer who sees evil triumphing: do not be deceived by the temporary scene. The throne is not for the beast, authority is not for the dragon, Babylon will fall, the witnesses will rise, Christ will come, evil will be judged, and God will dwell with His people. Therefore, the book is, in essence, a book of hope for the oppressed as much as it is a book of judgment for the wicked.

67. The Great Movement of the Book of Revelation

The book moves from Christ in the midst of the churches to the throne of God in heaven, from the throne to the Lamb who opened the scroll, from the seals, trumpets, and bowls to the appearance of the beast, the dragon, and Babylon, then to the fall of Babylon and the defeat of the beast and Satan, then the resurrection and judgment, and finally to the new heaven, the new earth, and the new Jerusalem. Thus the whole book moves from the suffering church in history to the glorified church in the presence of God, from the cross to the throne, from death to life, and from the old world to the new creation.

68. The Encyclopedic Summary

The Book of Revelation is the unveiling of Jesus Christ and His final victory over evil and the fulfillment of God's purpose in history. The book begins with Christ standing and glorified in the midst of the churches; He examines their lives and calls them to repentance, perseverance, and faithfulness. Then John lifts his gaze to heaven, where the throne is set, and where the slain Lamb is revealed as the only One worthy to open the scroll. From there begins a series of seals, trumpets, bowls, and conflicts that reveal that earthly history is not separate from God's judgment, and that the powers of evil—persecution, deception, economic and spiritual corruption, and political corruption—will all reach their end. The dragon appears as Satan, the beast as the hostile power against Christ, and Babylon as the image of the corrupt world system, but all these powers remain temporary before the authority of God and the Lamb. Then Christ comes in glory, defeats the beast, the false prophet, and Satan, and the resurrection and judgment come, and death and Hades end. After that, John sees the new heaven and the new earth and the new Jerusalem, where God dwells with humanity, and there will be no more death, sorrow, crying, or pain; the river of the water of life flows from the throne of God and the Lamb, the tree of life grows, there will be no curse afterward, and the servants of God see His face. Thus the book ends as every biblical revelation should end: Christ is the First and the Last, the Lamb is the victor, the church is called to faithfulness, evil has an end, and God dwells with His people forever.
