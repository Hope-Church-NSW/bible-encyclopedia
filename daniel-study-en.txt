Introduction to the Book of Daniel

The central idea of the book: The Book of Daniel is a book of God's sovereignty over kingdoms, human faithfulness in the midst of a pagan world, the sanctity of life in captivity, the revealing of mysteries of the future, and the triumph of God's kingdom over all earthly kingdoms. The book uniquely combines historical narratives in chapters 1–6 and prophetic visions in chapters 7–12, and therefore does not separate between faith and daily life on the one hand, and between history and prophecy on the other. Daniel and his companions live in the heart of the Babylonian Empire, but they refuse to allow the political and cultural environment surrounding them to reshape their identity, and at the same time the book declares that kingdoms that appear eternal in human sight are not eternal before God, because 'the Most High is sovereign over the kingdoms of men.' From here the book moves from captivity to faithfulness, from trial to revelation, from kingdoms of men to the kingdom of God, and from earthly history to eternal hope.

1. Name of the book and its meaning

The name of the book is Daniel, and the Hebrew name is דָּנִיֵּאל—Dāniyyēl, and it consists of the element dān—דִּין connected to judgment or ruling, and the element ēl—אֵל which means God. Therefore the name carries a meaning close to 'God the judge' or 'God is the judge.' And this meaning fits deeply with the content of the book, because Daniel lives before earthly kings who possess power and strength, but the events reveal that the final judgment is not in the hand of Nebuchadnezzar or Darius or any other king, but in the hand of God who raises up kings and removes them and determines the times of kingdoms and their endings.

2. Who is Daniel?

Daniel was a young Jewish man who was taken to Babylon among the captives in the days of Nebuchadnezzar, and was chosen along with a group of young men to study in the king's palace. It appears from the book that he was endowed with intelligence and wisdom and ability to understand languages and sciences, but he did not allow these abilities to separate him from his faith in God. His character is distinguished by the fact that his faithfulness was not connected to his presence in Jerusalem or to the presence of a believing community around him, but he was able to maintain his relationship with God while living in the heart of a pagan global system.

3. The year of Daniel's birth and death

The Holy Bible does not provide the date of Daniel's birth or the date of his death, and therefore they cannot be determined precisely. But he was a young man when he was taken to Babylon, and his professional and prophetic life continued during the reign of several kings, from Nebuchadnezzar to Belshazzar then Darius, and perhaps his service extended to the beginning of the reign of Cyrus the Persian. This means that Daniel lived through a long period of major political transformations, and witnessed the transfer of power from Babylon to Media and Persia, and yet remained steadfast in his testimony to God.

4. The time of the book

The historical framework of the book is related to the sixth century before Christ, beginning from the captivity of Judah to Babylon and continuing through the transformation from the Babylonian Empire to the Median-Persian Empire. The narrative places the beginning of events in the third year of the reign of Jehoiakim, while some visions extend to later years of Daniel's life. As for the question of the date of the final composition of the book, scholars have discussed it at length, the Jewish and Christian tradition attributes the book to Daniel himself in the sixth century before Christ, while some critical scholars see a later date for the formulation, especially because of the nature of the prophecies related to kingdoms. Therefore it is important in academic study to distinguish between the history that the book presents of events and visions and between the critical debate over the date of its final composition.

5. For whom was the book written?

The book addresses God's people who live in a hostile or oppressive political and cultural environment, and therefore carries a direct message to the believer who finds himself far from his homeland or surrounded by a culture that does not share his faith. It also addresses every generation living under the authority of human empires and needs to remember that the power of the state is not the ultimate power in history. From here Daniel can be read as a book that presents to the people a fundamental question: Can you be faithful to God when circumstances change around you?

6. Reason for writing the book

The first reason is to present a practical model of faithfulness to God in the midst of captivity and cultural pressure, but the book goes beyond that to declare that God is the Lord of history. He is the one who gave Judah into the hand of Nebuchadnezzar, he is the one who revealed the king's dream, he is the one who rescued the young men from the fire, he is the one who shut the lions' mouths, he is the one who announced the fall of Babylon, and he is the one who revealed to Daniel the succession of coming kingdoms. Therefore the book does not only want to say to the believer: be faithful, but adds to it a greater truth: be faithful because God is the owner of history, and the kingdoms that frighten you today are only a temporary phase in his story.

7. The general division of the book

The book is essentially divided into two major sections. Chapters 1–6 present historical narratives about Daniel and his companions in the royal court, and include the food, the golden image, the fiery furnace, the dream of the tree, the writing on the wall, and the lions' den. Meanwhile chapters 7–12 present prophetic visions related to successive kingdoms and historical conflict and persecution and judgment and hope and resurrection. The general structure can be summarized thus: faith in history, then the interpretation of history in the light of God's revelation.

8. Daniel 1—Faithfulness in food

Daniel's story begins in a situation that appears small but reveals a great principle, as Daniel decided not to defile himself with the royal food and wine. The problem here is not merely a difference in diet, but a matter of identity and loyalty and holiness, because Daniel found himself within an integrated program to reshape the identity of young Jewish men in Babylon. Therefore Daniel's first victory was not over a lion or a king, but an internal victory: to remain with God while living in a culture that wants to redefine him.

9. The changing of names of Daniel and his companions

New Babylonian names were given to Daniel and his companions, and this step has clear cultural significance, because the name in the ancient world was connected to identity and belonging. The empire wanted to reshape these young men through language and education and names and food, but the changing of the name did not change the reality of their belonging to God. And here the book presents a very important principle: the environment can change what surrounds a person, but it cannot change his internal identity unless he allows it to.

10. 'But Daniel resolved'

This phrase is one of the most important keys to the book:

'But Daniel resolved that he would not defile himself with the king's food, or with the wine that he drank.'

Daniel did not wait until the crisis began to decide his stance, but made the decision beforehand. This reveals that faithfulness in a moment of great trial is often built from previous small decisions, because when a person determines his loyalty before pressure comes, he becomes more able to stand firm when the pressure arrives. Therefore the Book of Daniel begins from a decision of the heart before it begins from a miracle of God.

11. Daniel 2—The dream of the image

Nebuchadnezzar dreams of a great image whose head was of gold, and its chest and arms of silver, and its belly and thighs of bronze, and its legs of iron, and its feet of iron and clay. Then stones come and cut out without hands and strike the image, and crush the kingdoms that it represents, and the stone becomes a great mountain that fills the earth. This vision presents one of the greatest biblical images of the weakness of earthly kingdoms before God's kingdom.

12. The interpretation of the image

Daniel declares that the gold represents the kingdom of Nebuchadnezzar, and then other kingdoms come after it, and the vision ends with the stone which symbolizes a kingdom established by the God of heaven. Scholars have disagreed in determining the details of the subsequent kingdoms, especially in some different historical readings, but the basic axis of the text is firm: history is not a random series of empires, but falls under the authority of God, and in the end God's kingdom triumphs over the kingdoms of men.

13. The stone that became a mountain

The stone that is cut out without hands is one of the most theologically important images in the book, because it does not come as a result of human military power or a political revolution led by a man. It is a divine work that strikes the system that appears solid before men, then transforms into a mountain that fills the earth. In the Christian reading this text became an important part of the study of God's kingdom and Christ, because the New Testament declares that God's kingdom entered history decisively through Christ, and will reach its fullness in the end.

14. Daniel 3—The fiery furnace

Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego refuse to bow down to the golden image that Nebuchadnezzar set up, so the king issues an order to throw them into the fire. This story presents a powerful image of the conflict between worship of the state and worship of God, because what was asked of them was not merely to respect the king but to offer religious worship to an image made by man. And here the question becomes: does a person preserve his life at any cost, or does he remain faithful to God even when the cost is death?

15. 'Our God whom we serve is able to deliver us'

The three reply to the king:

'Behold, our God whom we serve is able to deliver us from the burning fiery furnace, and he will deliver us out of your hand, O king.'

But they add a greater meaning when they declare that they will not bow down even if God does not deliver them. This is one of the deepest images of faith in the book, because their faith does not rest on a bargain with God, but on God's faithfulness even when they do not understand how he works. Therefore biblical faith does not only say: 'God will deliver me,' but can also say: 'And if he does not, I will not forsake him.'

16. The fourth one like a son of the gods

Nebuchadnezzar sees inside the furnace four men instead of three, and describes the fourth in an exceptional way. Interpreters have differed in identifying this fourth figure: some have seen him as an angelic appearance, and others have seen in him a divine appearance or a later messianic reading. Therefore one should not be dogmatic about what the text does not explicitly state, but the core message is clear: God did not necessarily prevent the trial, but he entered with his servants into the midst of the trial and did not leave them alone.

17. Daniel 4 — Pride and the Fall

Nebuchadnezzar dreams of a great tree that extends to the sky and from which everyone eats, then the command comes to cut it down. Daniel interprets the dream as a warning to the king about his pride, and then the word is fulfilled when Nebuchadnezzar speaks boastfully of his greatness and his kingdom. Here one of the most important principles of the book appears: whoever raises himself above the bounds of humanity discovers that his authority is limited before God.

18. "That the living may know that the Most High rules"

This phrase sums up the political theology of the book:

"That the living may know that the Most High rules in the kingdom of men, and gives it to whomever he wills, and sets over it whomever he wills."

God does not compete with kings on an equal political level; rather, he is the possessor of the supreme authority who permits kingdoms to rise and fall. Therefore, the book of Daniel presents an entirely different perspective on history: what appears in earthly news as a struggle between kings and empires appears in heaven under the sovereignty of the true King of kings.

19. Daniel 5 — The Writing on the Wall

At Belshazzar's feast a hand appears and writes on the wall:

"MENE, MENE, TEKEL, and PARSIN."

Daniel declares that the days of the kingdom have been numbered, that the king has been weighed in the balances and found wanting, and that his kingdom will be given to the Medes and Persians. That very night Belshazzar falls, and thus the writing on the wall becomes a powerful symbol of the truth that political power cannot postpone the hour of judgment that God has determined.

20. The Fall of Babylon

The fall of Babylon in the book is not merely a military event; it carries a clear theological meaning, for Babylon, which seemed so very powerful, was not outside God's authority. This links Daniel to the broader biblical message that sees Babylon as a symbol of human pride and of the system that resists God. Therefore, the story of its fall becomes a testimony that every empire, however great its power, has an end when its time comes before God's authority.

21. Daniel 6 — The Lions' Den

In the reign of Darius, Daniel refuses to stop praying despite the issuance of a decree forbidding it. He continues to pray three times a day as was his custom, and so he is thrown into the lions' den. But God sends his angel and shuts the lions' mouths, and Daniel comes out unharmed. This story confirms that Daniel's faithfulness was not a temporary stance that appeared before Nebuchadnezzar, but a way of life that continued through the changing of kings and empires.

22. Why did Daniel pray despite the decree?

Daniel was not trying to create a personal act of heroism; rather, he was maintaining a steady spiritual practice that preceded the crisis. Therefore, Daniel's strength in chapter six did not begin when he entered the lions' den, but began in the room where he prayed daily. This teaches that the secret life with God is what produces public steadfastness before the world.

23. Daniel 7 — The Four Beasts

The book moves from narratives to visions, and Daniel sees four beasts rising from the sea. The vision represents human kingdoms that appear in a beastly form, in contrast to the Son of Man who comes to the Ancient of Days and is given authority, glory, and a kingdom. This is among the most important visions in the whole book, because it moves from the image of the beasts representing earthly kingdoms to the image of the man who is associated with the eternal kingdom.

24. "One like a son of man"

The text says:

"And behold, with the clouds of heaven one like a son of man was coming, and he came to the Ancient of Days, and they brought him near before him. And to him was given authority, glory, and a kingdom, that all peoples, nations, and languages should serve him. His authority is an everlasting authority which shall not pass away, and his kingdom is one that shall not be destroyed."

This text has become one of the most important messianic texts in the Bible, because Christ repeatedly used the expression "Son of Man" of himself in the Gospels. Therefore, Daniel 7 forms an important bridge between the expectation of the kingdom in the Old Testament and Christ's proclamation of his kingdom in the New Testament.

25. The Difference Between the Beasts and the Son of Man

The beasts represent political powers that possess strength but appear in animal form, while the kingdom associated with the Son of Man comes in human form. The contrast is most likely intentional: human kingdoms, when separated from God, turn into a predatory power, while the kingdom that comes from God appears in the form of the man who receives the everlasting authority. Here the book presents a profound theological critique of authority when it turns into predation and tyranny.

26. Daniel 8 — The Ram and the Goat

Daniel sees a ram with two horns and a goat with a great horn, and the vision presents details connected to specific kingdoms and historical events. The traditional interpretation of the vision connects it with the Medo-Persian and Greek empires, and then with the conflicts that came in the Hellenistic era. This vision reveals that God foreknows the course of great events, and that history, which seems to humans full of surprises, falls within the scope of God's knowledge and sovereignty.

27. Daniel 9 — The Prayer of Confession

Among the greatest prayers of the book is Daniel's prayer in chapter nine, where the prophet does not stand before God merely accusing his people, but enters with them into confession and says:

"We have sinned and committed iniquity and done wickedly and rebelled, even turning aside from your commandments and from your judgments."

This is very important, because Daniel himself was among the figures against whom the book records no clear sin, and yet he does not stand outside the sins of his nation. He prays as one who is part of a people who need mercy, and this is a magnificent picture of intercession and confession of sin before God.

28. The Seventy Weeks

After Daniel's prayer comes the prophecy concerning the seventy weeks, which is among the most discussed prophetic texts in biblical studies. Interpreters have differed on how to calculate them and determine their points of beginning and ending and the details of their connection to Christ, Jerusalem, and the final events. But the center of the text is that God has set a divine framework for history, and that sin, judgment, restoration, Christ, and the final events all fall within God's timeline.

29. Daniel 10–12 — The Spiritual and Historical Struggle

In the final chapters, the book enters a more complex level of visions, where a struggle connected with kings and nations appears, and the spiritual dimension behind political events also appears. This reveals that earthly history is not the whole story, and that behind visible struggles lie spiritual dimensions that man does not see directly. Nevertheless, the book does not give man a call to obsess over hidden details, but always returns him to steadfastness, faithfulness, and awaiting God's end.

30. The Resurrection in the Book of Daniel

The book contains one of the clearest Old Testament texts about the resurrection:

"And many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt."

Here the book moves from history to eternity, and from the question of kingdoms to the question of man's ultimate destiny. For even if empires prevail for a time, and even if believers die under persecution, the end is not death, for God will raise the dead and declare judgment and eternal life.

31. "Those who turn many to righteousness"

The book concludes with a wonderful picture of the faithful believers:

"And those who are wise shall shine like the brightness of the firmament, and those who turn many to righteousness, like the stars forever and ever."

This image reveals that the value of service is not measured only by what the eye sees in the present, for Daniel lived in a time when evil seemed stronger than good, and yet the book declares that the faithfulness of those who serve God will have eternal glory. Therefore, Daniel's message is not only: do not bow down to the idol, but also: continue to turn others to righteousness even if you do not see the full result now.

32. The Main Characters in the Book
Daniel

The prophet and sage who remained faithful to God through the exile and the changing of empires, and through whose life God used to reveal his sovereignty over history.

Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah

Daniel's three companions who refused to bow down to Nebuchadnezzar's statue, and entered the furnace trusting in God.

Nebuchadnezzar

The great king of Babylon, whom the book reveals that however great his power, it remains subject to God's authority.

Belshazzar

The king in whose reign, at his feast, the writing on the wall appeared, and in whose days Babylon fell.

Darius

The king whose reign was linked to the story of the lions' den and Daniel's deliverance.

Cyrus

The Persian king who appears in the final time-frame of the book, and represents the transition to a new stage of imperial history.

The Angel and the Heavenly Visions

Angelic beings appear in some scenes of the book as servants in the declaration of divine messages, without worship being directed to them.

33. The Most Important Events in the Book

The exile of Daniel and his companions to Babylon, Daniel's refusal to be defiled by the king's delicacies, the young men's success in wisdom, the interpretation of the dream of the statue, the three men's refusal to bow down to the golden statue, their deliverance from the furnace, the dream of the tree and Nebuchadnezzar's fall because of pride, the writing on the wall and the fall of Babylon, Daniel's being thrown into the lions' den and his deliverance, the visions of the four beasts, the vision of the Son of Man, the vision of the ram and the goat, Daniel's prayer for the people, the prophecy of the seventy weeks, the final visions, and the declaration of resurrection and eternal life.

34. The Most Important Message the Book Presents

God is the true Lord of history, and earthly kingdoms, however great their power, are not eternal; therefore the believer can live faithfully to God within a society, culture, and politics that do not share his faith. For true success is not that a person leave the world, but that he remain God's while living in it, and that he trust that God's kingdom will in the end overcome all human kingdoms, and that those who stand firm in righteousness will shine "like the stars forever and ever."

35. What Does the Book of Daniel Point Us To?

The book points first to the fact that spiritual identity must be stronger than the pressure of culture, for Daniel lived in Babylon without Babylon becoming the final point of reference for his life. It points second to the fact that political authority is limited, for Nebuchadnezzar, Belshazzar, Darius, and all the kingdoms appear under God's authority. It points third to the fact that prayer is not a marginal habit, but a source of steadfastness in the believer's life, as clearly appears in Daniel's life. It points finally to the fact that history is moving toward an eternal kingdom, eternal life, and a final judgment, and therefore the believer's faithfulness today has a value that transcends the bounds of history.

36. Daniel and Christ

The book of Daniel occupies a central place in the study of Christ in the Old Testament, especially in the vision of the Son of Man in chapter seven. Christ repeatedly used this title of himself, and linked his appearing with judgment, the kingdom, and authority. Therefore, Daniel 7 is not merely a text about a vague future figure, but is one of the most important biblical backgrounds for understanding Christ's declaration of himself as the Son of Man, possessor of authority and the everlasting kingdom.

37. Daniel and the Kingdom of God

The kingdom is one of the most important threads that link the parts of the book together: in the dream of the statue we see a stone that the God of heaven sets up and that becomes a kingdom that shall not be destroyed, and in the vision of the Son of Man we see an everlasting authority and a kingdom that shall not pass away. Thus the book declares that history does not end with the victory of the strongest empire, but with the victory of God's kingdom. This makes Daniel one of the most important books for understanding the relationship between the present kingdom and the coming kingdom.

38. Daniel and the Resurrection

The book holds a special place in the study of the doctrine of the resurrection in the Old Testament, for Daniel 12 clearly declares the awakening of those who sleep to eternal life or to eternal shame. This makes clear that man's destiny does not end at death, and that God will raise the dead and carry out judgment. In light of the New Testament, this text becomes an important part of the background that illuminates the declaration of Christ and the apostles concerning the resurrection, eternal life, and the final judgment.

39. Daniel and Faithfulness in Exile

Perhaps the most important practical message in the book is that place does not determine a person's faithfulness to God. Daniel could have said that the exile excused him from responsibility, but he did not, and he did not say that the pagan environment forced him to compromise. Rather, he lived in Babylon, learned the language and culture of the court, and served in the administration, but he did not allow Babylon to possess his heart. Here the book presents a highly important model for the believer who lives within an intellectually and culturally diverse society: participation in society does not mean dissolving into it.

40. The Great Movement of the Book of Daniel

The book can be summarized in this path:

The exile.

Then:

The test of identity.

Then:

Honesty.

Then:

Wisdom.

Then:

The disclosure of the kings' secrets.

Then:

Deliverance from the fire.

Then:

The humbling of pride.

Then:

The fall of Babylon.

Then:

Deliverance from the lions.

Then:

Visions of the kingdoms.

Then:

The appearance of the Son of Man.

Then:

Prayer and confession.

Then:

Revelation of the future.

Then:

The resurrection.

Then:

Eternal life.

Thus the book moves from the land of captivity to the Kingdom of God, from the palace of the king to the throne of God, from the power of the beasts to the authority of the Son of Man, and from death to eternal life.

📚 Primary References for Study

Core biblical texts: the Book of Daniel 1–12, together with a study of 2 Kings 24–25, 2 Chronicles 36, and Jeremiah 25, 29, and 52 to understand the Babylonian captivity, then Ezra 1 to understand the beginning of the return in the reign of Cyrus. For Messianic study, a special connection should be made between Daniel 7:13–14 and Matthew 24–26, Mark 13–14, and Luke 21, and the study of Daniel 12 together with John 5, Acts 24, 1 Corinthians 15, and Revelation 20–22 is also helpful in tracing the theme of the resurrection, the judgment, and the kingdom.

🔑 The sentence that summarizes the Book of Daniel

"The Book of Daniel places the believer within the heart of empires to teach him that Babylon may change its name but cannot change its identity, that the king may change the laws but cannot change the authority of God, and that the fire may blaze and the lions may surround him, yet the faithfulness of God does not change; at the same time, the book lifts the reader's eyes above kingdoms that seem great, so that he sees the Son of Man coming on the clouds of heaven, given authority and glory and an eternal kingdom, then hears at the end of the book the promise of resurrection and eternal life, and discovers that all of history moves from the changing kingdoms of men to the Kingdom of God that never passes away, and that those who stood firm in faith and righteousness will not lose their faithfulness, but rather 'shall shine like the stars forever and ever.'"
