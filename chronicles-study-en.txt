1 and 2 Chronicles

Methodological Note: First Chronicles and Second Chronicles were originally a single work, then divided into two books. In the Hebrew Bible the work is known as דִּבְרֵי הַיָּמִים — Divrê Hayyāmîm, that is, «matters of the days» or «events of the days,» while it carried in the Greek translation the name Paraleipomena, that is, «omitted/overlooked matters,» because the translators saw in it additional material to the history of Samuel and Kings. But this description does not give the book its due; it is not merely a repetition of what came before, but a theological rereading of the history of Israel after the exile, with the aim of restoring identity, worship, and hope in God's covenant with David.

6
First: The Book of First Chronicles
1. The Name of the Book and Its Meaning

The Hebrew name דִּבְרֵי הַיָּמִים — Divrê Hayyāmîm literally means «matters of the days» or «events of the days,» and it is an expression close in meaning to historical records or royal annals and events. As for the name that has come to us in modern languages, «Chronicles,» it is connected to the title by which the book became known in the Latin tradition.

This name is important because the book does not just want to tell us what happened, but wants to ask: What does what happened mean before God?

The writer goes back to the past, but he addresses a people living after the catastrophe of exile, and therefore the study of history becomes a means of rebuilding faith, identity, and hope.

2. The Author

The book does not explicitly mention the name of the author.

Scholars generally call the author «the Chronicler» or «the writer of Chronicles» — The Chronicler, and it is understood from the nature of the work that he was a writer with extensive knowledge of genealogies, the temple, the Levites, systems of worship, and historical records. Some studies suggest that he was a priest or Levite with scriptural training and a connection to the service of the temple in the Persian period.

As for the ancient Jewish tradition, it attributed the book to Ezra the Scribe, and there are literary and historical reasons that led some scholars to support this view, especially the similarity between the end of Second Chronicles and the beginning of the Book of Ezra. But from a scientific standpoint we cannot say that Ezra is the author with certainty, because the text itself does not explicitly state this.

3. The Time of Writing of the Book

The date of writing is clearly connected to the period following the Babylonian exile.

The book ends with the decree of Cyrus king of Persia in the year 538 B.C., which allowed the Jews to return to Jerusalem and rebuild the house of the Lord, and the genealogy of David in 1 Chronicles 3 extends to generations after Zerubbabel. For this reason many scholars place the final composition in the second half of the fifth century before the common era, approximately 450–400 B.C., with variations in determining the precise date.

This period is very important for understanding the book.

The original reader is not an Israelite living in the days of David, but a person who has returned from exile and sees Jerusalem partly rebuilt, and asks:

Who are we? And do God's promises to us still stand? And do we still have a place in the story of the covenant?

4. For Whom Was the Book Written?

It was written essentially for the Jewish community that returned from exile and settled in the land of Judah around Jerusalem.

And this community faced a deep crisis of identity; the ancient kingdom had ended, the Davidic throne was no longer standing as it was, the temple built by Solomon was destroyed and then rebuilt, and the people came to live under Persian rule.

Therefore the book presents to them a powerful answer:

You are not a people without history, and you are not a people without promise, and you are not a people whose story ended with exile.

5. The Reason for Writing First Chronicles

There are several interconnected purposes.

First is the reestablishment of Israel's identity through genealogy.

Second is the affirmation of the continuation of God's covenant with David.

Third is the recentering of the temple, worship, priesthood, and the Levites.

Fourth is to give the people returning from exile hope for the future.

The writer does not narrate the past merely for nostalgia, but wants to say:

The God who preserved the lineage through the centuries is able to continue to fulfill His promise.

6. Why Does the Book Begin with Genealogies?

The genealogies may at first seem like the most difficult part to read.

But they are actually among the most important keys to the book.

The writer begins from:

Adam → the generations → Abraham → Israel → Judah → David.

And thus places Israel within a larger story that begins from creation itself.

And the message is clear:

The exile did not erase the people's identity, and it did not cut off God's work in history.

Rather, the genealogies connect the generation returning from exile with the generations that preceded it.

7. The Division of First Chronicles

The book can be divided into four main sections:

First Chronicles 1–9

Genealogies of Israel from Adam to the generation returning from exile.

First Chronicles 10

The death of Saul.

First Chronicles 11–21

David and the establishment of the kingdom.

First Chronicles 22–29

David and the preparation for building the temple and organizing worship.

Here we notice something very important:

The book progresses from:

Identity → David → the temple.

And this is not coincidence, but rather the fundamental theological structure of the book.

8. Saul in Chronicles

Unlike First Samuel, First Chronicles does not give much space to Saul's life.

It begins approximately at the end.

In 1 Chronicles 10 we see the fall of Saul and his death.

The writer does not care much about recounting the details of his life, because his main aim is to move on to David.

Then he explains Saul's death as the result of his unfaithfulness to the Lord and his disobedience to the word of the Lord.

And this prepares the way for the king who will come after him.

9. David

Here begins the largest section of the book.

And David appears in a very positive light.

Not because the writer completely ignores the existence of his sin, but because his main purpose is to highlight David the king connected to God's covenant and the temple and worship.

This is one of the important differences between Chronicles and Samuel and Kings.

The writer selects events that serve his theological message.

10. The Unity of Israel Around David

The book presents long lists of mighty men who came to David.

And David becomes the center of national unity.

No longer the question:

Who follows Saul?

But rather:

Who gathers around the king whom God has chosen?

And from here appears a very important concept in Chronicles:

The people united around the king of God and around the worship of God.

11. Jerusalem

David conquers Jerusalem.

And makes it the city of David.

And this event is not merely political.

Because Jerusalem will become the center of:

The kingdom.

The temple.

Worship.

The Davidic hope.

And thus the city becomes the meeting point between political promise, worship, and divine presence.

12. The Ark of the Covenant

David attempts to transfer the Ark of the Covenant to Jerusalem.

And the tragedy of Uzzah occurs.

Then David learns that the transfer of the Ark must be done according to the order prescribed by God.

And here an important principle emerges:

Enthusiasm alone is insufficient in worship; worship requires holiness and obedience.

And this is a recurring theme in Chronicles.

13. David and Worship

One of the matters most emphasized by the book is that David was not merely a military leader.

He was concerned with organizing:

The priests.

The Levites.

The singers.

The gatekeepers.

Temple service.

The sacrifices.

And this means that David understood that the kingdom could not be politically established apart from worship.

14. God's Covenant with David

In 1 Chronicles 17, the book presents again God's covenant with David.

And God promises him that his descendants will continue and that God will establish his throne.

And this is the heart of Davidic theology in Chronicles.

For the kingship is not merely a political office.

It is connected to a divine promise.

And the temple is also connected to this promise.

And therefore become:

The Davidic Covenant + The Kingdom + The Temple

Three intertwined elements in the book's vision.

15. Why Did David Not Build the Temple?

David desires to build a house for the Lord.

But God declares to him that his son is the one who will build it.

Nevertheless, David does not stop.

Rather, he begins to prepare:

The gold.

The silver.

Bronze.

Wood.

Stones.

And he also prepares the system of service.

And here a profound leadership lesson appears:

God may not permit you to see the project you dreamed of completed, but he may permit you to lay its foundation for future generations.

16. Solomon in First Chronicles

The book concludes with David handing over the task to Solomon.

And Solomon here does not appear merely as the son of David.

But as the son who will build the temple.

And thus First Chronicles concludes while looking toward the future.

David prepares.

And Solomon will build.

And the people will worship.

And the covenant will continue.

🔵 The Most Important Message in First Chronicles

The exile did not erase the identity of God's people, nor did it annul God's covenant with David; hope returns through the people, the kingdom, the temple, and worship, because God's faithfulness is greater than the fluctuations of history.

📖 Second: The Book of Second Chronicles
1. The Main Topic

If First Chronicles emphasizes greatly David and the preparation for the temple, then Second Chronicles begins directly from Solomon and the building of the temple and then continues the history of the kingdom of Judah until the exile.

And here the question becomes:

How does God's people live under the temple and the covenant?

And the answer that the book provides is:

By returning to God, prayer, worship, repentance, and seeking the face of the Lord.

2. Solomon

The book begins with Solomon.

And Solomon asks for wisdom.

Then he undertakes the project that David prepared for him:

Building the temple.

And the author connects Solomon's wisdom with the building of God's house.

And this makes the beginning of the book connected to the fulfillment of David's dream.

3. The Building of the Temple

Solomon builds the temple in Jerusalem.

And this event is the heart of Second Chronicles 2–7.

Materials are prepared.

Then the building.

Then the implements.

Then the bringing in of the Ark.

Then the appearance of the glory of the Lord.

And here the narrative reaches its climax.

4. The Glory of the Lord

When the temple is completed and consecrated, the glory of the Lord appears.

And worship becomes connected to God's presence.

And here the book presents a powerful picture:

The temple is not merely a national center; it is the place where the people of God are connected to His worship, His covenant, and His presence.

But as we saw in Kings, that does not mean that the building itself protects a person from disobedience.

5. Solomon's Prayer

Solomon's prayer in Second Chronicles occupies a very important place.

It is a prayer connected to the future of the nation.

And it repeatedly says:

If the people sin.

If the people are struck.

If drought comes.

If the enemy comes.

If the people return and pray.

And here one of the most important principles of Chronicles appears:

Returning to God opens the way of restoration.

6. 2 Chronicles 7:14

Among the most famous texts of the book is the Lord's saying:

"If My people who are called by My name humble themselves and pray and seek My face and turn from their wicked ways, then I will hear from heaven and forgive their sin and heal their land".

This verse must be understood in its original context.

It is not an abstract political promise to every nation in every circumstance.

Rather, it is part of God's covenant with Israel and the context of the temple, repentance, and return.

But the deep spiritual principle is clear:

Humility, prayer, seeking God's face, and repentance are the way of restoration.

7. The Kingdom of Judah

After Solomon, Second Chronicles focuses heavily on the kings of Judah.

And it does not give the northern kingdoms the same space that we find in Kings.

Because its primary goal is to trace:

The Davidic line.

The temple.

Worship.

Jerusalem.

For this reason, the history of Judah becomes a mirror of the relationship between the king, the people, and the Lord.

8. Rehoboam

Rehoboam begins badly.

But Chronicles adds important details.

When the leaders of Israel humble themselves because of the threat of Egypt, the Lord lessens the judgment.

And this agrees with one of the great principles in the book:

Humility changes a person's standing before God.

9. Asa

Asa appears as a reforming king.

He asks Judah to seek the Lord.

And he removes the foreign altars.

But his life also reveals the problem of relying on human alliances.

For in the end, the question does not remain:

"Did he begin well?"

But rather:

"Did he remain faithful to the end?"

10. Jehoshaphat

Jehoshaphat is concerned with teaching the people the law of the Lord.

And he sends teachers to the cities.

And this is a matter of utmost importance.

For reformation does not come only by demolishing idols.

But also:

By teaching the word of God.

And thus the book combines:

Reformation + teaching + worship.

11. Hezekiah

Hezekiah occupies a very important space.

And the reason is that he represents one of the greatest models of reformation after a long period of corruption.

He opens the doors of the temple.

He purifies it.

He restores the priests and the Levites.

He restores the sacrifices.

Then he calls the people to celebrate the Passover.

And here reformation becomes a spiritual rebuilding of the nation.

12. The Passover in the Days of Hezekiah

Hezekiah invites even the remnants of the northern tribes to come to Jerusalem.

And this is a very important point.

For the writer does not see Israel as merely two separate political groups.

Rather, he sees behind the political division one people with shared roots in the covenant.

And for this reason, worship becomes a means of restoring unity.

13. Manasseh

Manasseh begins in a very evil way.

But in Second Chronicles he presents an element not found in the same form in Kings:

Manasseh humbles himself and returns to the Lord.

And this is one of the strongest messages in the book.

For the writer wants to say to the community returning from exile:

Even the king who reached the utmost degrees of evil can find the door of repentance when he humbles himself before God.

14. Josiah

Josiah represents the last great reformation before the exile.

He begins with the repair of the temple.

Then the Law is discovered.

And when he hears it, he repents.

Then he renews the covenant.

Then he celebrates the Passover.

And the author presents Josiah as a king who understood that genuine reformation does not begin from politics.

But from:

The Word of God.

15. The Fall of Jerusalem

Despite the reformations, the nation does not continue in faithfulness.

And the Babylonians come.

Jerusalem is besieged.

The Temple is destroyed.

Many are killed.

And the people are taken into exile.

And here appears the danger of the historical accumulation of sin.

God is longsuffering.

But he does not ignore evil forever.

16. Why the Exile?

The book presents a direct explanation.

The exile came because the people continued in rejecting God.

And the land itself is transformed into a field of judgment.

But there is a very important detail:

The author connects the duration of the desolation with the sabbaths of the land that were not observed.

And this returns the reader to the Law of Moses.

That is, the history of the exile was not merely a military event.

But it was a fulfillment of the principle of the covenant.

17. Cyrus

But the book does not end with the exile.

At the end appears:

Cyrus, king of Persia.

And he issues a decree for the rebuilding of the house of the Lord.

And this ending is exceedingly important.

Because the book does not say:

"The story of Israel is ended."

But it says:

"The way of return has begun."

And for this reason, the conclusion of Chronicles serves as a direct bridge to the book of Ezra.

18. Why does the book end with Cyrus?

Because the goal of the author is not to produce a tragedy.

His goal is to produce hope.

The city fell.

And the Temple was destroyed.

And the king was gone.

And the people were taken into exile.

But:

God did not forget his promise.

And thus the final words of the book become the beginning of a new phase.

Adam

Beginning of the genealogical line, to affirm that the story of Israel is part of the story of all humanity.

Abraham

The origin of the covenant and the people.

Jacob/Israel

The tribal origin of the twelve tribes.

Jude

The tribe from which David came.

David

The king to whom the covenant, the Temple, and worship are connected.

Solomon

Builder of the Temple.

Rehoboam

The king in whose reign came the beginning of the kingdom's division.

Asa

The reformer king.

Jehoshaphat

The king who was concerned with teaching the Law.

Hezekiah

King of reform and reorganization of worship and Passover.

Manasseh

A powerful example of fall and then repentance.

Josiah

The last of the major reformations before the exile.

Cyrus

The Persian king who opened the way for return and the rebuilding of the Temple.

20. The Most Important Events

Genealogical line from Adam.

Establishing the line of Judah and David.

The death of Saul.

The unification of Israel around David.

The transfer of the Ark of the Covenant to Jerusalem.

God's covenant with David.

David's preparation for building the Temple.

Solomon's building of the Temple.

The dedication of the Temple.

The appearance of the glory of the Lord.

Solomon's prayer.

The division of the kingdom.

The reform of Asa.

The reform of Jehoshaphat.

The reform of Hezekiah.

The repentance of Manasseh.

Josiah's reform.

The fall of Jerusalem.

The Babylonian exile.

Cyrus’s decree.

21. The theological difference between Kings and Chronicles

This is a very important point for the researcher.

Kings looks at history from the angle of the question:

Why did the covenant people end up in exile?

Chronicles, however, looks at history from another angle:

How can the people, after exile, recover their identity, worship, and hope?

Therefore, we find a clear difference in emphasis.

Kings gives much space to the prophets and to northern Israel.

Chronicles, however, focuses more strongly on:

David.

The temple.

The priesthood.

The Levites.

Jerusalem.

The kings of Judah.

Reform and worship.

22. Chronicles and genealogies

Genealogies are not merely names.

They are a theological declaration:

God preserves the line that He promised.

For this reason, the writer reaches David.

Then he continues the line of David.

Then Zerubbabel and those who came after him appear.

Thus the reader sees that exile did not cut off history.

The line continues.

Identity continues.

The promise continues.

23. Chronicles and the Temple

The Temple is one of the most important keys to the book.

But it must be understood correctly.

The Temple is not merely a religious building.

It symbolizes:

The presence of God.

Worship.

The covenant.

Atonement and sacrifices.

The identity of the people.

The Davidic king.

For this reason, the rebuilding of the Temple after exile becomes part of the restoration of the people’s identity.

24. Chronicles and prayer

One of the most frequently repeated concepts:

Seeking the Lord.

Prayer.

Humility.

Seeking His face.

Return.

This appears in many stories.

The writer therefore wants to teach the new generation that the future does not depend only on rebuilding the walls.

But on rebuilding the relationship with God.

25. Chronicles and the king

The king in Chronicles is not an absolute monarch.

He is under the authority of God.

The righteous king leads the people to the Lord.

The wicked king leads the people away from Him.

For this reason, we find a clear relationship between:

The condition of the king

and

The condition of the people

and

The condition of worship.

26. Chronicles and the Messiah

Here we reach one of the deepest themes of the book.

Chronicles looks at:

David → the covenant → David’s line → the throne → the Temple → the promised king.

But history reveals that human kings cannot achieve perfection.

Solomon is wise, but he dies.

Hezekiah is faithful, but he dies.

Josiah is reforming, but he dies.

Therefore the question remains:

Where is the king whose kingdom will never end?

Here the biblical line turns toward the Messiah.

For Jesus is announced in the New Testament:

Son of David.

And He is the king in whom the promise is fulfilled.

27. The Temple and the Messiah

The Temple in Chronicles points to the center of God’s presence among His people.

But the New Testament lifts the concept of divine presence to a greater level in the person of Christ.

For Christ is not merely a king from the line of David.

But He is the One who comes to fulfill God’s purpose in dwelling among His people.

For this reason, studying Chronicles is very important for understanding the relationship between:

David — the Temple — worship — presence — the kingdom — Christ.

🔵 The most important message in First and Second Chronicles

God is faithful to His covenant even though humanity is not; therefore, exile was not the end of the story, but a discipline within the history of the covenant. Through David, the Temple, worship, and returning to God, hope remains open before His people.

🔑 The encyclopedic summary of the two books

Hebrew name: דִּבְרֵי הַיָּמִים — Divrê Hayyāmîm
Meaning: the matters of the days / the events of the days / the historical records.

Author: not certain; Jewish tradition attributes it to Ezra, while modern scholarship often speaks of the “writer of the annals” or “the historian.”

Probable date of writing: after the exile, generally in the fifth century BCE, with differences among scholars regarding the exact date.

Audience: the community of Judah returning from exile.

Subject: Reinterpreting the history of Israel in light of the covenant, temple, king, and worship.

Beginning of the story: Adam.

The human axis: David.

The royal axis: the line of David.

The cultic axis: the temple.

The priestly axis: the priests and Levites.

Crisis of the story: exile.

Window of hope: Cyrus and the return of the people.

The messianic axis: the expected Davidic king.

🔑 The sentence that summarizes all of Chronicles:

“Exile did not nullify the promise, ruin did not nullify the covenant, and the absence of the king did not nullify the hope of the coming King; for God, who preserved the line of David and called his people to his house, is the same one who opens the door of return and leads history toward the fulfillment of his promise.”

Thus, the sequence of books up to this point has become very clear: Samuel lays the foundation for the Davidic kingdom, Kings reveals the failure of kings and exile, and then Chronicles rereads history from the perspective of the covenant, temple, worship, and hope; this is an absolutely necessary angle before moving on to Ezra and Nehemiah, where we practically see the return of the people and the rebuilding of the temple and Jerusalem.
