The Second Letter of Peter
1. The Epistle’s Name and the Meaning of the Name

It is known as the Second Epistle of Peter and is one of the General Epistles in the New Testament. The name “Peter” is the name that the Lord Jesus gave to Simon son of Jonah, and its meaning is connected with a stone or rock. The nature of this epistle differs from the First Epistle; the first emphasizes hope and endurance amid suffering, whereas the second places strong emphasis on growth in the knowledge of Christ, steadfastness in truth, warning against false teachers, and awaiting the Lord’s coming and judgment. Therefore, it may be viewed as an epistle protecting the church from doctrinal and moral deviation and from losing hope in Christ’s coming.

2. The Author and His Character

The writer opens the epistle by saying: “Simon Peter, a servant and apostle of Jesus Christ, to those who have obtained with us an equally precious faith through the righteousness of our God and Savior Jesus Christ” (2 Peter 1: 1). Here he presents himself by the name “Simon Peter,” combining his former name with the apostolic name Christ gave him. His identity also appears in the reference to his approaching death, as he says: “knowing that the putting off of my tent is near, as our Lord Jesus Christ has also made known to me” (2 Peter 1: 14). This makes the epistle appear as though it were a final spiritual testament from an apostle who wishes to leave the church steadfast in truth after his departure.

3. To Whom Was the Epistle Written?

In the Second Epistle, Peter does not identify the regions in which the readers live as he did in the First Epistle, but he addresses believers who share Christian faith with him. In chapter three, he says: “This is now the second epistle I write to you, beloved” (2 Peter 3: 1), indicating that the Second Epistle is directed to an audience that had previously received his teaching. Therefore, its purpose is not to establish faith from the beginning, but to remind believers of what they knew and protect them from forgetfulness, deception, and deviation.

4. The Reason for Writing the Epistle

The main reason for the epistle is to establish believers in sound faith and warn them against false teachers who will enter among God’s people, in addition to addressing the question concerning the delay of Christ’s coming. Peter warns that some will say: “Where is the promise of his coming? For since the fathers fell asleep, all things continue as they were from the beginning of creation” (2 Peter 3: 4). Therefore, the epistle combines safeguarding doctrine, holiness of conduct, expectation of the coming, and confidence in God’s promises.

5. Date of the Epistle

The writer connects the epistle with an advanced stage of his ministry and refers to his approaching death; therefore, it is generally understood as one of his final writings. Scholars differ concerning the precise date and the historical attribution of the epistle to Peter, but traditional Christian reading attributes it to the apostle Peter, while critical studies discuss questions of style, language, and historical circumstances. From an encyclopedic perspective, a distinction should be made between what the text itself states and the historical conclusions over which studies differ.

6. The Main Theme

The central theme of the epistle is steadfastness in the knowledge of Christ and truth, spiritual growth, holy living, resistance to false teaching, and expectation of the day of the Lord. Therefore, the epistle begins with growth and ends with expectation of Christ’s coming; between its beginning and end, Peter warns against everything that may cause the believer to fall away from truth, whether fleshly desire, distorted teaching, or doubt concerning God’s promises.

7. Grace, Peace, and the Knowledge of God

Peter says: “May grace and peace be multiplied to you in the knowledge of God and of Jesus our Lord” (2 Peter 1: 2). From the beginning, he places knowledge at the center of Christian life, but he does not mean merely theological information; rather, he means a living knowledge of God and Christ that affects conduct. Therefore, true knowledge in the epistle is not separate from holiness, but leads to it.

8. Divine Power and Everything for Life and Godliness

Peter declares: “As his divine power has given to us all things pertaining to life and godliness, through the knowledge of him who called us by glory and virtue” (2 Peter 1: 3). This is an important foundation in the theology of the epistle: God does not call the believer to a holy life and then leave him without spiritual resources; rather, in Christ he grants him everything he needs for life and godliness.

9. The Great Promises

He says:

“By which he has given to us exceedingly great and precious promises, that through these you may become partakers of the divine nature, having escaped the corruption that is in the world through lust”
(2 Peter 1: 4).

The expression “partakers of the divine nature” does not mean that a human being becomes God in essence; rather, it refers to the believer’s participation in God’s life, grace, and holiness, and separation from the world’s corruption.

10. The Ladder of Spiritual Growth

Peter presents a consecutive sequence:

“But also for this very reason, giving all diligence, add to your faith virtue, to virtue knowledge, to knowledge self-control, to self-control perseverance, to perseverance godliness, to godliness brotherly kindness, and to brotherly kindness love” (2 Peter 1: 5-7).

Here we see that Christian faith is not an arrival point, but the beginning of a path of growth in which the believer moves from faith to virtue, knowledge, self-control, perseverance, godliness, and love.

11. Freedom from Spiritual Barrenness

Peter says that if these qualities are present and increasing, they make the believer:

“neither idle nor unfruitful in the knowledge of our Lord Jesus Christ”
(2 Peter 1: 8).

Knowledge that produces no moral and spiritual fruit becomes practically incomplete knowledge. Therefore, Peter measures the soundness of knowledge by its fruit.

12. The Danger of Spiritual Forgetfulness

He says concerning the one in whom these qualities are absent:

“he is blind, short-sighted, having forgotten the cleansing of his former sins”
(2 Peter 1: 9).

The danger is not merely ignorance, but forgetting what God has done in Christ. When the believer forgets the grace of forgiveness, he becomes more vulnerable to returning to his former life.

13. Confirming Calling and Election

He says:

“Therefore, brethren, be even more diligent to make your calling and election sure, for if you do these things you will never stumble”
(2 Peter 1: 10).

Peter does not present diligence as a means of purchasing salvation, but as a call to demonstrate the reality of faith in a steadfast practical life.

14. Entering the Eternal Kingdom

He says:

“For so an entrance into the eternal kingdom of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ will be abundantly provided for you.”
(2 Peter 1: 11).

The end toward which the believer looks is not earthly success, but the eternal kingdom of Christ.

15. Peter as a Servant Who Reminds the Church

Peter clearly repeats the idea of reminder: “Therefore I will not neglect to remind you always of these things, though you know them and are established in the present truth” (2 Peter 1: 12). This is an important point in apostolic ministry; Christian teaching does not always require new information, but also continual reminder of known truth, lest the believer forget it.

16. The Nearness of Peter’s Departure

He says:

“Knowing that the putting off of my tent is near, as our Lord Jesus Christ also made known to me.”
(2 Peter 1: 14).

This gives the epistle a powerful testamentary character; Peter knows that his earthly ministry is approaching its end, and therefore he wishes to leave behind a faith firmly grounded in the word of God and not dependent solely on his personal presence.

17. We Do Not Follow Cleverly Devised Myths

He says:

“For we did not make known to you the power and coming of our Lord Jesus Christ by following cleverly devised myths, but we were eyewitnesses of His majesty.”
(2 Peter 1: 16).

Here Peter addresses the possibility of the Christian message being accused of being a religious myth; he affirms that the apostles did not build their faith upon cleverly devised myths, but upon testimony and eyewitness observation.

18. The Transfiguration and Peter’s Testimony

Peter clearly refers to the event of the Transfiguration, when he was with Christ, James, and John, and says:

“For He received from God the Father honor and glory when such a voice came to Him from the Excellent Glory: ‘This is My beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased.’”
(2 Peter 1: 17).

For Peter, the Transfiguration was not merely a spiritual experience, but testimony to Christ’s glory and His identity as the Son.

19. The Prophetic Word

He says:

“And we have the prophetic word made more sure, to which you do well to pay attention, as to a lamp shining in a dark place.”
(2 Peter 1: 19).

Here Peter connects apostolic testimony with the prophetic word, presenting the word of God as a lamp that shines amid darkness.

20. The Source of Prophecy

He says:

“For prophecy never came by the will of man, but holy men of God spoke as they were moved by the Holy Spirit.”
(2 Peter 1: 21).

This is one of the foundational texts in the study of biblical inspiration; the epistle affirms that the ultimate source of prophecy is not human will, but the work of the Holy Spirit in the prophets.

21. False Teachers

In the second chapter, Peter turns to a warning:

“But there were also false prophets among the people, even as there will be false teachers among you, who will secretly introduce destructive heresies.”
(2 Peter 2: 1).

The danger does not come only from outside; it may arise within the religious community itself through teaching that appears Christian but carries destructive deviation.

22. Denying the Master Who Bought Them

He adds:

“And in their greed they will exploit you with fabricated words.”
(2 Peter 2: 3).

Peter joins doctrinal deviation with moral greed, because for him false teaching is not merely an intellectual error, but may become a means of exploiting people.

23. Examples of Divine Judgment

Peter uses examples from biblical history to demonstrate that God does not overlook evil: the angels who sinned, the ancient world in the days of Noah, and Sodom and Gomorrah. The purpose is to show that God is able to preserve and rescue the godly while at the same time judging the wicked.

24. Noah and Lot

Peter describes Noah as:

“A preacher of righteousness.”
(2 Peter 2: 5).

He also speaks of Lot, who was tormented by the corruption of Sodom. Through these 2 figures, Peter presents a clear principle: God knows how to rescue the godly from temptation and to keep the unrighteous until the day of judgment (Second Peter 2: 9).

25. The Danger of Lust and Corruption

Peter connects false teaching with moral dissipation, warning of people:

“Having eyes full of adultery and that cannot cease from sin.”
(2 Peter 2: 14).

This confirms that doctrine and ethics are connected; deviation in the knowledge of God can produce deviation in life.

26. Balaam Son of Beor

Peter recalls the figure of Balaam, who knew the will of God but went astray because of greed, and says that the false teachers:

“Have forsaken the right way and gone astray.”
(2 Peter 2: 15).

Balaam becomes an example of the servant who may know religious matters but allows greed and personal interest to corrupt his ministry.

27. False Freedom

He says of the false teachers:

“While they promise them freedom, they themselves are slaves of corruption.”
(2 Peter 2: 19).

True freedom in the Bible is not that a person does everything he desires, but that he is freed from the dominion of sin in order to live for God.

28. Returning to Sin

Peter presents a most powerful image:

“What happened to them is according to the true proverb: ‘A dog has returned to its own vomit,’ and, ‘A washed sow to wallowing in the mire.’”
(2 Peter 2: 22).

The image emphasizes the ugliness of returning to corruption after knowing the way of righteousness, and reveals the danger of dealing superficially with faith.

29. Chapter Three and the Second Coming

Peter begins the third chapter by reminding believers that the prophets and apostles spoke about the coming of the Lord, because the fundamental problem believers will face is mockery of the promise of His coming.

30. Where Is the Promise of His Coming?

He says:

“Saying, ‘Where is the promise of His coming? For since the fathers fell asleep, all things continue as they were from the beginning of creation.’”
(2 Peter 3: 4).

Peter responds to this objection by reminding them of the Flood, affirming that history is not evidence that God does not intervene; He has intervened in judgment before.

31. God Is Not Slow Concerning the Promise

He says:

“The Lord is not slow concerning His promise, as some count slowness, but is patient toward us, not willing that any should perish but that all should come to repentance.”
(2 Peter 3: 9).

This is among the most important verses for understanding the delay of the coming; the apparent delay is neither inability nor forgetfulness, but is related to God’s patience and the granting of opportunity for repentance.

32. The Day of the Lord

He says:

“But the day of the Lord will come as a thief in the night, in which the heavens will pass away with a great noise, the elements will melt with fervent heat, and the earth and the works that are in it will be burned up.”
(2 Peter 3: 10).

Thus, the coming is not merely an emotional event for believers, but is also connected with cosmic judgment and the declaration of God’s ultimate sovereignty.

33. Awaiting New Heavens and a New Earth

After the scene of judgment, he says:

“But according to His promise, we await new heavens and a new earth, in which righteousness dwells.”
(2 Peter 3: 13).

This is the positive conclusion of the eschatological vision: a world in which righteousness dwells.

34. What should the believer's life be like?

Peter asks:

“Since all these things are to be dissolved, what sort of people ought you to be in holy conduct and godliness?”
(2 Peter 3: 11).

This verse reveals the relationship between eschatology and ethics: knowledge of the future must transform the present.

The believer who awaits Christ's coming does not live indifferently, but lives in holiness and godliness.

35. Awaiting and hastening the Day of God

Peter speaks of awaiting the Day of God, not to drive the believer into chronological calculations, but to move him toward spiritual readiness.

Biblical prophecy does not aim to satisfy curiosity about history as much as it aims to form a people prepared to meet the Lord.

36. The Lord's patience is salvation

Peter says of Paul:

“Consider the patience of our Lord as salvation, just as our beloved brother Paul also wrote to you according to the wisdom given to him.”
(2 Peter 3: 15).

This is an important link between Peter's ministry and Paul's ministry, and it provides early testimony to the esteem in which Paul's teaching and writings were held.

37. Difficult writings

Peter indicates that there are matters in Paul's letters:

“In them are some things difficult to understand.”
(2 Peter 3: 16).

This clarifies that some New Testament texts themselves require careful study and must not be distorted, because the unstable may distort them “to their own destruction.”

38. Warning against falling

He says:

“Therefore, beloved, since you know this beforehand, beware lest you be carried away by the error of the wicked and fall from your steadfastness.”
(2 Peter 3: 17).

Prior knowledge is not a guarantee of indifference; rather, it makes the believer responsible for caution and steadfastness.

39. Growing in grace and knowledge

The epistle concludes with one of its most important summaries:

“But grow in the grace and knowledge of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ. To Him be the glory now and to the day of eternity. Amen.”
(2 Peter 3: 18).

The epistle began with knowledge and growth, and it ends with growth in grace and knowledge; it is as though Peter sets before the church the positive remedy for every danger against which he warned: do not remain stagnant, but grow in Christ.

40. Christ in the Second Epistle of Peter

Christ is the center of the entire epistle; He is our Lord and Savior, the possessor of glory, who will come again, and whose coming is associated with judgment and the renewal of creation. Christ appears at the beginning of the epistle as the source of grace and peace, at the Transfiguration as the beloved Son whose glory the Father proclaimed, and at the end of the epistle as the coming Lord whose arrival the church awaits and in whose knowledge believers must grow. Therefore, the epistle does not separate Christ who saved, Christ who sanctifies, and Christ who will come.

41. Connections with the Old Testament

The Second Epistle of Peter rests on a clear Old Testament background, especially in its discussion of creation, the waters, the Flood, Noah, judgment, Sodom and Gomorrah, Balaam, the Day of the Lord, the new heavens, and the new earth. The Flood in particular appears as historical and theological evidence that God is able to preserve the godly and judge the wicked world, while the image of the new heavens and new earth becomes an extension of the prophetic hope for the restoration of creation and the establishment of righteousness.

42. The relationship between the First and Second Epistles of Peter

A clear complementarity can be observed between the two epistles. The first confronts suffering and persecution from without and teaches the believer how to follow the suffering Christ in hope and holiness, whereas the second confronts danger from within: false teaching, moral corruption, and doubt concerning the coming. In the first, the believer awaits glory after suffering; in the second, he awaits the Day of the Lord and the new heavens and new earth. Thus, the two epistles together concern the life of the church in the world: steadfastness under external pressure and steadfastness in the face of internal deviation.

43. The major movement of the epistle

The epistle begins with the faith God grants and the call to grow in virtue, knowledge, self-control, patience, godliness, and love. It then affirms that the believer is called to confirm his calling and election and avoid spiritual barrenness. After this, Peter presents his apostolic testimony concerning Christ and His glory at the Transfiguration, and affirms the certainty of the prophetic word and its inspiration by the Holy Spirit. The discourse then turns to warning against false teachers who distort the truth, exploit believers, and turn freedom into an avenue for corruption, citing the judgment of angels, the ancient world, Sodom and Gomorrah, and the example of Balaam. In the third chapter, Peter moves to the crisis of doubt concerning Christ's coming, explaining that God is not slow but patient, and that the Day of the Lord will certainly come. He then lifts the church's eyes to the end: new heavens and a new earth in which righteousness dwells, and transforms the expectation of the future into a practical call to holiness, before concluding with the comprehensive exhortation: “Grow in the grace and knowledge of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ.”

44. Encyclopedic summary

The Second Epistle of Peter is an epistle of steadfastness, growth, discernment, and eschatological hope. It teaches that true faith does not remain stagnant, but grows in virtue, knowledge, self-control, patience, godliness, and love. It strongly warns against false teachers who distort doctrine, exploit believers, and turn freedom into an opportunity for corruption. At the same time, the epistle addresses one of the earliest objections to the Christian faith, namely the question of the delay of Christ's coming, declaring that the delay of the appointed time is not forgetfulness on God's part, but patience, mercy, and a call to repentance. Nevertheless, the Day of the Lord is certainly coming, judgment will come, and, according to God's promise, new heavens and a new earth in which righteousness dwells will appear. Therefore, the epistle does not call the believer to determine the time of the coming, but to be prepared for it, to live in holiness and godliness, to beware of error, and to remain steadfast in the truth. The epistle ends where the whole life of the believer should begin and end: in Christ, growing in His grace and knowledge, and giving glory to Him now and to the day of eternity.
