Introduction to the Book of Nahum

The central idea of the book: The Book of Nahum is a book of judgment upon Nineveh and the fall of Assyrian power, but in depth it is not merely a record of the fall of a great city, but an announcement that God does not allow injustice and violence and tyranny to continue forever. If the Book of Jonah showed Nineveh responding to the call and repenting so that judgment was lifted from it, then Nahum comes later to announce that Nineveh has returned to violence and evil, and the hour of reckoning has come. Therefore the two books together form a deeply theological testimony: God is merciful toward the repentant, but just toward persistent evil. And Nahum announces that the Lord is 'slow to anger and great in power, and will by no means clear the guilty,' and at the same time says to the suffering people: 'The Lord is good, a stronghold in a day of trouble, and He knows those who take refuge in Him.'

1. Name of the book and its meaning

The name of the book is Nahum — נַחוּם — Naḥûm, and it is a Hebrew name derived from the root נחם — naḥam, associated with the idea of comfort or consolation or solace. Therefore the name Nahum can be understood to mean 'the Comforter' or 'the One who comforts.' And this meaning becomes of utmost importance when we read the book from the perspective of Judah, because the fall of Nineveh was not merely a military report, but was a comfort to a people who lived under the threat of the Assyrian Empire.

2. Who is Nahum?

The book describes its author by saying:

'The book of the vision of Nahum of Elkosh.'

And the writing does not provide much information about the personal life of the prophet. Therefore we do not know with certainty the names of his parents or the duration of his ministry or the year of his birth or the year of his death. But his connection with Elkosh gives him his geographical identity, and scholars have differed in determining the exact location of this town, and therefore one should be cautious about asserting one place as certain.

3. The Birth and Death Year of Nahum

The Bible does not record the birth year of Nahum or the year of his death. Also, the book itself does not provide a personal biography of the prophet, but focuses almost entirely on the message of judgment against Nineveh. Therefore, we cannot give a precise date for his birth or death without entering into unverified historical hypotheses.

4. Date of writing the book

Determining the time of the book can be narrowed through the events that Nahum mentions. He speaks of the fall of No-Amon, that is, Thebes in Egypt, as an event that actually occurred, while at the same time he prophesies the fall of Nineveh. Thebes fell before the Assyrians in the seventh century B.C., then Nineveh fell in 612 B.C., and therefore many scholars believe that the Book of Nahum was written in the period between these two events approximately.

5. The Historical Background of the Book

Assyria in Nahum's time was one of the most violent imperial powers in the ancient world. Its authority extended over vast areas of the Near East, and subjugated many peoples through military force, deportation, and political violence. Judah had lived under intense Assyrian pressure, and therefore the announcement of the fall of Nineveh was for God's people an announcement that the empire which seemed incapable of falling was not beyond God's dominion.

6. The Relationship between Jonah and Nahum

This is a fundamental point for understanding the book. In the Book of Jonah we see Nineveh responding to the call and repenting, so God lifts judgment from it, but in the Book of Nahum we see Nineveh having returned to violence and evil, so comes the announcement of judgment upon it. Therefore the two books should not be read completely separately, but can be seen as two complementary images of the nature of God: mercy toward the repentant, and judgment toward persistent evil.

7. The Reason for Writing the Book

The direct reason is the announcement of the fall of Nineveh. But the theological purpose is much deeper, because the book wants to affirm that God is Lord of history and of nations, and that empires no matter how powerful cannot place themselves beyond divine judgment. The book also offers comfort to peoples who suffered from Assyrian oppression, and announces that God sees violence and does not forget the oppressed.

8. The Division of the Book of Nahum

The book consists of three chapters, and can be divided in a simple way into three interconnected axes. The first chapter announces the character of God as a just judge and a stronghold for His people, then the second chapter depicts the attack on Nineveh and the fall of its power, while the third chapter reveals the reasons for the judgment and announces finally that Nineveh's wound will not heal. And so the book moves from who is God? to what will He do? then to why will Nineveh fall?

9. Nahum 1 — The Announcement of God's Character

The book begins with powerful words:

'The Lord is a jealous and avenging God; the Lord is avenging and wrathful; the Lord takes vengeance on His adversaries and keeps wrath for His enemies.'

These words may seem harsh at first, but they come in the context of an announcement of God's justice toward evil. Nahum does not present a capricious God or quick to anger, but says immediately after:

'The Lord is slow to anger and great in power, and will by no means clear the guilty.'

And here the book combines God's patience with His justice.

10. God is Slow to Anger

This phrase connects Nahum with a broad biblical line that appears in the Old Testament, and especially in the Lord's declaration about Himself to Moses in Exodus 34. God is not swift in executing judgment, and this appeared clearly in the history of Nineveh. The city was given an opportunity in the days of Jonah, but God's patience does not mean that evil will not be held accountable forever.

11. 'And Will By No Means Clear the Guilty'

This phrase completes the picture. God does not rush to punishment, but He also does not declare that evil is of no consequence. And if a person or society or empire insists on injustice, then the delay of judgment does not mean its cancellation. Therefore Nahum presents a very important concept of God's patience which does not mean His abandonment of justice.

12. God's Dominion Over Nature

Nahum depicts the Lord in a way that connects with nature:

'The mountains quake before Him, the hills melt; the earth heaves before Him, the world and all who dwell in it.'

The purpose is not to present a scientific description of nature, but to announce that the God who judges Nineveh is the same one who created nature and has dominion over it. And therefore the most powerful military empire in the region is not stronger than a God before whom the sea and mountains and earth move.

13. 'The Lord is Good'

In the midst of this fearful announcement appears one of the most beautiful phrases of the book:

'The Lord is good, a stronghold in a day of trouble, and He knows those who take refuge in Him.'

And here appears the meaning of the book for the believing person. For judgment upon evil is not terrifying news for one who trusts in God, but can be a source of comfort. The God who is fire for judgment upon evil is the same one who is a stronghold for those who seek Him.

14. Nahum and the Comfort of the Oppressed

The name Nahum itself is connected with comfort, and this idea appears in the message of the book. When a people lives under the authority of an unjust power, evil can seem as if it has triumphed finally. But Nahum announces that history is not the possession of empires, but the possession of God. And therefore the fall of Nineveh becomes a message of hope for all who ask: how long will injustice continue?

15. Nineveh under judgment

After declaring the character of God, the book moves directly to Nineveh. The city that was the object of repentance in Jonah’s time has now become the object of judgment. This does not mean that God has changed, but that humanity’s response to God has real consequences, and the mercy shown in Jonah’s day was not a permanent license to continue in sin.

16. “It is shattered.”

The book declares that the power of Nineveh will end and that the Lord will remove its yoke from his people. Here the function of Nahum as a message of liberation also appears. The judgment that falls on the oppressor becomes relief for the oppressed, and therefore the book combines the judgment of the aggressor with the comfort of the suffering.

17. Feet on the Mountains

Nahum says:

“Behold, upon the mountains are the feet of a herald, proclaiming peace.”

This is a beautiful image in the midst of a book that speaks of war. After the threat of the enemy collapses, the news of peace comes to God’s people. For this reason, this verse became especially important in later Christian reading, because it opens a door to understanding the idea of the gospel and peace.

18. Nahum 2 — The Siege of Nineveh

Chapter two turns into a rapid military scene. There are attackers, walls, chariots, gates, military movement, and then the collapse of the city. The prophet uses powerful pictorial language that makes the reader feel as though he is seeing the battle before his eyes. This style makes the book closer to a cinematic prophetic scene of the fall of an empire.

19. The Walls of Nineveh

Nineveh was a vast city surrounded by strong fortifications, so its fall seemed, from a human standpoint, extraordinarily difficult. Yet Nahum uses this very strength to make the fall more significant: all that human beings build to protect themselves cannot prevent the judgment of God when the time comes.

20. The Spoils of the City

The book points to the looting of the city and the emptying of its treasuries. The wealth accumulated by empire through force does not become a guarantee of its survival. This is an important point in the prophetic message: wealth not based on justice cannot buy security.

21. The Image of the Lion

Nahum uses images of lions when speaking of Nineveh:

“Where is the lion’s den and the pasture of the lion cubs, where the lion walked and the cub...”

This is an intentional image, because Assyria represented predatory power in the region. The city that had devoured many peoples became itself prey to judgment. This is one of the strongest literary images in the book: the predator becomes the prey.

22. Nahum 3 — Why Is Nineveh Judged?

Chapter three begins with a clear cry:

“Woe to the city of blood! It is full of lies and plunder; there is no end to the spoils.”

Here the prophet gives the reasons for judgment. Nineveh is not a city that falls because of a sudden military weakness, but a city in which violence, deceit, robbery, and plunder have accumulated. Therefore, its fall comes as a moral and spiritual consequence, not merely a political loss.

23. “The City of Blood”

This expression summarizes the nature of the Assyrian empire as the book presents it. Military power was transformed into a system of domination and subjugation, and the city benefited from the conquest of peoples. Therefore, God declares that the violence practiced by Nineveh will not remain without account.

24. Nineveh and Moral Corruption

The accusation is not limited to military violence, but includes deceit, seduction, sorcery, and figurative fornication associated with political alliances and treachery. Thus the book presents a picture of a city that combines power, violence, deception, and spiritual and moral corruption.

25. The Fall of No-amon

Nahum cites the city of No-amon, that is, Thebes in Egypt, as an example of a city that was powerful and fortified yet still fell. He says:

“Are you better than No-amon, who sat among the rivers, with water all around her, whose fortress was the sea and whose wall was the sea?”

The point is clear: if a great city such as Thebes has fallen, Nineveh cannot claim that her fortifications make her an exception to history.

26. “Is She Better than No-amon?”

This comparison carries strong historical and theological logic. Nineveh sees herself as great, but God reminds her that great cities before her thought the same of themselves. Therefore, the book warns against the illusion of political immortality. Empires rise and fall, but God does not change.

27. The Historical Fall of Nineveh

Nineveh fell in 612 B.C. to a coalition of Babylonians and Medes, and with that her position as the capital of the Assyrian empire ended. This event provides a strong historical background for understanding the confident tone of the book of Nahum. The prophet declares that the city that appears strong will fall, and history records its fall.

28. Nahum and the Fall of Empires

One of the book’s most important ideas is that political power is not the measure of what is right. An empire may be great militarily but morally corrupt, and in such a case its strength is not a guarantee of its survival. This principle recurs throughout the Bible in Babylon, Egypt, Edom, Assyria, and others: God judges nations according to their deeds.

29. Nahum and Divine Justice

The book does not say that every pain will end immediately, but it does declare that injustice will not be the final word. This is a central idea in biblical faith, because human beings sometimes see evil as strong and enduring, yet the prophet looks at history from the perspective of God. Therefore Nahum gives the believer the ability to trust that delay is not the cancellation of justice.

30. Nahum and Jonah — The Deep Comparison

The two books can be placed side by side:

Jonah Nahum
Nineveh hears the warning Nineveh faces judgment
Nineveh repents Nineveh persists in sin
God shows mercy to the city God condemns the city
Jonah is angry at mercy Nahum proclaims the justice of God
Focus on repentance Focus on judgment
“Merciful and gracious” “and will by no means clear the guilty.”

Thus there is no conflict between the two books; rather, a clear harmony between mercy and justice appears.

31. Nahum and Christ

When reading the book messianically, one should not turn every detail of Nineveh’s fall into a direct symbol of Christ. Yet the principles announced in the book are consistent with the fuller revelation of the New Testament: God is holy, evil will be judged, the oppressed find hope, and peace comes from God. Likewise, the image of “the herald of peace” in Nahum 1:15 enters into the biblical line that later reaches the gospel proclamation.

32. “The Herald of Peace”

The verse:

“Behold, on the mountains are the feet of a herald, proclaiming peace.”

It became especially important when compared with Isaiah 52:7 and the other Old Testament texts that connect the coming of the messenger with the proclamation of salvation and peace. In the New Testament this idea expands to become the gospel proclamation, where true peace is associated with reconciliation with God.

33. Nahum and the Gospel

It may seem strange that a short book about the fall of a pagan city has anything to do with the gospel, but the central idea is that God defeats evil in order to open the way for peace. The book does not present peace as merely the absence of war, but as a state that comes after the removal of the power that enslaved and terrorized. This idea reaches its fulfillment in Christ, who proclaims the kingdom of God and reconciles humanity to God.

34. What Does Nahum Teach Us about God?

It teaches us that God is slow to anger, great in power, just, holy, merciful, and a refuge for those who trust in him. These attributes should not be separated from one another, because God is not merciful at the expense of his holiness, nor just without mercy. The book of Nahum presents this truth with great concentration through the fate of Nineveh.

35. What Does It Teach Us about Evil?

Evil may appear at a certain time to have triumphed decisively, but it cannot guarantee its future. Nineveh possessed an army, wealth, fortifications, and power, yet it could not prevent its fall. Therefore the book declares that power without righteousness carries within it the seeds of its own collapse.

36. What Does It Teach Us about the Oppressed?

The oppressed may not see justice immediately, but the book does not ask them to believe that God is absent. Nahum says that the Lord is “a refuge in the day of distress” and that he knows those who trust in him. This means that God’s knowledge of the oppressed is not merely theoretical knowledge, but a declaration that God is present in the history of pain and persecution.

37. What Do We Learn about Empires?

No human empire is eternal. Assyria seemed, in its own time, to be invincible, yet Nahum announced its fall before it happened. Thus the book presents a historical and theological principle of great importance: God permits empires to rise for a time, but he does not grant them an eternal right to practice evil.

38. What Do We Learn about Repentance?

Jonah declares that Nineveh can repent, and Nahum declares that Nineveh will be judged. Their harmony confirms that repentance is not a mere formality. God’s mercy is real, but it is not an open license to continue in sin, because persistence in sin ultimately leads to judgment.

39. The Spiritual Message for the Church

Nahum warns the church not to interpret God’s patience as approval of sin. He also warns it not to trust in human power, institutions, or influence as if they can protect a person from the consequences of deviation. In contrast, the book gives the church an important comfort: God does not forget the oppressed, and he does not allow evil to have the final word.

40. The Most Important Persons and Peoples in the Book
Nahum the Elkoshite

The prophet who announced the fall of Nineveh and gave a message of comfort to God’s people.

Nineveh

The Assyrian capital that became the focus of judgment in the book.

The Assyrians

The imperial power that exercised domination and violence over many peoples.

The People of Judah

The indirect recipient of the comfort of the book, because he lived under the threat of Assyria.

No-amon / Thebes

An Egyptian city that Nahum used as an example of the fall of a mighty power that seemed fortified.

The Lord

The central figure in the book, as the just Judge and refuge of the faithful.

41. The Most Important Events in the Book

A declaration of God's nature, a declaration of His slowness to anger and His greatness, a declaration of His protection of those who trust in Him, the coming fall of Nineveh, a depiction of the military siege, the collapse of the city's power, the plundering of its wealth, the fall of the image of the lion, the exposure of the crimes of the city of bloodshed, the comparison between Nineveh and No-amon, and finally the declaration that Nineveh's wound will not heal.

42. The Most Important Message Presented by the Book

God does not ignore injustice forever, nor does He allow human power to become a god, and the delay of judgment does not mean that evil has prevailed. God gave Nineveh an opportunity to repent in the days of Jonah, but its persistence in violence and evil led to its judgment in the days of Nahum. Therefore, the book brings together two truths that must not be separated: “The LORD is slow to anger and great in power” and “He will by no means clear the guilty.” He is simultaneously a judge of evil and a fortress for the oppressed. Thus, the fall of Nineveh declares that history is not in the hands of empires, but in the hand of God.

43. The Major Movement of the Book of Nahum

The book can be summarized in this path:

Nineveh's evil.

Then:

God's patience.

Then:

The continuation of evil.

Then:

The declaration of judgment.

Then:

The siege.

Then:

The fall of the city.

Then:

The oppressed are set free.

Then:

The message of peace.

Thus, the book moves from the power of the empire to the sovereignty of God, from fear to consolation, and from violence to peace.

🔑 The Sentence That Summarizes the Book of Nahum

“The Book of Nahum is the other side of Nineveh's story in the Book of Jonah. In Jonah, we see God show mercy to the city when it repents, while in Nahum, we see the same city face judgment after returning to the path of violence and evil. Therefore, the book does not teach us that God is harsh; rather, it teaches us that His mercy does not cancel His justice and that His patience does not mean His approval of evil. The LORD is slow to anger and great in power and will by no means clear the guilty, yet at the same time He is good, a stronghold in the day of trouble, and He knows those who trust in Him. Thus, the fall of Nineveh becomes a message of consolation to every oppressed person: evil may appear strong for a time, but no empire is stronger than God, and no injustice can escape God's justice forever.”
