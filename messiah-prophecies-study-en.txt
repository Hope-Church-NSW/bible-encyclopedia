Christ in the Prophecies
An encyclopedic theological study of the person of the Messiah in the Old Testament, His fulfillment in the New Testament, and the Jewish thought concerning the Messiah
1. The Meaning of the Word “Christ” and the Question of the Messiah

The Arabic word Masih corresponds to the Hebrew מָשִׁיחַ — Mashiach, meaning “the anointed one.” The verb originally refers to anointing with oil for consecration to a sacred task or office. Therefore, the term “Christ” in the Old Testament was not restricted to one person; it was applied to the king, the priest, and, in some passages, to a person whom God raised up to fulfill a particular purpose, so that even Cyrus the Persian was called “my anointed” in Isaiah 45:1. Yet as biblical hope developed, especially after the collapse of the Davidic monarchy and the exile, “the anointed one” gradually came to signify an awaited future figure associated with the Davidic king and the restoration of God's people. Historical studies confirm that the idea of “messianism” in its organized sense developed gradually and was not a fully developed doctrinal system in a single form in the Old Testament.

2. The True Beginning of Messianic Prophecies: The Seed of the Woman

The first thread in the messianic fabric appears immediately after the fall:

“And I will put enmity between you and the woman, and between your seed and her seed. He shall crush your head, and you shall crush his heel.”
(Genesis 3:15).

The text does not use the title “Christ,” but it presents the first structure of the story of salvation: the seed of the woman, conflict with the serpent, a wound in the heel, and final victory over the serpent's head. In Christian interpretation, this text becomes the basis for what is sometimes called the Protoevangelium — the first Gospel; that is, the first biblical declaration of the victory of human offspring over the source of evil.

Methodologically, however, a distinction must be made: interpreting the text as a direct prophecy about Jesus is a later Christian theological reading, not the only ancient Jewish interpretation, nor even the only apparent meaning of the text in its Genesis context. This distinction is very important so that we do not turn every text about “seed” into a direct prophecy without studying its context.

3. The Promise to Abraham: In Your Seed All Nations Are Blessed

The Lord says to Abraham:

“And in your seed all the nations of the earth shall be blessed, because you have obeyed my voice.”
(Genesis 22:18).

Here the messianic line expands from human being → family → people → all nations. The promise to Abraham speaks not only of Israel's prosperity but also carries within it a universal purpose: the blessing of the nations through the “seed.”

This becomes very important in the New Testament, where Paul connects the promise of the seed with Christ in Galatians 3:16. Thus, in the apostolic reading, Christ becomes the connecting link through whom Abraham's promise reaches the nations.

4. Judah and “Shiloh”

Among the most important ancient texts is:

“The scepter shall not depart from Judah, nor the ruler's staff from between his feet, until Shiloh comes; and to him shall be the obedience of peoples.”
(Genesis 49:10).

This text was very important in later Jewish interpretation. Targum Onkelos understands “Shiloh” in an explicitly messianic direction and translates the meaning as indicating that the king will come from the house of Judah and that the nations will submit to him. The Talmud likewise connects “Shiloh” with the Messiah in its discussion of the origin of the Messiah's name.

Thus, from a very early time, we have a clear line:

Judah → king → Shiloh/Messiah → submission of the nations.

This is important because it makes the Messiah in Jewish thought not merely a religious reformer, but a king whose authority extends beyond Israel to the nations.

5. The Star of Jacob

Balaam says:

“I see him, but not now; I behold him, but not near. A star shall come forth from Jacob, and a scepter shall rise from Israel; it shall crush the borders of Moab and destroy all the sons of tumult.”
(Numbers 24:17).

Three elements appear here: a star, a scepter, authority, and victory.

This text became part of Jewish messianic thought; indeed, Jewish tradition connected the figure of Bar Kokhba with the idea of the “star” in the second century AD, and thus his name bore the meaning “son of the star.” This background is very important because it clarifies that the expectation of the Messiah was not merely the expectation of a spiritual preacher, but could become the expectation of a leader who would liberate Israel and defeat its enemies.

6. The Promise to David: The Heart of Messianic Prophecy

If we wish to know the origin of the concept of the Messiah, the Son of David, we must return to 2 Samuel 7.

The Lord says to David:

“When your days are fulfilled and you lie down with your fathers, I will raise up your seed after you, who shall come forth from your body, and I will establish his kingdom... And I will establish the throne of his kingdom forever.”
(2 Samuel 7:12–13).

Here the backbone of Davidic hope was born:

David → seed → king → throne → kingdom → forever.

The question that remained open after David's death was: Who is the king able to fulfill the promise of the everlasting throne?

7. “I Will Be His Father, and He Will Be My Son”

The Lord says concerning David's seed:

“I will be his father, and he shall be my son.”
(2 Samuel 7:14).

In the context of the book of Samuel, the text first refers to David's royal seed, especially Solomon, but it carries within itself a larger horizon because of the phrase “the throne of his kingdom forever.”

For this reason, “Son of David” became a powerful messianic title in Jewish and Christian tradition. In the New Testament, Jesus repeatedly appears as the Son of David.

8. The Royal Psalms and the Messiah

The royal Psalms contain a very important collection of texts that became central to understanding Christ:

Psalm 2, Psalm 16, Psalm 22, Psalm 45, Psalm 72, Psalm 89, Psalm 110, Psalm 118.

However, an important methodological point must be noted: not all these Psalms are direct future prophecy in the same sense. Some are associated with a historical king, while others use royal language that transcends the historical person in order to open a future horizon.

This is one of the keys to reading prophecy: prophecy sometimes does not come in the form of a separate prediction, but in the form of a historical pattern that reaches its fulfillment in Christ.

9. Psalm 2: “You Are My Son”

The Lord says to the king:

“You are my Son; today I have begotten you.”
(Psalm 2:7).

Then it says:

“Ask of me, and I will give you the nations as your inheritance, and the ends of the earth as your possession.”
(Psalm 2:8).

This image brings together the son, the king, the nations, and universal authority.

Here a very important point appears in Jewish messianic thought: the Messiah is not merely a local Jewish king; the great royal texts speak of an authority extending to the nations.

The New Testament uses Psalm 2 in speaking of Christ, his resurrection, and his kingship, while the text itself remains part of the tradition of the royal Psalms.

10. Psalm 72: The King Who Reigns in Justice

Psalm 72 portrays an ideal king:

“May he judge your people with righteousness, and your afflicted with justice.”
(Psalm 72:2).

Then:

“May he rule from sea to sea, and from the River to the ends of the earth.”
(Psalm 72:8).

The image reaches its climax:

“May all nations be blessed in him; may they call him blessed.”
(Psalm 72:17).

This is the king who brings together justice, peace, universal authority, and the blessing of the nations.

These characteristics appear again in Isaiah 11, Jeremiah 23, Zechariah 9, and Daniel 7.

11. Psalm 110: King and Priest Together

The Psalm begins:

“The Lord said to my Lord: ‘Sit at my right hand until I make your enemies a footstool for your feet.’”
(Psalm 110:1).

Then:

“The LORD has sworn and will not relent: You are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek”
(Psalm 110:4).

And here something profoundly theological occurs:

The kingly Messiah also becomes associated with priesthood.

This is unusual in the Levitical system; the king comes from the tribe of Judah and the priest from the tribe of Levi. But Psalm 110 opens a door for a king who has a priestly function in the pattern of a royal Zadok.

This text later becomes one of the most important Christian texts in the book of Hebrews.

12. Isaiah 7:14 and Immanuel

“Therefore the Lord himself will give you a sign. Behold, the virgin will conceive and bear a son, and she will call his name Immanuel.”
(Isaiah 7:14).

This text is one of the most hotly debated texts between Jews and Christians.

The Christian reading, as appears in Matthew 1:23, sees in the birth of Christ a deeper fulfillment of the verse.

But from a scholarly standpoint it is essential not to ignore the context of Isaiah 7; the prophecy came in a historical crisis related to Ahaz and Jerusalem and the events of the eighth century BCE. Therefore the text may have both an immediate historical reference and a broader horizon in Christian interpretation.

This is an excellent model for the idea of double fulfillment or prophetic fulfillment.

13. Isaiah 9: The Child-King

Isaiah says:

“For to us a child is born, to us a son is given; and the government shall be upon his shoulder, and his name shall be called Wonderful Counselor, Mighty God, Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace.”
(Isaiah 9:6).

Then:

“Of the increase of his government and of peace there will be no end, on the throne of David and over his kingdom”
(Isaiah 9:7).

Here several very powerful elements converge:

Child → son → the throne of David → an eternal kingdom → peace without end → titles with extraordinary theological content.

From a Christian perspective, it is difficult to separate this image from Christ.

In Jewish interpretation, however, there are different approaches to understanding the child, the king, and the historical context, and it is not correct to say that all Jews interpreted Isaiah 9 in a Christian messianic way.

14. Isaiah 11: The Messiah, Son of Jesse

Isaiah says:

“A shoot shall come out from the stump of Jesse, and a branch shall grow out of his roots.”
(Isaiah 11:1).

Then:

“And the Spirit of the Lord shall rest upon him, the Spirit of wisdom and understanding, the Spirit of counsel and might, the Spirit of knowledge and the fear of the Lord.”
(Isaiah 11:2).

This text is one of the clearest in depicting the image of a future messianic king descended from David.

The classical Jewish interpretation of Rashi reads the text explicitly in the horizon of “the days of the Messiah,” and links the coming forth of the branch from the stump of Jesse to the restoration of Israel, the gathering of the exiles, and the return of kingship.

Then comes the result:

“The wolf shall dwell with the lamb, and the leopard shall lie down with the young goat...”
(Isaiah 11:6).

This clarifies the essence of traditional Jewish expectation: the Messiah does not come only to forgive sins, but to bring a new age of peace, justice, and stability.

15. The Messiah and the Gathering of the Exiles

Isaiah 11 continues:

“And in that day the Lord will seek the remnant of his people... and gather the dispersed of Israel.”
(Isaiah 11:11–12).

Here we find one of the primary reasons many Jews rejected Jesus as the final Messiah: Christian prophecies speak of the gathering of Israel, world peace, and the establishment of the kingdom.

This objection must not be concealed in a serious Christian study.

Rather, we must ask:

Did Christ fulfill these things in his first coming, or do the prophecies extend to his second coming?

The New Testament answers that the kingdom of Christ began in his person, death, and resurrection, but it has not yet reached final fulfillment.

16. Isaiah 42: The Servant of the Lord

The series of “Songs of the Servant of the Lord” begins in Isaiah 42:

“Behold my servant, whom I uphold, my chosen one in whom my soul delights. I have put my Spirit upon him; he will bring forth justice to the nations.”
(Isaiah 42:1).

Note the sequence:

Servant → Spirit of God → justice → the nations.

Here the Messiah is not merely a military king; he is a servant bearing the message of God to the nations.

This is a very important element in understanding why the image of Christ in the Gospels differed from the expectations of some Jewish groups in the first century.

17. Isaiah 49: The Servant of the Lord and the Light of the Nations

The Lord says:

“It is too little for you to be my servant to raise up the tribes of Jacob and to restore the preserved of Israel; I will make you a light for the nations, that my salvation may reach to the ends of the earth.”
(Isaiah 49:6).

Here a profound paradox appears:

The Messiah is bound to Israel, but he is not sent for Israel alone.

This later explains why the nations become an essential part of the message of Christ and the apostles.

18. Isaiah 50: The Suffering Servant

In Isaiah 50, the image of a servant appears who obeys God despite beatings and humiliation:

“I gave my back to those who struck, and my cheeks to those who tore out the beard; I did not hide my face from shame and spitting.”
(Isaiah 50:6).

This image is very important because it begins to change the shape of hope:

The Messiah is not only the victorious king; there is also a suffering servant.

19. Isaiah 52:13–53: The Suffering Servant of the Lord

This is one of the most complex and profound points in Jewish-Christian dialogue.

The text says:

“Behold, my servant shall act wisely; he shall be exalted and lifted up, and shall be very high.”
(Isaiah 52:13).

Then:

“He was despised and rejected by men, a man of sorrows and acquainted with grief.”
(Isaiah 53:3).

Then comes the decisive statement:

“He was wounded for our transgressions; he was crushed for our iniquities; upon him was the chastisement that brought us peace, and by his wounds we are healed.”
(Isaiah 53:5).

Then:

“Yet it was the Lord’s will to crush him with suffering. When he makes his soul an offering for guilt, he shall see his offspring; he shall prolong his days, and the pleasure of the Lord shall prosper in his hand.”
(Isaiah 53:10).

And in the end:

“Therefore I will allot him a portion with the great, and he shall divide the spoil with the strong, because he poured out his soul to death and was counted among the transgressors; yet he bore the sin of many and made intercession for the transgressors.”
(Isaiah 53:12).

From the Christian perspective, this is one of the fullest prophecies in depicting Christ’s atoning death, resurrection, and glorification.

But here we must be very precise in presenting Jewish thought: Jewish interpretation is not uniform in viewing Isaiah 53 as a prophecy concerning a single suffering Messiah. There are Jewish traditions that see the “Servant” in Isaiah 40–55 as Israel, and other traditions that connect the text to a messianic figure or to a composite interpretation. Modern scholarship specifically warns against saying that the collective interpretation of Isaiah 53 was invented only by Rashi or that Jewish interpretation was always uniform.

But what is especially striking is that the Talmud itself, in Sanhedrin 98b, gives an interpretation that links some of the language of Isaiah 53 to the Messiah; indeed, when asked about the name of the Messiah, it records an opinion that says, “Ḥawara debi Rabi Shmu,” meaning “the afflicted/leprous one from the house of Rabbi,” based on Isaiah 53:4.

This is a very important point in theological discussion: the claim that "the Jews never saw any connection at all between Isaiah 53 and the Messiah" is historically inaccurate.

20. Micah 5: The Birthplace of the Ruler

Micah says:

"But you, Bethlehem Ephrathah, though you are small among the clans of Judah, out of you will come for me one who will be ruler over Israel, whose origins are from of old, from ancient times."
(Micah 5:2).

Matthew 2 applies this passage to Bethlehem and the birth of the Messiah.

Here we have a striking correspondence:

Place: Bethlehem
Tribe: Judah
Role: Ruler of Israel
Root: Davidic/ancient

This aligns with 2 Samuel 7 and Isaiah 11.

21. Jeremiah 23: The Righteous Branch

Jeremiah says:

"The days are coming, declares the Lord, when I will raise up for David a righteous Branch, and he will reign as king and will prosper and will execute justice and righteousness in the land."
(Jeremiah 23:5).

Then:

"In his days Judah will be saved and Israel will live securely."
(Jeremiah 23:6).

Here again the Davidic branch appears.

The Jewish interpretation in sources such as Steinsaltz reads the passage as referring to the righteous son of David and the king who is to come in the future to establish justice.

22. Ezekiel 34: The One Shepherd

After condemning the corrupt shepherds, the Lord says:

"I will set over them one shepherd, my servant David, and he shall shepherd them; he shall be their shepherd."
(Ezekiel 34:23).

Here the concept of David is repeated after David himself.

It is not necessarily meant that the historical David will return literally, but rather that the ideal Davidic king who is coming appears.

This passage becomes especially important when compared with John 10:

"I am the good shepherd."

In other words, Jesus assumes the image of the Davidic shepherd.

23. Ezekiel 37: David the King

The Lord says:

"My servant David will be king over them, and they will all have one shepherd."
(Ezekiel 37:24).

Then the passage speaks of an everlasting covenant of peace and the dwelling of God with his people.

So the Messiah in Ezekiel brings together:

David + king + shepherd + covenant + peace + the presence of God.

This pattern comes very close to the picture of Christ in the Gospels, but it also extends to the eschatological fulfillment that has not yet been fully realized.

24. Daniel 7: The Son of Man

Here we arrive at one of the most important prophecies in all study of Christ.

"I was watching in my vision by night, and there before me was one like a son of man, coming with the clouds of heaven. He approached the Ancient of Days and was led into his presence. He was given authority, glory and sovereign power; all nations and peoples of every language worshiped him. His dominion is an everlasting dominion that will not pass away, and his kingdom is one that will never be destroyed."
(Daniel 7:13–14).

This is not the image of an ordinary earthly king.

We have:

The clouds of heaven → the Son of Man → the Ancient of Days → universal dominion → worship/service of the nations → an eternal kingdom.

This is the passage that makes the title "Son of Man" in the Gospels so important.

Indeed, Jewish sources themselves show diversity in the interpretation of this image. And in later Jewish tradition, a comparison appears between Daniel 7:13 and Zechariah 9:9, where the Talmud in Sanhedrin 98a explains that the manner of the Messiah's coming differs according to Israel's deserving: "with the clouds of heaven" if they deserve it, or "humble and riding on a donkey" if they do not.

And this is one of the most beautiful points in the study of Christ:

Judaism itself connected Daniel 7 with the Messiah.

25. Daniel 9: The Messiah the Prince

Among the most controversial texts:

"Know and understand this: from the issuing of the decree to restore and rebuild Jerusalem until the Messiah, the Prince, there will be seven weeks and sixty-two weeks."
(Daniel 9:25).

Then it says:

"After the sixty-two weeks, the Messiah will be cut off and will have nothing."
(Daniel 9:26).

The traditional Christian reading sees in the text a prophecy of the Messiah's appearance and then his being cut off, and sees a strong connection between it and the death of Christ.

By contrast, traditional Jewish interpretation differs over identifying the "anointed one," calculating the weeks, and their historical reference; among the significant Jewish interpretations is Rashi's, which links Daniel 9 to the context of the destruction of the Second Temple and the history of the Temple.

Therefore, one should not build a sound messianic argument on arbitrary numerical calculations; rather, one must first understand the historical context of Daniel 9 and then examine how the early church read the passage.

26. Zechariah 9: The Humble King

He says:

"Rejoice greatly, Daughter Zion! See, your king comes to you, righteous and victorious, humble and riding on a donkey, on a colt, the foal of a donkey."
(Zechariah 9:9).

Matthew 21 and John 12 connect Jesus' entry into Jerusalem with this passage.

Here we see something of great importance in the study of Jewish messianic expectation:

Some were waiting for a warrior king.

But Zechariah presents:

a king → but humble → entering on a donkey → and then ruling the nations.

In other words, the image of the messianic king in the Old Testament is not merely that of a warrior.

27. Zechariah 12: The One They Look Upon

He says:

"They will look on me, the one they have pierced, and they will mourn for him as one mourns for an only child."
(Zechariah 12:10).

This passage is among the most difficult in Christian studies, especially because of the pronouns, the Hebrew text, and its ancient translations.

John 19:37 connects it with the piercing of Christ.

Here again we find a clear pattern:

Messiah → pain/piercing → looking at him → mourning → spiritual transformation.

28. Zechariah 13: The Struck Shepherd

He says:

"Awake, sword, against my shepherd, against the man who is close to me, declares the Lord of hosts. Strike the shepherd, and the sheep will be scattered."
(Zechariah 13:7).

Jesus himself applies the passage to the scattering of the disciples when he is arrested (Matthew 26:31; Mark 14:27).

Here an astonishing convergence occurs between:

the shepherd + the striking + the scattering of the sheep + the Messiah.

This intersects directly with Isaiah 53 and Ezekiel 34.

29. Malachi 3: The Messenger of the Covenant

He says:

"Behold, I am sending my messenger, and he will prepare the way before me. And the Lord whom you are seeking will come suddenly to his temple, and the messenger of the covenant whom you delight in."
(Malachi 3:1).

In Christian interpretation, the "messenger" is connected with John the Baptist, who prepares the way, and then the Lord comes to his temple.

And the important theological point is that the text does not only say that the Messiah comes to Israel; rather, he comes to the temple, that is, to the center of worship.

30. Malachi 4 and Elijah

Malachi concludes:

"Behold, I am sending you Elijah the prophet before the coming of the great and awesome day of the Lord."
(Malachi 4:5).

Therefore, the expectation of Elijah was closely connected with Jewish messianic hope.

And in the Gospels, Jesus explains that John the Baptist came "in the spirit and power of Elijah" (Luke 1:17), while the Jews themselves were waiting for Elijah before the coming of the Messiah.

31. Why Did Some Jews Reject Jesus as the Messiah?

This is a point that must be addressed with complete honesty.

The problem was not that the Jews knew nothing about the Messiah; on the contrary, there was a genuine and multifaceted messianic hope.

But many expectations included:

a Davidic king — liberation of Israel — defeat of enemies — restoration of the kingdom — gathering of the dispersed — universal peace — rebuilding of the temple — establishment of an age of justice.

And Jewish and historical studies show that messianic expectation in the Second Temple period was highly diverse, with a strong presence of the royal military image.

And when Jesus appeared:

He healed, taught, forgave sins, entered Jerusalem on a donkey, but he did not drive out the Romans, and he was killed on the cross.

Therefore, it seemed to many that the cross contradicted the image of the conquering Messiah.

32. The Christian Surprise: The Messiah Comes Twice

Here the New Testament presents a theologically crucial key.

The Old Testament combines in some places:

the suffering Messiah

and

the conquering Messiah-King.

But it does not always explain clearly and temporally how they come together.

The New Testament proclaims:

The First Coming = the Incarnation, the cross, the Resurrection, redemption.

The Second Coming = the public Kingdom, judgment, subjugation of enemies, ultimate renewal.

And this allows understanding why Isaiah 53 can be correct, while at the same time Isaiah 11, Daniel 7, and Zechariah 14 have not yet reached their final fulfillment.

33. The Suffering Christ and the Glorified Christ

This is the great theological key.

In Isaiah 53:

The Messiah bears sins and dies.

In Daniel 7:

The Son of Man comes in the clouds and reigns.

In Zechariah 9:

The King enters humbly.

In Zechariah 14:

The Lord comes and reigns over all the earth.

In Psalm 22:

The sufferer concludes with praise before the nations.

In Psalm 110:

Christ sits at the right hand of God and subjugates his enemies.

Therefore, the image is not a weak messiah instead of a kingly messiah, but rather:

The suffering Christ is the same as the conquering Christ.

34. Why Was the Cross a "Stumbling Block"?

Paul says:

"For we proclaim Christ crucified, a stumbling block to the Jews and foolishness to the Gentiles."
(1 Corinthians 1:23).

This verse reveals the messianic shock.

The expected Messiah for many was supposed to be the conquering King.

But Christianity says:

The King triumphed first by the cross.

And here Christianity does not annul the royal prophecies; rather, it connects them with the prophecies of the suffering servant.

35. Jewish Thought about the Messiah in the Time of Jesus

It is very important not to speak of "the Jews" as if they were a single group with a single conception.

Studies on Second Temple Judaism point to the existence of several models:

the Davidic Royal Messiah

a king from the line of David, who restores Israel and defeats its enemies.

the Priestly Messiah

a priestly figure with a role in purifying worship or leading the people.

the Prophetic Messiah

a prophetic figure connected with Moses or Elijah.

the Heavenly Messiah

a figure connected with the visions of Daniel and apocalyptic literature, especially the image of the Son of Man.

And specialized studies in Second Temple Judaism confirm that these models were not always completely separate, but could overlap, and that some texts even speak of both a royal messiah and a priestly messiah together.

36. Qumran and the Idea of the Messiah

The Dead Sea Scrolls are very important because they reveal that Jews in the first century were not necessarily waiting for a single messianic figure in the way we imagine today.

Some Qumran texts speak of a Messiah of Israel and a Messiah of Aaron, that is, a royal aspect and a priestly aspect.

And this is very important in the study of the New Testament; because Christianity will proclaim in Jesus something different:

He is the Davidic King, and he is the High Priest, and he is the Prophet, and he is the heavenly Son of Man.

And here appears the uniqueness of Christian Christology in comparison with the multiple Jewish conceptions in the Second Temple era.

37. Were the Jews Waiting for a Divine Messiah?

The question is complex.

Judaism did not generally assert that the Messiah is "God incarnate" as in Christian doctrine.

But ancient Jewish literature contains images of heavenly figures, transcendent ones, or those connected with the divine throne, most notably the image of the Son of Man in Daniel 7, and some later texts that expand this image.

Therefore, both extreme statements should be avoided:

"The Jews were all waiting for an incarnate God" — incorrect.

and "There is no idea of a heavenly messiah in ancient Judaism" — also incorrect.

Rather, more precisely: there were different Jewish currents with graduated conceptions of the Messiah, and some of them approached a heavenly transcendent figure.

38. Why is "Son of God" a Messianic title?

In Psalm 2, the Davidic king is called the Son of God.

Therefore, when Peter says:

"You are the Christ, the Son of the living God."
(Matthew 16:16)

he is using well-known royal Messianic language.

But the New Testament goes beyond mere royal meaning; the relationship between the Father and the Son in John, Hebrews, Colossians, and Philippians exceeds a mere royal title.

And here the transition occurs from:

Son of God = the Davidic king

To:

the Son = a unique and eternal relationship with the Father.

39. Christ in the Gospel of John: The Word

John does not begin the genealogy of Christ with Abraham or David; rather, he begins:

"In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God."
(John 1:1).

Here Christology goes beyond merely:

Son of David.

and reaches:

the eternal Word who became flesh.

This explains why Christianity sees Jesus not merely as the political Messiah some expected, but as the Messiah who reveals the identity of God and accomplishes salvation.

40. Christ and the Lamb

One of the most beautiful convergences of prophecy is that John the Baptist says:

"Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!"
(John 1:29).

Here intersect:

Passover — Isaiah 53 — the sacrifice — the Lamb of God — the cross.

And the last book, Revelation, takes this title and makes it central:

the Lamb.

For the Christ who died as a Lamb is the same one who opens the scroll and rules history.

41. Christ and the Shepherd

From Ezekiel 34:

David/the one shepherd.

And from Ezekiel 37:

David the king.

And from Zechariah 13:

the struck shepherd.

Then Jesus says:

"I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep."
(John 10:11).

Here Jesus unites the Davidic shepherd with the suffering servant.

And this is one of the strongest Christological combinations in the New Testament.

42. Christ and the Temple

In traditional Jewish expectation, the era of the Messiah is strongly associated with the temple and the restoration of worship; later Jewish sources, including the view of Moses Maimonides, connect the Messianic age with the restoration of Davidic kingship, the gathering of the diaspora, and the rebuilding of the temple.

But Jesus says:

"Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up."
(John 2:19).

Then John explains:

"But he was speaking about the temple of his body."
(John 2:21).

And here a profound Messianic shift occurs:

from the temple as a building → to Christ as the place of God's presence.

43. Christ and the Kingdom

The Messiah in the prophets is associated with the kingdom.

But Jesus begins his ministry by saying:

"The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand. Repent and believe in the gospel."
(Mark 1:15).

In other words, the Messiah does not merely wait for the kingdom; he is the one who announces its entry into history.

But the kingdom in the New Testament has two dimensions:

present: it began with the work of Christ.

future: it will appear in full glory at his coming.

This is the key to understanding the difference between the initial fulfillment and eschatological completion.

44. Judaism and the Second Coming

One interesting point is that traditional Judaism awaits the coming of the Messiah in the future, whereas Christianity says that the Messiah has already come and that he will have a glorious return.

Therefore, there is a radical difference on one point:

Judaism: the Messiah has not yet come.

Christianity: the Messiah came, died, rose, and will come again.

For this reason, much of the disagreement is not only about the existence of prophecies, but about the order in which their fulfillment is arranged.

45. The Messiah in Jewish thought after the destruction of the temple

After the destruction of the temple in 70 CE, and then the Bar Kokhba revolt in 132–135 CE, the Messianic hope took different forms and developed within Rabbinic Judaism.

Academic sources indicate that subsequent Jewish thought focused heavily on the Davidic redeemer, the gathering of Israel, world peace, the removal of evil, and the return of Israel’s glory.

This explains why the criterion of the Messiah in Jewish thought remained closely tied to what the Messiah accomplishes in the historical world.

46. Moses Maimonides and the Messiah

In medieval Judaism, belief in the Messiah became an important part of the formulation of Jewish doctrine in Moses Maimonides — Rambam/Maimonides.

Contemporary Jewish sources present his conception of the Messiah as a king from the line of David who restores Davidic kingship, gathers the Jews to the land of Israel, rebuilds the temple, and leads an age of stability and prosperity.

This is important because the definition of “the Messiah” in later traditional Judaism became more specific than what we find in the diverse ancient Jewish texts.

47. Where does the fundamental difference between Christianity and Judaism lie?

It can be summarized in four questions:

1. Has the Messiah come?

Christianity: Yes, Jesus is the Messiah.
Traditional Judaism: No, the Messiah has not yet come.

2. Does the Messiah die?

Christianity: Yes, his death is part of his salvific work.
Judaism: There is no ancient Jewish consensus that the death of the Messiah is a basic element of his image.

3. Is the Messiah divine?

Christianity: Christ is the eternal Son incarnate.
Judaism: In the traditional conception, the Messiah is a royal human descended from David, although some ancient Jewish literature contains more complex heavenly conceptions.

4. Why was peace and the gathering of Israel not realized?

The Jew asks: If Jesus is the Messiah, where is the age of peace?

The Christian answers: The kingdom began in Christ, but it is not yet complete; its fulfillment will come in the second coming.

49. Prophecies that require future fulfillment

Here we must be very precise.

There are prophecies fulfilled clearly in the first coming:

The birth, Bethlehem, the seed of David, the entry into Jerusalem, betrayal/sufferings, the cross, the resurrection.

But there are prophecies that did not reach their final fulfillment:

Global peace, the full subjection of the nations, the final gathering of Israel, the removal of death, the general judgment, cosmic renewal, the final kingdom.

Therefore, it is not correct to say that everything prophesied in the Old Testament was fulfilled completely in the first century.

And the biblical solution offered by Christianity is:

Christ has already come, but he will come again.

And this is the basis of the eschatological hope in the New Testament.

50. Christ in prophecy: one person or a collection of images?

If we gather all the prophecies, we find that the required person must be:

Of the seed of the woman.
Of the seed of Abraham.
Of the tribe of Judah.
Of the seed of David.
Born in Bethlehem.
Anointed by the Spirit.
A prophet, king, and shepherd.
A light to the nations.
Humble.
Suffering.
Bearing sin.
Pierced.
Exalted and glorified.
One with universal authority.
An eternal king.
Associated with the temple and the presence of God.

And here the power of the Christian messianic pattern appears: Jesus does not fulfill only one prophecy, but enters into a broad network of patterns, covenants, and prophecies.

51. Prophecy is not a “game of numbers”

A common mistake in Christological studies is the use of every number in the Old Testament to prove a specific date for the birth of Christ, his crucifixion, or his second coming.

The more accurate approach is:

Text first → context second → Jewish background third → the relation to the New Testament fourth → theological application last.

This is especially important in Daniel 9.

52. Prophecy is not always a direct future statement

There are three levels that should be distinguished:

Direct prophecy

As with Micah 5:2 in its messianic reading.

The prophetic pattern

As with David, the king who becomes a pattern for the coming messianic king.

Fulfillment/realization

Where the text has a near historical meaning, but finds a fuller realization in the work of Christ.

This explains many prophecies that seem in the Old Testament to be related to a king or a historical event, but which the New Testament then uses in speaking of Christ.

53. The greatest messianic paradox

The entire study can be summarized in one paradox:

The Jew awaits the king.

The New Testament says: The King has come.

But when He came:

He did not begin with the sword, but with the cross.

He did not begin by killing His enemies, but by forgiving sins.

He did not first sit on an earthly throne, but was hung on a piece of wood.

He did not first defeat Rome, but defeated sin and death.

Then He rose.

Then He ascended.

Then He will come to reign openly.

Thus the cross does not abolish the kingdom; rather, the kingdom passes through the cross to the resurrection and then to glory.

54. Christ Is the Key to Reading the Prophecies

Christ said to the two disciples of Emmaus:

"O foolish ones, and slow of heart to believe in all that the prophets have spoken! Ought not Christ to have suffered these things, and to enter into His glory?"
(Luke 24:25–26).

Then it says:

"Then beginning at Moses and all the Prophets, He expounded to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning Himself"
(Luke 24:27).

This verse, in my opinion, is the greatest methodological key to studying Christ in the prophecies.

Christ Himself did not say that only one or two prophecies speak of Him; rather, He gave a comprehensive reading of Scripture: Moses, the Prophets, and the Psalms, in light of His person and work.

55. The great theological conclusion

When we place all the threads side by side, one coherent picture emerges:

In Genesis: the seed of the woman defeats the serpent.
In Abraham: the seed carries a blessing for the nations.
In Judah: the ruler comes, and the peoples submit to him.
In David: a son comes whose throne is established forever.
In the Psalms: the royal Son appears, the King who holds worldwide authority, the eternal Priest, and the sufferer who enters into glory.
In Isaiah: Immanuel appears, the child King, the Branch of Jesse, the Servant of the Lord, the light of the nations, and the suffering Servant who bears sins.
In Micah: the ruler from Bethlehem appears.
In Jeremiah: the righteous Branch appears.
In Ezekiel: the one shepherd and David the king appear.
In Daniel: the heavenly Son of Man, who holds the eternal kingdom, appears.
In Zechariah: the humble King appears, the pierced one, and the stricken shepherd.
In Malachi: the Lord comes to His temple after the way has been prepared before Him.

Then the New Testament comes and places all these threads in one person:

Jesus of Nazareth.

He is the seed of Abraham, the son of David, the Son of Man, the King, the Shepherd, the suffering Servant, the Lamb of God, the Priest, the Word, the Son of God, the light of the nations, and the Christ.

But Christianity does not say that all the prophecies ended at the cross; rather, it says that the cross and the resurrection are the center of the first fulfillment, while the kingdom awaits its completion at the second coming.

Here appears the profound difference between the Christ proclaimed by the prophecies and the narrow political image that some Jews were expecting: Christ came to reign, but He first reigned through the cross; He came to defeat the enemies of God, but He began by defeating sin and death; He came to gather the nations, and He opened the covenant to Israel and the nations; He came to establish the kingdom, and the kingdom began in Him, but it will be completed at His coming.

Fifth: Second Temple Judaism

One of the most important findings to be retained in the study encyclopedia is that Judaism at the time of Christ did not hold a single conception of the Messiah; the sources point to royal, priestly, prophetic, and heavenly models, and the idea of the royal military Messiah was one of the most prevalent models. The Qumran texts and apocalyptic literature also reveal even greater diversity in conceptions.

Sixth: Contemporary Jewish Sources

Interpretive and introductory material from My Jewish Learning was used to present the traditional Jewish conception of the messianic era, including the gathering of the Jews, the rebuilding of the Temple, world peace, and the connection of the Messiah to the Davidic line, without considering these sources alone as representative of all of Judaism.

Seventh: Academic Studies on Jesus and the Messiah

Among the useful studies in the area of Jewish-Christian dialogue is David Mishkin's study on the development of Jewish views on the messiahship of Jesus, which affirms the importance of returning to the Second Temple Jewish background to understand issues such as the death of the Messiah and the possibility of a heavenly Messiah.

Final Summary

Christ in the prophecies is not an idea that suddenly appeared in the New Testament, nor can it be reduced to a single prophecy such as Isaiah 53 or Micah 5:2. He is the outcome of a long biblical development that begins with the seed of the woman, passes through Abraham, Judah, and David, expands in the Psalms, takes on royal and spiritual dimensions in Isaiah, Jeremiah, and Ezekiel, then reaches an apocalyptic climax in Daniel, and intersects with the King, the Shepherd, the Servant, the Priest, and the Son of Man.

More importantly, Jewish thought itself carried a genuine expectation of the Messiah, but it was not a single expectation. Some Jews awaited a liberating Davidic king, some traditions retained a priestly image, some literature presented a heavenly figure, and some texts linked the Messiah to the Temple, the gathering of Israel, and world peace. This diversity makes the appearance of Jesus all the more fascinating to study, because the New Testament does not reject the royal prophecies but rearranges them around the crucified and risen Christ.

From here comes the greatest statement summarizing the whole study:

The Messiah whom the prophecies awaited is the King who comes from the seed of David, but in God's plan He first appears as a suffering Servant and a Lamb who bears sin; then He rises from the dead, sits at the right hand of the Father, returns as King and Judge, and establishes His complete kingdom.
