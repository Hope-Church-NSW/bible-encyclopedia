📖 Introductions to the Books of the Bible
The Eleventh and Twelfth Books: First and Second Kings

Methodological note: First and Second Kings were originally a single book in the Hebrew tradition, then divided into two books. They do not present merely a history of the kings of Israel and Judah, but rather present a theological interpretation of history: why did some kings endure, why did others fall, and why did the kingdom end in exile despite the existence of the temple, the king, and worship?

7
First: The First Book of Kings
1. The Name of the Book and Its Meaning

The Arabic name is Kings, and the Hebrew name in the Jewish tradition is מְלָכִים — Melakhim — Melakhim, meaning "Kings."

The name is appropriate because the book deals with a long succession of kings, but it is not concerned with kings merely for the sake of knowing their names and political deeds.

Rather, the fundamental question that governs the book is:

Was the king faithful to the Lord and to the covenant or not?

For this reason we find that the writer does not measure the king's success by the number of battles he won, nor by the size of his palace, nor by the wealth of his kingdom, but by his relationship with the Lord and his worship of and obedience to him.

2. The Author

The book does not explicitly mention its author's name.

First and Second Kings belong to what is called in biblical studies Deuteronomistic history, that is, history that reads the events of Israel in light of the covenant principles that appear especially in the book of Deuteronomy.

It appears that the final writer drew upon royal records and prior historical sources, and he himself points to sources such as "The Book of the Acts of Solomon" and "The Book of the Annals of the Kings of Israel" and "The Book of the Annals of the Kings of Judah."

This is very important, because the Bible is not ashamed to point to the existence of other historical sources, but it presents the selected material within a theological perspective.

3. The Time of Events of the Two Books

First Kings begins at the end of David's life and the beginning of Solomon's reign.

Then it continues to the division of the kingdom after Solomon, and First Kings ends approximately at the time of Elijah and Ahab.

As for Second Kings, it continues the history from Ahaziah and Jehoram and then the fall of Samaria, and then the history of the kings of Judah until the fall of Jerusalem and the Babylonian exile.

Thus the two books cover several centuries of Israel's history.

4. For Whom Was the Book Written?

The book was written for an audience that knew the history of Israel but needed a theological interpretation of a painful question:

How did the people of the covenant come to exile?

For the exile was not merely a military defeat.

It was a theological crisis:

How does God allow Jerusalem to be destroyed?

How is the temple burned?

How are the people of the Lord taken into exile?

The answer that Kings provides is that the problem was not God's weakness, but the repeated disobedience of the people and their kings to the covenant.

5. Reasons for Writing Kings

The objectives of the book can be summarized in five points:

Recording the history of the kings.

Explaining the division of the kingdom.

Showing the danger of idol worship.

Highlighting the role of prophets in confronting the kings.

Explaining the exile as a historical and spiritual consequence of disobedience.

Thus Kings becomes a book about kingship under God's authority.

6. Division of the First Book of Kings
1 Kings 1–2

The end of David and the rise of Solomon.

1 Kings 3–4

Solomon's wisdom and the expansion of the kingdom.

1 Kings 5–8

The building and dedication of the temple.

1 Kings 9–11

The glory of Solomon, then his deviation and his fall.

1 Kings 12–16

The division of the kingdom and the emergence of the kingdoms of Israel and Judah.

1 Kings 17–22

The ministry of Elijah and his struggle with Ahab and Jezebel.

7. Solomon

The name Solomon — שְׁלֹמֹה — Shelomoh is connected to the root שׁלם, which carries the meanings of peace, perfection, and completeness.

Solomon begins in a very shining manner.

God asks him what he wants.

He asks neither for gold, nor for a long life, nor for the destruction of his enemies.

Rather, he asks for:

"An understanding heart."

Here the book presents a model of the king who understands that true governance requires wisdom that comes from God.

8. The Wisdom of Solomon

Solomon is renowned for his wisdom.

The story of the two women disputing over the child appears as an early test of this wisdom.

But the book does not allow the reader to believe that wisdom alone guarantees success.

A person may possess great wisdom yet fall when he allows his heart to deviate.

This is exactly what will happen to Solomon.

9. The Building of the Temple

The greatest event in the first part of the book is the building of the Lord's Temple in Jerusalem.

Here what David could not accomplish is fulfilled.

Solomon builds a house for the Lord.

The Temple becomes the center of worship in Israel.

But Solomon himself realizes in the dedication prayer that:

"Behold, the heavens and the heaven of heavens cannot contain You; how much less this house that I have built!"

This is a great theological idea:

The Temple does not confine God.

Rather, it is a place God chose to declare his presence among his people.

10. Solomon's prayer

The prayer of 1 Kings 8 is one of the most important theological texts in the Old Testament.

Solomon speaks about:

Prayer.

Forgiveness.

Repentance.

War.

The famine.

Illness.

The foreigner.

The exile.

This reveals that the Temple, in biblical thought, is not merely a building for sacrifices, but is connected to the covenant relationship between God and his people.

11. The Glory of Solomon

The kingdom reaches, in the days of Solomon, an enormous level of prosperity.

The Queen of Sheba comes to see his wisdom.

And she sees his glory.

But the book presents, from the beginning, the seeds of the problem:

Marriage to many women from the nations.

Gold and wealth.

Horses.

Political relations.

And matters that at first appear to be signs of success gradually turn into doors to downfall.

12. The Fall of Solomon

The book says that Solomon's wives:

"Turned away his heart after other gods."

So he worships Ashtoreth, Milcom, and other gods.

Here the tragedy comes.

The man who built the Temple for the Lord allows his heart to be divided.

And the lesson is clear:

A person can build a great house for God while losing his own heart.

13. The Division of the Kingdom

After the death of Solomon, his son Rehoboam comes.

The people demand the lightening of the yoke.

But Rehoboam rejects the wise counsel and chooses the harsh advice.

So the people are divided.

The Kingdom of Judah

In the south, with its capital Jerusalem.

The Kingdom of Israel

In the north, with its capital later Samaria.

And from here begins a tragic phase in biblical history.

14. Jeroboam

Jeroboam — יָרָבְעָם — Yarov'am.

He sets up two golden calves in Bethel and Dan.

The reason is political and religious:

He does not want his people to go to Jerusalem.

But the political decision turns into a spiritual catastrophe.

For this reason, the expression becomes:

"The sins of Jeroboam son of Nebat"

A recurring formula in the Book of Kings.

15. Elijah

Elijah appears — אֵלִיָּהוּ — Eliyahu, and the meaning of his name:

"The Lord is God."

This name summarizes his message.

Elijah appears at a time when the worship of Baal had come to threaten Israel's spiritual identity.

The fundamental battle is not only between two prophets.

Rather, it is between:

Who is the true God?

16. Elijah and Ahab

King Ahab and his wife Jezebel represent a very dangerous phase.

The worship of Baal enters the kingdom with force.

And Elijah stands almost alone in confronting the royal system.

Here the function of the prophet appears:

The prophet is not an employee of the king.

Rather, he is the voice of God before the king.

17. Mount Carmel

On Mount Carmel, Elijah meets with the prophets of Baal.

And he sets forth the challenge:

The God who answers by fire is God.

The prophets of Baal pray, and nothing happens.

Then Elijah prays.

So the fire descends.

Here the people cry out:

“The LORD, he is God! The LORD, he is God!”

This sentence summarizes Elijah’s message.

18. The Fire and the Rain

After the confrontation on Carmel, Elijah prays for rain.

Then the cloud comes after years of drought.

Here two things come together:

God’s judgment on evil.

Then:

God’s mercy after repentance and return.

19. Elijah in the Wilderness

After the great victory, Elijah flees because of Jezebel’s threat.

This is one of the most human scenes in the book.

The prophet who confronted hundreds of prophets feels broken and alone.

But God does not reject him.

Rather, he cares for him, feeds him, and leads him.

Then he appears to him in a low, gentle voice.

Here we learn that God works not only in fire and earthquakes, but also in silence and stillness.

🔵 The Most Important Message in First Kings

The true king is not the one who possesses the greatest kingdom, but the one who submits to the King of kings; and the temple, worship, and prosperity cannot compensate for a heart far from God.

📖 Second: The Book of Second Kings
1. The Theme of the Book

Second Kings continues the story of the kingdom’s collapse.

And the events become increasingly dark.

Israel refuses to repent.

Then Samaria falls before Assyria.

Judah continues for a longer period, then Jerusalem falls before Babylon.

But amid this collapse, God continues to send prophets who warn and proclaim his mercy.

2. Elijah and Elisha

The role passes from Elijah to Elisha — אֱלִישָׁע — Elisha.

The meaning of the name is:

“God is salvation” or “God is the salvation.”

This name corresponds to his ministry, since Elisha is associated with a large number of miracles.

3. The Ascent of Elijah

At the beginning of Second Kings, Elijah ascends to heaven in the whirlwind.

And Elisha sees this.

The prophetic responsibility is transferred.

This declares that God’s work does not stop with the death or departure of a servant.

The servant goes, but the word of God continues.

4. The Miracles of Elisha

Second Kings presents a broad series of miracles:

The purification of the waters.

The multiplication of the oil.

The raising of the Shunammite woman’s son.

The cleansing of leprosy.

The floating of the iron.

The rescue of armies.

These miracles affirm that God works in the lives of ordinary people, not only in the palaces of kings.

5. Naaman

One of the most famous stories in the book is the story of Naaman the Syrian.

He was a great commander, but he was afflicted with leprosy.

And he goes to Elisha.

The prophet asks him to wash in the Jordan River seven times.

So Naaman becomes angry at first.

Then he humbles himself.

And he obeys.

So he is healed.

Here the story presents a great lesson:

A person may ask God for a method suited to his greatness, but God sometimes asks him for simple obedience that reveals the humility of the heart.

6. The Fall of Samaria

The northern kingdom of Israel continues in the worship of idols.

And Assyria comes.

In approximately the year 722 B.C., Samaria falls.

And the existence of the northern kingdom of Israel as an independent entity comes to an end.

Here the warning of the prophets is fulfilled.

The fall was not an isolated political incident.

Rather, it was the result of a long accumulation of disobedience.

7. Judah and Hezekiah

In Judah, King Hezekiah appears.

He is one of the best kings of Judah.

He destroys the high places.

He smashes the idols.

He redirects the people to the Lord.

And when Assyria comes, he faces a huge crisis.

8. Sennacherib and Jerusalem

Sennacherib, king of Assyria, comes and besieges the cities of Judah.

Then he threatens Jerusalem.

But Hezekiah enters the house of the Lord and lays the matter before God.

And prays.

Then the Lord intervenes.

And the army of Assyria is struck.

Here it appears again that the salvation of Israel does not depend only on military strength.

9. Manasseh

After Hezekiah comes Manasseh.

He is one of the worst kings in the history of Judah.

He restores the idols.

He builds strange altars.

He practices many evils.

And the narrative affirms that Manasseh's corruption had a severe impact on Judah's course toward judgment.

10. Josiah

Then Josiah appears.

He is one of the greatest kings in religious reform.

The Book of the Law is discovered in the temple.

And it is read.

He tears his clothes.

Then he begins a broad reform.

Here an important principle appears:

True reform begins when a person hears the word of God and takes it seriously.

11. Passover in the days of Josiah

Josiah renews the celebration of Passover in a powerful way.

And this connects the reform to the history of the Exodus.

For the people who forget their ancient salvation lose their identity.

Therefore Josiah restores the people to:

The Law.

Passover.

Worship.

The covenant.

12. The Fall of Jerusalem

After Josiah, the kingdom deteriorates rapidly.

The Babylonians come.

They besiege Jerusalem.

Then the city falls.

And the temple is burned.

And the walls are torn down.

And the people are taken into exile.

Here we come to one of the saddest moments in the Old Testament:

The city chosen by God is exposed to ruin because of the rebellion of His people.

13. Why did God allow the temple to fall?

This is a very important question.

Because Israel might have thought that the presence of the temple guaranteed protection no matter what happened.

But the kings reject this way of thinking.

The temple is not a talisman.

And the Ark is not a talisman.

And the holy city does not protect a people who insist on rebellion.

God is faithful to His covenant, and therefore He is also faithful in His discipline.

14. Babylonian Exile

Judah falls.

And many of the people are taken to Babylon.

Here a new era begins in biblical history.

But the narrative does not end with the words:

"Everything is over."

But it ends with the scene of King Jehoiachin, the captive, being lifted from the prison table and eating before the king of Babylon.

And this small detail carries great hope.

15. Why is the ending important?

If the author had wanted to make the narrative only a story of judgment, it could have ended with the death of Jehoiachin or the destruction of Jerusalem.

But he leaves a window open.

The Davidic king has not ended completely.

And this becomes very important in light of the previous promises to David.

Even in the midst of exile:

The promise did not die.

16. Key Figures
David

The royal benchmark by which subsequent kings are measured.

Solomon

The wise king who built the temple and then whose heart turned away.

Rehoboam

The king who contributed to the division of the kingdom.

Jeroboam

The founder of the worship of the calves in the northern kingdom.

Ahab

The king of Israel who faced Elijah in the struggle against Baal worship.

Jezebel

The influential figure in spreading Baal worship and resisting the prophets.

Elijah

The prophet of monotheism and confrontation with Baal.

Elisha

The prophet who continued Elijah’s ministry and demonstrated God’s power through miracles.

Hezekiah

The king of Judah who trusted in the LORD before Assyria.

Manasseh

A king associated with the most severe forms of religious apostasy.

Josiah

The king of reform and return to the Law.

Sennacherib

The king of Assyria who threatened Jerusalem.

Nebuchadnezzar

The king of Babylon associated with the fall of Jerusalem and the Exile.

17. The Main Events in the Two Books

Solomon’s accession to the throne.

Building the temple.

The dedication of the Temple.

Solomon’s glory.

Solomon’s spiritual downfall.

The division of the kingdom.

Worship of the calves.

Elijah’s ministry.

Mount Carmel.

Elisha’s ministry.

Healing Naaman.

The fall of Samaria.

The reform of Hezekiah.

Sennacherib’s siege.

Josiah's reform.

The fall of Jerusalem.

The burning of the temple.

The Babylonian exile.

18. The Most Important Theological Message

The book of Kings does not say:

“Israel had evil kings.”

Rather, it says something deeper:

When the king and the people cease to place God at the center, political success turns into spiritual collapse.

That is why the phrase is repeated:

“And he did what was evil in the sight of the LORD.”

For the eye that judges is not the king’s eye.

Nor the people’s eye.

But rather:

The eye of the LORD.

19. Kings and Prophets

One of the most important things to note is that the prophets continually appear alongside the kings.

Elijah before Ahab.

Elisha before the kings of Israel.

Isaiah before Hezekiah.

This reveals that the Bible does not allow political power to be absolute.

The king is under the word of God.

The state is under the authority of God.

Worship is under the authority of God.

20. Kings and the Temple

There is a central paradox:

Solomon builds the Temple.

Then:

The people go astray.

Then:

The Temple itself is burned.

This teaches us that a sacred building cannot replace holiness.

You may have:

An altar.

A temple.

Priests.

Sacrifices.

But if the heart is far from God, religious appearances become powerless to save a person from the consequences of disobedience.

21. Kings and the Davidic Covenant

Despite the fall of the kings, a consistent theological thread remains:

God’s promise to David.

Every king descended from David is measured within the framework of this promise.

This opens the way to awaiting:

The perfect king.

The king who will not ultimately act like Solomon.

And will not fail like Rehoboam.

And will not fall like many kings.

Rather, he will be:

A just, faithful, eternal king.

Here the history of the kings gradually moves toward Christ the King.

22. Christ in Light of the Books of Kings

We should not turn every event in the book into a direct symbol of Christ.

But there is a clear theological trajectory:

David → covenant → kings → failure of the kings → expectation of the perfect king.

Solomon was wise, but he fell.

Hezekiah was faithful, but he died.

Josiah was righteous, but he died.

Every human king demonstrated that humanity needs a greater king.

And in the New Testament, Jesus comes as:

Son of David.

He is the king whose reign does not end in death.

🔑 Summary of First and Second Kings

Name: מְלָכִים — Melakhim — Kings.

Theme: The history of the kings of Israel and Judah in light of God’s covenant.

Period: From the end of David and Solomon to the Babylonian Exile.

Geographical focus: Jerusalem, Samaria, Judah, Israel, Assyria, Babylon.

Most important religious event: The construction and dedication of the Temple.

Most important political development: The division of the kingdom.

Most important spiritual conflict: The LORD against the worship of Baal and idols.

Most prominent prophets: Elijah and Elisha.

Most prominent reforms: Hezekiah and Josiah.

The greatest catastrophe: the fall of Jerusalem, the burning of the Temple, and the captivity.

The most important question: Why did the covenant people fall?

The answer: Because of continual disobedience, rejection of the word of God, and idolatry.

The hope: God's promise to David was not canceled.

The messianic focus: The failure of human kings leads to waiting for the perfect King from the line of David.
