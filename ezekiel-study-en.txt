Introduction to the Book of Ezekiel

The central idea of the book: The Book of Ezekiel is the book of the glory of God, judgment, exile, personal responsibility, renewal, hope, and the restoration of God's presence among his people. Ezekiel lived during one of the most turbulent periods in the history of Israel and Judah: the people were in Babylonian exile while Jerusalem was still standing at the beginning of his ministry; then the prophet witnessed in his visions the fall of the city and the destruction of the temple. The book therefore combines majestic heavenly scenes with painful earthly events, declarations of judgment with promises of restoration, the heart of stone with the new heart, and the temple that is abandoned because of sin with the temple to which the glory of the Lord returns at the end of the book. The book's overarching movement may be summarized in this statement: the glory of God appears, sin is judged, Jerusalem falls, the people are scattered, and then God gathers his people, gives them a new heart, and restores his glory among them.

1. Name of the book and its meaning

The name of the book is Ezekiel, and the Hebrew name is יְחֶזְקֵאל — Yeḥezqēʾl. It generally consists of an element related to the Hebrew verb חזק — ḥazaq, which conveys the meaning of strength or strengthening, and the element El — אֵל, which refers to God. The meaning of the name is therefore commonly understood as "God strengthens," a meaning highly consistent with the prophet's life and the message of the book. Ezekiel did not minister in a period of prosperity and stability, but amid exile and national collapse. Thus, the declaration that God strengthens his servant was especially fitting for the prophet who needed extraordinary strength to bear a weighty message to a broken people.

2. Who was Ezekiel?

The book presents Ezekiel as "Ezekiel son of Buzi the priest," revealing that the prophet came from a clearly priestly background. He was in Babylonian exile by the Kebar River when God called him to prophetic ministry, and therefore he had an experience different from that of the prophets of Jerusalem who ministered within the city before its fall. Nevertheless, his priestly background made him deeply concerned with the temple, holiness, sacred things, and the divine presence. This explains why the temple and the glory of the Lord occupy such a large place at both the beginning and the end of the book.

3. The Year of Ezekiel's Birth and Death

The Bible does not directly provide the year of Ezekiel's birth; therefore, we cannot determine the year of his birth or death with historical precision. However, the period of his ministry can be identified through the dates recorded in the book. His calling began in the fifth year of the exile of King Jehoiachin, that is, around 593 B.C. according to traditional dating. His ministry appears to have extended into the first decades of the sixth century B.C., while the text provides no certain information about the date of his death or place of burial. Historical estimates should therefore not be turned into definitive facts.

4. The Time of Ezekiel's Ministry

Ezekiel's ministry began at a time when Judah had already entered the sphere of Babylonian domination, but Jerusalem had not yet fallen. This is highly important for understanding his message, because many of the exiled Jews thought that Jerusalem would remain standing and that the exile would be brief. Ezekiel, however, proclaimed that judgment was not yet over and that Jerusalem itself would be judged because of its sin. After the city's fall around 586 B.C., the focus of the book shifts more clearly from the proclamation of judgment to the proclamation of hope, restoration, the rebuilding of the people, and the return of the glory of the Lord.

5. Where Was Ezekiel?

Ezekiel was in the land of Babylon by the Kebar River, where he saw visions of God and his ministry began. This bears a very profound theological meaning, because the prophet's presence in the land of exile reveals that God's presence is not limited to the land of Israel and that Babylon cannot become a place beyond the Lord's authority. The people thought that the loss of the land meant the loss of the divine presence, but the Book of Ezekiel begins in a striking way: the glory of the Lord appears to the prophet in the land of exile itself.

6. To Whom Did Ezekiel Write?

The primary message was directed to the house of Israel and the exiles in Babylon, but it also encompasses Jerusalem, Judah, and the surrounding nations. Ezekiel addressed a community that needed an explanation of what had happened to it, because exile was, for many, a crisis of both identity and faith. Through the prophet, God therefore intended to declare that the exile was not evidence of the Lord's defeat before the gods of Babylon, but the result of judgment upon his people; and that God himself would work through this crisis to sanctify his name before the nations and restore his people.

7. The Reason for Writing the Book

The first purpose is to proclaim God's judgment upon Judah and Jerusalem because of sin, idolatry, and impurity, and then to proclaim God's judgments upon the nations surrounding his people. Yet the book moves beyond judgment to a comprehensive project of restoration, in which God promises to give his people a new heart and a new spirit, gather the dry bones, establish David as a shepherd over them, restore the land, build the temple, and restore the glory of the Lord. Thus, the book is not merely a book of destruction; it is a book of re-creation, as though God takes a people who appear dead and begins a new work with them.

8. The Structure of the Book of Ezekiel

The book can be clearly divided into four principal sections, while noting that scholars offer other detailed divisions. Chapters 1–24 focus on Ezekiel's calling and the proclamation of judgment against Judah and Jerusalem, whereas chapters 25–32 contain prophecies against the surrounding nations, especially Ammon, Moab, Edom, Philistia, Tyre, Sidon, and Egypt. Chapters 33–39 then proclaim the transition from judgment to hope and the restoration of Israel, while chapters 40–48 present the vision of the new temple, the new land, and the return of the glory of the Lord. The structure of the book may therefore be seen as follows: the glory of God → judgment → the nations → restoration → the glory of God in the temple.

9. Ezekiel 1 — The Vision of the Glory of the Lord

The book opens with one of the most mysterious and majestic visions in the Bible. Ezekiel sees a storm wind, a great cloud, and continual fire; then he sees the living creatures, the wheels, the throne, and the glory of the Lord. This vision is not merely an imaginative description, but a theological declaration of God's sovereign mobility, because the glory of the Lord appears far from Jerusalem and in the land of exile. The central meaning is that God was not defeated because his people were exiled; rather, his throne is present even in the place where human beings think God is absent.

10. The Living Creatures and the Wheels

The vision presents living creatures associated with wheels, and Ezekiel describes the movement of these wheels in an unusual manner, so that the scene seems to exceed the human capacity for ordinary expression. The purpose is not to give humanity a geometric map of the heavenly world, but to reveal the freedom of movement of the divine throne and God's absolute sovereignty. God is not confined within a particular geography, and the glory of the Lord can appear in Babylon as it appeared in Jerusalem. This becomes one of the most important theological keys for understanding the entire book.

11. Ezekiel the Watchman

Ezekiel was also called to be a watchman over the house of Israel, and this image gives the prophet a clear responsibility. The watchman sees danger and sounds the warning; therefore, he cannot remain silent when God commands him to speak. This reveals the servant's responsibility before God: he is not required to guarantee people's response, but he is responsible for faithfully proclaiming and warning.

12. Eating the Scroll

In a powerful symbolic scene, God commands Ezekiel to eat the scroll containing God's words, and he says that the words became in his mouth like honey for sweetness. The image means that the word of God must enter the prophet's life before it comes forth from his mouth, so that he is not merely a transmitter of religious information, but a person whose life has been shaped by the message he bears. At the same time, the sweetness of the word does not mean that carrying it out will be easy, because Ezekiel will bear a weighty message to a rebellious people.

13. Ezekiel's Prophetic Signs

Ezekiel used a large number of symbolic actions to explain his message to the people, such as lying on his side, drawing Jerusalem, carrying the baggage of exile, and displaying signs of siege. This method makes the prophet himself part of the message: people do not merely hear words, but see God's message before their eyes. This resembles some earlier prophets, but in Ezekiel it reaches a clear level of prophetic enactment of the coming event.

14. Ezekiel 8 — The Abominations of Jerusalem

In chapter eight, Ezekiel sees in a vision abominations occurring within Jerusalem and the temple, including various forms of idolatry and corrupt worship. The scene reveals a fundamental reason for judgment: the problem was not merely the presence of sins outside the temple, but that impurity had entered the very center of worship. Therefore, the later departure of the glory of the Lord from the temple is not arbitrary, but a response of God's holiness to the corruption that had entered the holy place.

15. Ezekiel 10 — The Glory of the Lord Leaves the Temple

One of the most awe-inspiring scenes in the book is the departure of the glory of the Lord from above the threshold and its movement away from the city. This reflects a profound spiritual catastrophe, because the temple that was meant to be the place where God's presence was revealed had become a place of impurity. The book therefore declares that God does not allow religious form to contain his glory while the people persist in disobedience, and that God's holiness is more important than the religious institution itself.

16. Judgment and Personal Responsibility

One of Ezekiel's most important teachings appears in chapter eighteen, where he rejects the proverb repeated by the people: "The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children's teeth are set on edge." God declares that the soul who sins is the one who dies, and that each person is responsible before God for his or her attitude and conduct. This does not deny the effects of collective or familial sin, but it affirms that a person cannot use the sins of his or her ancestors as an excuse to reject personal responsibility before God.

17. "Do I Take Pleasure in the Death of the Wicked?"

God declares:

"Do I indeed take pleasure in the death of the wicked? says the Lord God. Rather, do I not take pleasure when he turns from his ways and lives?"

This is one of the most important statements in the book because it reveals God's heart amid the message of judgment. God is just and punishes evil, but he takes no delight in human destruction; rather, he calls the wicked to turn and live. Judgment in Ezekiel is therefore not contrary to mercy; mercy itself explains why God sends warning before carrying out judgment.

18. Chapter 18 and Repentance

God repeatedly calls for repentance in clear terms, affirming that a person can turn from his wicked way and live. This makes the book far removed from the idea of spiritual fatalism, which says that a person cannot change his course. The prophetic message gives people real responsibility: turn back, abandon evil, do what is just, and live. Repentance is therefore not a marginal idea in Ezekiel, but part of the heart of the prophetic message.

19. Chapter 24 — The Death of Ezekiel's Wife

God commands Ezekiel not to mourn in the customary manner when his wife dies, and this becomes a prophetic sign of what will happen to the people when the sanctuary in which they take pride is destroyed. This is one of the harshest parts of the book from a human perspective, because the prophet's own life became part of the cost of the message. It reveals that the prophets in the Bible were not merely speakers about events, but sometimes bore in their personal lives a miniature image of God's message to the nation.

20. Prophecies Against the Nations

After proclaiming judgment upon Judah, the book turns to the surrounding nations, declaring judgment upon Ammon, Moab, Edom, Philistia, Tyre, Sidon, and Egypt. This affirms that Jerusalem's fall does not mean that the other nations have become beyond God's authority; rather, the Lord is the judge of all peoples. These prophecies also reveal that God does not permit the nations to rejoice forever over the fall of his people or to exploit their weakness to fulfill their ambitions.

21. Tyre — The Pride of the Commercial City

Among the longest prophecies against the nations are those concerning Tyre, the powerful coastal commercial city. Ezekiel portrays its economic prosperity and commercial skill, then reveals how success became pride that led the city to elevate itself to a level unbecoming to humanity. Here the book offers a principle that transcends historical Tyre: when success turns into arrogance, it can become a cause of the fall of a person, an institution, and a civilization.

22. Egypt

Egypt receives extensive attention in Ezekiel's prophecies because it was a major political power in the region. The book reveals that Egypt, which some thought could save Judah from Babylon, is not the true source of salvation, and that reliance upon political power instead of God leads to disappointment. Thus, the same theme seen in Isaiah and Jeremiah continues: political alliances cannot take the place of reliance upon God.

23. Ezekiel 33 — The Transition After the Fall of Jerusalem

When news reaches Ezekiel that the city has fallen, an important transition occurs in the structure of the book. Before the fall, the message focused heavily on warning and judgment; after the fall, the focus begins to shift to hope and restoration. This transition is not a change in God's nature, but a change in the historical stage, because the people who first needed to understand the reason for their fall now needed to know that God had not abandoned them permanently.

24. The Corrupt Shepherds

In chapter thirty-four, God declares judgment upon the shepherds of Israel who fed themselves and did not shepherd the flock. This image refers to the political and religious leadership that exploited the people instead of serving them. God therefore declares that he himself will seek out his sheep and gather them. Then comes the promise to establish one shepherd over them, David, an expression connected with the Davidic royal promise that becomes highly important in later messianic interpretation.

25. God Shepherds His People

The Lord says that he will seek his sheep and rescue them from all the places to which they have been scattered. This image reverses the entire situation, because the people who became without good leadership will find that God himself intervenes as a shepherd. Here appears one of the most important images of God in the book: God is not only the judge who condemns, but the shepherd who gathers, heals, restores, and feeds.

26. Ezekiel 36 — The New Heart

The book reaches one of the greatest promises of the Old Testament:

"And I will give you a new heart, and put a new spirit within you; and I will remove the heart of stone from your flesh and give you a heart of flesh."

The problem was not merely the land, the political system, or the king, but the human heart. God therefore does not merely restore the people to the land, but promises an inward work that changes the nature of their relationship with him. Here we see that true restoration is not merely a geographical return, but a spiritual renewal that makes the people able to walk in God's ways.

27. "And I Will Put My Spirit Within You"

The promise continues:

"And I will put my Spirit within you and cause you to walk in my statutes, and you will keep my judgments and do them."

Here the Spirit is directly connected with obedience, so Ezekiel does not present the Spirit as an experience separate from holiness, but as a divine work that leads to new life. This text therefore becomes highly important in the study of the theology of the Holy Spirit, because it reveals that God's purpose is to change the inner person so that obedience may appear outwardly.

28. The Valley of Dry Bones

One of the most famous visions in the Bible is the vision of the valley of dry bones in the thirty-seventh chapter. Ezekiel sees very many bones, and they are very dry. Then God asks him, "Son of man, can these bones live?" The prophet answers, "O Lord GOD, you know." Then God commands the bones to come together, and sinews, flesh, skin, and spirit come upon them, so they become a great army. The vision becomes a powerful image of God's ability to bring to life what seems impossible to human beings.

29. Do the Dry Bones Speak Only of Resurrection?

The direct meaning in the context of the book is the house of Israel, because God says explicitly, "These bones are the whole house of Israel." The people were saying that their hope had perished and that they were cut off, but God declares that he is able to open the graves, raise up his people, and bring them back to their land. This does not prevent a later theological application to the concept of resurrection, but the first interpretation of the text must respect its context, where it speaks directly about the restoration of Israel after the exile.

30. The Union between Judah and Israel

In the same chapter, Ezekiel takes two pieces of wood, one for Judah and the other for Joseph, and joins them in his hand as one piece. Then God declares that he will make the people one nation under one king. This promise is very important because it speaks not only of a geographical return, but of the restoration of the unity that had been broken since the division of the kingdom. Thus, restoration in Ezekiel becomes a restoration of identity, unity, kingship, and worship together.

31. David in the Book of Ezekiel

The name David recurs in the context of the future, with a promise that there will be one shepherd over the people. The meaning is not that the historical David will return to rule again, but that the name becomes a symbol of the Davidic king who will fulfill God's promises to his people. In a Christian reading, this line naturally moves toward Christ, the king who is the Son of David and the good shepherd, but the basis of the image in Ezekiel is the restoration of just rule under the shepherd whom God establishes.

32. Ezekiel 40–48 — The New Temple

The final part of the book presents a vast vision of the temple, the gates, the chambers, the altars, the land, and its division. Interpreters throughout history have differed over how to understand this vision. Some read it in a literal future sense, some read certain elements of it symbolically and theologically, and some combine the two dimensions. Yet however much the interpretive details differ, the central message is clear: God reorders worship, presence, and land around the holiness of his presence.

33. The Return of the Glory of the LORD

After the glory of the LORD left the temple in the tenth chapter, the book reaches, at its end, an almost opposite scene, as the glory of the LORD returns to the place. This is one of the greatest theological movements in the whole book, because the story began with glory departing because of impurity and ends with glory returning in the context of restoration. Therefore, the basic question is not only: Will the people return to the land? Rather, it is: Will God return to be in the midst of his people?

34. The Name of the City at the End of the Book

The book ends with a great phrase:

"And the name of the city from that day shall be YHWH Shammah."

The Hebrew phrase יְהוָה שָׁמָּה — YHWH Shammah means "The LORD is there." This conclusion nearly summarizes the whole book, because the problem at the beginning was not merely the loss of a city or a land, but a crisis in the people's relationship with the presence of God. In the end, the name of the city itself becomes a declaration: the LORD is there.

35. The Theological Movement of the Glory of the LORD

The glory of the LORD can be followed in the book as a clear dramatic line: it appears in the first chapter, then exposes the corruption of the temple, then leaves the temple in chapters 10–11. Then come judgment and exile; after that, God promises renewal; and finally, the glory returns to the temple in chapter 43. This movement makes the Book of Ezekiel one of the clearest books in the study of the theme of God's presence. True restoration is not completed merely by the human being's return to his place, but by the experience of the return of God's presence into his midst.

36. The Most Important Persons in the Book
Ezekiel

The prophet and priest who carried the message to the exiles, and whose visions and symbolic actions were an essential part of his ministry.

Buzi

Ezekiel's father, mentioned as the father of the prophet and priest to whom Ezekiel belongs.

Jehoiachin

The king of Judah who fell into the Babylonian exile, and the time of his exile is connected with the historical framework in which Ezekiel began his ministry.

Zedekiah

The last king of Judah, whose end coincided with the fall of Jerusalem and the destruction of the temple.

Nebuchadnezzar

The king of Babylon with whom the events of the siege, the exile, and the fall of Jerusalem were connected.

The Elders of Israel

They appear before Ezekiel in several places, and they represent the leadership that needed to confront the word of God concerning the sins of the people.

The Shepherds of Israel

They are an image of the corrupt leaders who exploited the people, and they are set against God's promise of the shepherd who gathers the flock and shepherds it.

37. The Most Important Events in the Book

The call of Ezekiel by the River Chebar, the vision of the glory of the LORD, the eating of the scroll, his appointment as a watchman over Israel, the symbolic actions concerning the siege of Jerusalem, the visions of the abominations of the temple, the departure of the glory of the LORD from the temple, the announcements of judgment against Judah, the prophecies against the nations, the fall of Jerusalem, then the transition to promises of restoration, the vision of the dry bones, the promise of the new heart and the new spirit, the unity of Judah and Israel, the vision of the new temple, the return of the glory of the LORD, and the naming of the city "YHWH Shammah."

38. The Most Important Message the Book Presents

God is holy, and therefore sin cannot continue without judgment, nor can the temple, national belonging, or a glorious past protect a human being from the consequences of his disobedience. But the same God who judges is the one who creates hope: he gathers his people, gives them a new heart and a new spirit, restores their unity, worship, and land, and, most importantly, restores his presence to their midst.

39. What Does the Book of Ezekiel Point Out to Us?

The book points out first that the glory of God is not limited by geography, because God appeared to his prophet in the land of exile when some thought that God's presence was connected with Jerusalem alone. Second, it declares that the fundamental problem in the human being is the heart; therefore, reforming external circumstances is not enough if the heart remains stone, but the human being needs God's inward work. Third, it reveals that God is able to restore life to what seems completely dead, as in the vision of the dry bones, and that true restoration includes spirit, unity, worship, and the divine presence. Finally, the book ends with its greatest image of hope: "YHWH Shammah — the LORD is there."

40. Ezekiel and Christ

The Book of Ezekiel contains important messianic lines, although it is not entirely direct prophecies about Christ. The image of the one shepherd who leads God's people, the promise of the Davidic king, and the image of God who gathers his flock all enter into the background that the New Testament uses to understand Christ as the good shepherd and the king who is the Son of David. The promise of the new heart and the new spirit also finds a clear echo in the teaching of the New Testament about the new birth and the work of the Holy Spirit in the believer.

41. Ezekiel and the Holy Spirit

The Spirit occupies a very important place in the Book of Ezekiel, both in moving the prophet and in God's promise to give his people a new spirit. This connects spiritual life with obedience, because God does not give the Spirit merely for an external experience, but so that the people may walk in his ways and keep his commandments. In light of the New Testament, this promise becomes an important part of understanding the work of the Holy Spirit in renewing the human being and giving him the ability to live according to the will of God.

42. Ezekiel and Resurrection

The vision of the dry bones is not a direct text about the resurrection of the dead in its original context, but it presents a very powerful biblical image of God's ability to give life to what seems dead. For this reason, the vision has become of great value in biblical theology when studying resurrection, restoration, and new creation. The principle it presents is clear: what the human being sees as a final end may, with God, be the beginning of a new work.

43. Ezekiel and the Temple

The temple in Ezekiel is not merely a building, but the center of the theme of holiness, presence, and worship. The book begins with the glory of God away from the temple, then explains the reason for the departure of the glory, and ends with a vision of the restoration of the temple and the return of the glory to it. Therefore, the study of the temple in Ezekiel must not be separated from the study of God's holiness and God's dwelling in the midst of his people.

44. The Great Movement of the Book of Ezekiel

The book can be summarized in this path:

The glory of God appears.

Then:

God exposes sin.

Then:

Jerusalem is judged.

Then:

The glory of God leaves the temple.

Then:

The nations are judged.

Then:

The exile becomes a reality.

Then:

God promises restoration.

Then:

A new heart and a new spirit.

Then:

The dry bones live.

Then:

The people are united.

Then:

The one shepherd rules.

Then:

The temple is restored.

And finally:

"The LORD is there."

This movement makes the book one of the most integrated books in depicting judgment that leads to renewal and the return of the divine presence.

📚 Primary References for Study

Biblical text: the Book of Ezekiel, chapters 1–48, with reference to 2 Kings 24–25, 2 Chronicles 36, and Jeremiah 24, 29, and 52 in order to understand the historical background of the exile and the fall of Jerusalem. The study of Ezekiel is also particularly related to Genesis 1–3, Exodus 25–40, Leviticus 17–26, Isaiah, Jeremiah, and Daniel, because the themes of presence, holiness, covenant, exile, and restoration intersect across these books. In the New Testament, the study of John 10, John 3, Romans 8, Hebrews, and Revelation 21–22 is useful for tracing certain theological threads whose background is found in Ezekiel.

🔑 The sentence that summarizes the Book of Ezekiel

“The Book of Ezekiel is a journey from the glory of God appearing in the land of exile, to a people who discovered that sin cannot hide behind the temple or national identity; then to the glory of God departing from the holy place because of defilement; and afterward to a people who appear like dry bones that have lost all hope. Yet God does not leave them in their death; He promises them a new heart and a new spirit, gathers them from their dispersion, appoints for them one shepherd, restores worship and the land to them, and then returns His glory to their midst, so that the final word of the book is neither ‘exile,’ nor ‘desolation,’ nor ‘death,’ but: ‘The LORD Is There.’”
