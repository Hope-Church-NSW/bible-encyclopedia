Introduction to the Book of Obadiah

The central idea of the book: The Book of Obadiah is the shortest book of the Old Testament, yet it carries a profoundly deep theological message about the judgment of pride, human responsibility for one’s attitude toward one’s brother, the Day of the Lord, the fall of Edom, and the triumph of the kingdom of the Lord. The book focuses on Edom, the people associated with Esau, who rejoiced over the fall of Jerusalem and exploited its calamity; therefore, the relationship of brother to brother becomes an important moral axis in the prophecy. The book begins by announcing the fall of Edom despite its fortifications and self-confidence, then exposes the sin of gloating and standing aloof during the disaster, and ends by declaring that the kingdom will belong to the Lord. From here, the movement of the book can be summarized as: pride → fall → gloating → judgment → the Day of the Lord → the restoration of God’s people → the kingdom of the Lord.

1. Name of the book and its meaning

The name of the book is Obadiah — עֹבַדְיָה — ʿŌḇaḏyāh, which is a Hebrew name related to the phrase "servant of Yahweh" or "servant of the LORD." The name consists of an element denoting a servant or attendant and an element referring to the name of the LORD. Therefore, the prophet's name itself carries an important theological declaration: the true human being before God is a servant of the LORD, not the master of himself. The paradox is that the book bearing the name "servant of the LORD" announces the fall of a nation that trusted in its own power and status and forgot the authority of the LORD.

2. Who is Obadiah?

The book provides no information about Obadiah other than his name, and therefore we cannot determine with certainty his lineage, homeland, occupation, or social background. There are other people in the Old Testament who bear the name Obadiah, but there is no sufficient biblical basis to say they are the same person. Therefore, a distinction should be made between the prophet Obadiah, the author of the book, and the other individuals who bore the same name.

3. The year of Obadiah's birth and death

The Bible does not record the year of the prophet's birth or the year of his death. Nor does the book give us a direct date for his ministry through the name of a reigning king. Therefore, the determination of his time depends on the historical indications found in the book, especially the description of the enemies' attack on Jerusalem and Edom's exploitation of the disaster.

4. Date of writing the book

There are multiple views on determining the date of Obadiah, but one of the strongest tendencies links it to the period of the fall of Jerusalem before the Babylonians in the sixth century BC, particularly the events of the year 586 BC, because the book describes Edom's stance toward the fall of Jerusalem in a way that strongly corresponds to that period. However, a distinction must be made between what is historically probable and what is textually certain, because the book does not mention the name of Nebuchadnezzar or a specific year.

5. For whom was the book written?

The primary audience was the people of Judah, especially those who experienced the disaster of the fall of Jerusalem or carried its memory. But the message does not stop at Judah, because it is also directed toward Edom and toward the nations in general. The book wants to tell God's people that the devastation they went through is not the end of the story, and that injustice and gloating will not triumph forever.

6. Reason for writing the book

The book came to announce God's judgment on Edom because of her pride and hostility toward Judah. The problem was not that Edom was merely a foreign nation, but that her stance toward the fall of Jerusalem revealed a heart that rejoiced over a brother's calamity and exploited his weakness. Therefore, Edom's sin becomes a dangerous model for a person who is not content merely with failing to help his brother, but gloats over his fall and profits from his weakness.

7. Edom's relationship with Israel

Edom is connected to Israel through Esau and Jacob, for they are brothers according to the book of Genesis. Therefore, the enmity between the two peoples carries an ancient family background, but this does not justify the continuation of hatred. Rather, this history makes God's judgment more profound, because Edom was not entirely a stranger to Israel, but was a nation connected to it through a common origin.

8. The division of the book of Obadiah

The book can be divided into three main sections. The book begins in verses 1–9 with the announcement of Edom's fall and the exposure of her pride and her trust in her strongholds and geographical location, then verses 10–14 come to reveal the reasons for the judgment, especially violence against the brother and gloating over the fall of Jerusalem. Then verses 15–21 come to announce the day of the LORD, the judgment of the nations, and the restoration of God's people, and it ends with the decisive sentence: "and the kingdom shall be the LORD's."

9. "I have made you small among the nations"

The book opens with an announcement that the LORD will make Edom small among the nations. This is an important beginning because Edom saw herself as strong and secure, while God declares that human power does not determine the future. A person may see himself as great because of his location, wealth, or fortifications, but God can demolish in a moment what man has built over years.

10. Pride of heart

The LORD says of Edom:

"The pride of your heart has deceived you."

This phrase reveals that the problem was not only in geography or politics, but in the heart. Pride begins internally before it appears externally, and when a person becomes convinced that he cannot fall, he has already begun on the path to falling.

11. The rocks of Edom

Edom enjoyed a very strong geographical position, and her mountainous region was associated with rocks, heights, and natural fortifications. Therefore, Edom thought that her location made her safe from her enemies. But Obadiah declares that geographical fortification cannot protect a proud heart from God's judgment.

12. "Who will bring me down to the ground?"

The language of the book reveals that Edom said in her heart:

"Who will bring me down to the ground?"

This is not merely military confidence, but a spiritual stance based on excluding the possibility of human downfall. Here we find a recurring principle in the Bible: when strength turns into absolute self-confidence, blessing turns into a gateway to pride.

13. The eagles and the nest on high

The book uses a powerful image:

"Though you soar aloft like the eagle, though your nest is set among the stars, from there I will bring you down, says the LORD."

Even if a person manages to build for himself a place that seems beyond everyone's reach, God remains above every height. The image reminds us of a general principle in the Bible: there is no height that can place a person outside God's authority.

14. Edom's fundamental sin

The book arrives at the reason for the judgment:

"For the violence done to your brother Jacob, shame shall cover you, and you shall be cut off forever."

This is one of the most important statements of the book, because God does not view the relationship between nations merely as a political conflict, but sees in it a moral responsibility. Injustice becomes a doubled sin when it befalls a brother, because closeness and kinship increase responsibility rather than diminish it.

15. Standing on the other side

Obadiah says:

"On the day that you stood aloof, on the day that strangers carried off his wealth, and foreigners entered his gates and cast lots for Jerusalem, you were like one of them."

The problem here is that Edom was not merely a neutral bystander, but stood on the side of the attackers. This teaches us that neutrality in the face of injustice is not always neutrality, because standing beside the aggressor or profiting from the fall of the oppressed can make a person complicit in evil.

16. Gloating over your brother's day

The book says:

"Do not gloat over your brother's day, the day of his misfortune, and do not rejoice over the people of Judah in the day of their ruin."

This verse reveals that gloating itself is a sin. A person may not have caused the disaster, but when he rejoices over another's fall and considers his calamity an opportunity for revenge, he reveals a heart that does not know the mercy of God.

17. The plundering of Jerusalem

The book describes the entry of the enemies into Jerusalem and the plundering of her possessions, then accuses Edom of participating in this behavior. Here it becomes clear that Edom's sin was not merely internal feelings, but turned into practical behavior and exploitation of weakness. Therefore, God holds people accountable not only for what they think, but also for what they do when an opportunity to exploit another arises.

18. The sin of profiting from the downfall of others

One of the most dangerous elements of the book is that Edom saw the fall of Judah as an opportunity for her own benefit. This is a sin that repeats in every age: that a person sees another's loss as an opportunity to increase his own gains. But Obadiah declares that God sees these practices and judges them, because the LORD does not measure success only by what a person has gained, but by the manner in which he gained it.

19. "As you have done, it shall be done to you"

The book says:

"For the day of the LORD is near upon all the nations. As you have done, it shall be done to you; your deeds shall return on your own head."

Here the principle of moral harvest appears clearly. This does not mean that every event in a person's life is an immediate punishment for a specific sin, but the book declares that history is not without justice, and that evil will not escape God's judgment forever.

20. The day of the LORD

Obadiah moves from the case of Edom to a broader horizon:

"The day of the LORD is near upon all the nations."

This is very important, because God does not judge Edom alone, but declares that the day of the LORD is a day of reckoning for all the nations. Thus the tragedy of Jerusalem turns from a local incident into part of a greater declaration of God's sovereignty over history.

21. "As you have drunk on my holy mountain"

The image of the cup appears in the book, which is a prophetic image of wrath and judgment. And just as the nations have drunk from the consequences of their actions, an hour will come when the oppressor faces the result of his wickedness. Here Obadiah's message meets with many of the prophets who use the cup as a symbol of divine judgment.

22. Deliverance on Mount Zion

In the midst of judgment a promise appears:

"But on Mount Zion there shall be deliverance, and it shall be holy."

The book does not want to end with a message of revenge alone, but declares that God will preserve his people and rebuild what was destroyed. Here an important duality appears: judgment upon evil, and deliverance for God's people.

23. "And the LORD shall reign"

The book ends with this statement:

"And the kingdom shall be the LORD's."

This is one of the most powerful theological endings in the prophetic books, because it sums up the ultimate goal of all history. The end is not merely the fall of Edom or the return of Judah, but the declaration of God's sovereignty and kingship.

24. Obadiah, Esau, and Jacob

The background found in the book of Genesis is very important for understanding Obadiah, because Edom is the descendants of Esau, while Judah and Israel are connected to Jacob. Thus the prophecy becomes an extension of a long history of conflict between the two brothers. But the message of the book does not say that the enmity of the ancestors must continue forever, but reveals that God holds each generation accountable for its own actions.

25. Obadiah and Psalm 137

There is a clear echo between the bitterness of the fall of Jerusalem and what appears in Psalm 137, where the relationship between Babylon and Edom appears in the context of Jerusalem's destruction. This helps the researcher see that Obadiah belongs to a broad literary and historical world of texts that dealt with the fall of Jerusalem, the hostility of the nations, and the awaiting of God's justice.

26. Obadiah, Isaiah, and Jeremiah

The announcement of Edom's fall also appears in Isaiah 34 and 63 and Jeremiah 49, and therefore Obadiah should not be studied in isolation. These texts help build an integrated biblical picture of Edom and her relationship with Israel, and of God's stance toward pride, hostility, and vengeance. When these texts are gathered together, a clear theological unity appears: God is the judge of the nations and also the protector of his people.

27. Obadiah and Christ

The book does not present a direct messianic prophecy in the name of Christ, but it contributes to the biblical line that ends with the declaration of the LORD's kingship. In Christian reading, this line is connected to the kingdom of God that was revealed in Christ, where the ultimate goal is not merely the political restoration of Israel, but the establishment of God's authority. Therefore, the phrase "and the kingdom shall be the LORD's" carries a broad kingdom horizon that goes beyond the borders of Edom and Judah.

28. Obadiah and the kingdom

The kingdom is the final word in the book. The proud person says, "Who will bring me down?" but God declares that the kingdom ultimately belongs to him. This contrast sums up the theological conflict in the book: the kingdom of man built on pride versus the kingdom of God built on truth and justice.

29. What does the book teach us about pride?

Obadiah teaches us that pride does not begin when a person falls before people, but when he believes he cannot fall. Real fortifications may exist around a person, but if he makes them the ultimate source of his security, they turn into deception. Therefore, the book calls for humility and trust in God instead of reliance on strength, position, and possessions.

30. What does it teach us about gloating?

The book is very clear in its rejection of gloating. Another's calamity is not an opportunity to satisfy revenge, even when there is historical enmity. A person who sees his brother's fall should ask how he can help or pray, not how he can profit from his weakness. This makes Obadiah a book of great importance in the study of Christian ethics toward rivals and enemies.

31. What does the book of Obadiah point us to?

The book points to the fact that God sees what happens in the moments of the fall of nations and individuals even when he seems silent. It declares that pride cannot last forever, and that gloating and exploiting the oppressed have a reckoning before God. It also reveals that history is not a series of random events, but moves toward the day when God's authority will appear in final form. Therefore, the ending is not "Judah's victory over Edom," but "and the kingdom shall be the LORD's."

32. The most important people in the book
Obadiah

The prophet who carried God's declaration against Edom and announced the day of the LORD.

James

Represents the people of Israel and Judah in the background of the historical relationship with Edom.

Esau

Represents the ancestral origin of Edom, being the twin brother of Jacob.

Edom

Not a single person, but the people descended from Esau, to whom the book directs a message of judgment.

The inhabitants of Judah and Jerusalem

They represent the people who suffered downfall and plunder, and who receive the promise of deliverance and restoration.

33. The Most Important Events in the Book

The announcement of Edom’s downfall; the exposure of its pride; the revelation of its confidence in its mountainous location; the announcement that enemies will bring it down from its position; the accusation of violence against Jacob; the condemnation of its standing with the aggressors; the condemnation of rejoicing over Jerusalem’s downfall; the announcement of the day of the LORD against the nations; the promise of deliverance on Mount Zion; and finally, the announcement of the restoration of God’s people and the culmination of history in the reign of the LORD.

34. The Most Important Message the Book Presents

There are no fortresses that can protect a person from God’s judgment when security turns into pride, and no calamity that befalls a brother can make gloating a virtue, because God sees injustice just as he sees rejoicing over the downfall of the oppressed. The day of the LORD will come, when the motives of hearts and their deeds will be exposed. Yet amid judgment, hope remains for God’s people, because the true end is not the victory of one nation over another, but the revelation of the eternal truth: “The kingdom shall be the LORD’s.”

35. The Major Movement of the Book of Obadiah

The book can be summarized in this path:

Pride.

Then:

Self-confidence.

Then:

The brother’s downfall.

Then:

Gloating.

Then:

Exploitation.

Then:

Judgment.

Then:

The day of the Lord.

Then:

Deliverance.

Then:

Restoration.

Then:

The reign of the LORD.

Thus, the book begins with the proud person’s question, “Who will bring me down?”, and ends with God’s answer: “The kingdom shall be the LORD’s.”

🔑 The sentence that summarizes the Book of Obadiah

“The Book of Obadiah is short in length but profound in its exposure of the human heart. Edom did not fall because it was weaker than its enemies, but because it thought its strength placed it beyond falling. Then Jerusalem’s calamity revealed the gloating, hostility, and exploitation within its heart. God therefore declared that whoever rejoices over his brother’s downfall and participates in his oppression will face the day of the LORD, and that the deed a person commits can recoil upon his own head. Yet the story does not end with vengeance, but with the restoration of God’s people and the proclamation of his sovereignty, until the prophecy reaches its greatest statement: “The kingdom shall be the LORD’s.” Thus, the book moves from human pride to human downfall, and from the downfall of the nations to the appearing of God’s kingdom.”
