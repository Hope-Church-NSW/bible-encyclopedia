Introduction to the Gospel of Matthew
1. Name of the book and its meaning

The name Matthew is derived from the Greek name Ματθαῖος (Matthaios), which is connected to the Hebrew or Aramaic name meaning 'gift of God' or 'present.' Matthew himself is mentioned in the Gospel as a tax collector who was sitting at the tax office when Christ called him, saying: 'Follow me,' whereupon he left his place and followed Christ. The significance of this name appears in its connection to the story of a person who moved from a socially despised occupation to become a disciple, apostle, and witness to the Gospel, reflecting an important aspect of Christ's grace that calls a person from an old state into a new life.

2. The Author and His Character

Ancient Christian tradition attributes this Gospel to Matthew the Apostle, one of the Twelve whom Christ chose, who is the same person mentioned in the Gospel as the tax collector called by the Lord from the tax office. The Gospel is characterized by a clear concern for the Law, the Prophets, genealogies, and prophecies and their fulfillment in the life of Christ, which aligns with the strong Jewish background from which the book emerges. Although modern critical studies discuss certain details regarding the manner of the Gospel's composition and its sources, early church tradition consistently attributed it to Matthew, and one should not confuse the existence of an academic debate over authorship with the fact that ancient Christian tradition preserved the name of Matthew as the author of the Gospel.

3. Matthew's Birth and Death

The Bible does not provide specific information about the year of Matthew's birth or the year of his death, and therefore an exact date for these two events cannot be determined without relying on later traditional accounts. Different accounts exist in ancient Christian sources regarding Matthew's ministry and the end of his life, but their details are not agreed upon sufficiently to present a specific year as an established historical fact. What we can confirm scripturally is that Matthew was a tax collector, that Christ called him, that he became one of the Twelve, and that his name remained connected to the testimony of the Gospel in Christian tradition.

4. For Whom Was the Gospel Written?

The Gospel of Matthew shows special concern for the reader who knows the Jewish Bible and understands the language of covenant, prophecy, and the Law. Therefore, it frequently quotes from the Old Testament and demonstrates that the events of Christ's life came as a fulfillment of what God had previously proclaimed through Moses, the Prophets, and the Psalms. Nevertheless, the Gospel does not stop at the borders of Israel, but moves gradually from the promise to Abraham to Christ, from Christ to the disciples, and from the disciples to all nations; thus its ultimate message is universal and not restricted to a single people.

5. Reasons for Writing the Gospel

The primary purpose of the Gospel of Matthew is to present Jesus as the promised Messiah, the Son of David and the Son of Abraham, and the King awaited by the Old Testament narrative. For this reason, the Gospel begins with the genealogy rather than starting directly with ministry or miracles, because Matthew wants to establish from the beginning that Jesus did not appear detached from salvation history, but came at the very heart of the promises given by God to Abraham and David. This purpose appears repeatedly in phrases such as 'that it might be fulfilled which was spoken by the Lord through the prophet,' making prophetic fulfillment one of the main keys to understanding the Gospel.

6. Date of Writing of the Gospel

Matthew does not specify an explicit date for the writing of his Gospel, and therefore scholars have differed on determining the exact year in which it was written. Modern estimates often range between the sixties and eighties of the first century AD, with differences existing between critical and traditional schools in determining the date. The importance of this issue increases when studying Christ's discourse on the destruction of Jerusalem and the Temple, because the destruction of the Temple in the year 70 AD represents an important historical background in the debate surrounding the date of the Gospel's composition.

7. The Main Theme of the Gospel

The central theme in the Gospel of Matthew is Jesus Christ, the King and Messiah who came to announce the kingdom of heaven and fulfill God's promises in the Old Testament. Matthew presents Christ as the Son of David who has the right to the kingdom, the Son of Abraham to whom the promises of blessing for all nations are attached, the Son of Man who possesses authority, and the Son of God whose identity the Father declares before the world. Thus the Gospel combines discourse on the kingdom, teaching, miracles, prophecies, the Cross, and the Resurrection into one story whose purpose is to proclaim the identity and authority of Christ.

8. The Kingdom of Heaven

Among the most prominent expressions that distinguish the Gospel of Matthew is the phrase "the kingdom of heaven," which refers to God's reign and authority, decisively entering history through the person and work of Christ. In the Gospel, the kingdom is not merely a place to which a person goes after death; rather, it is God's authority revealed in Christ and entering human life. Therefore, Christ called people to repentance, faith, and readiness. The parables of the kingdom in Matthew 13 explain how God's work begins in a way that may seem small in human eyes, then spreads until it reaches its goal in an abundant harvest.

9. The Genealogy of Christ

Matthew opens his Gospel with the words: "The book of the genealogy of Jesus Christ, the son of David, the son of Abraham." This brief statement carries a profound theological key to the entire Gospel. The mention of Abraham links Christ to the promise of blessing that God gave to the nations, while the mention of David links him to the promise of kingship, the throne, and the seed that continues before God. Thus Matthew declares from the very beginning that Christ is the point of convergence of two major lines in the Old Testament: the promise of blessing to Abraham and the promise of kingship to David.

10. The Birth of Christ

Matthew declares that Mary conceived by the Holy Spirit and that the child born of her was not merely an ordinary child, but the one in whom God's saving purpose is fulfilled. The angel explains to Joseph that the name given to the child will be Jesus because "he will save his people from their sins," thus connecting Christ's name directly with the mission of salvation from his birth. Matthew then uses Isaiah's prophecy concerning Immanuel to declare that Christ's coming is connected with God's presence among his people, an idea that returns at the end of the Gospel when Christ says to his disciples: "Behold, I am with you always."

11. The Magi and Herod

The Magi come from the east seeking "the King of the Jews who has been born." Matthew presents them as Gentiles who came seeking the king whom many among his own people did not recognize. By contrast, Herod is troubled, and Jerusalem with him, because the appearance of the new king represents a threat to the existing human authority. From the beginning of the Gospel, the paradox that will continue to the cross appears: the Gentiles seek Christ while those in authority resist him, and the true king appears as a vulnerable child while earthly kingdoms tremble before him.

12. Christ and Israel

Joseph takes the child and his mother to Egypt, then returns after Herod's death, and Matthew quotes Hosea: "Out of Egypt I called my son." In this way, Matthew sees in Christ a deeper fulfillment of Israel's story, because Christ recapitulates in his own person the course of the son who came out of Egypt, entered the wilderness, and then walked in the path of obedience. This helps explain Christ's temptation in the wilderness, because Jesus faces temptation where Israel formerly failed and proves to be the obedient Son who lives by every word that comes from the mouth of God.

13. John the Baptist

John the Baptist appears in the wilderness proclaiming: "Repent, for the kingdom of heaven has drawn near." Thus, his voice becomes a direct introduction to Christ's ministry. Matthew connects John with Isaiah's prophecy concerning the voice crying in the wilderness, and his appearance also carries a clear echo of Malachi's words concerning the messenger who prepares the way before the Lord. Thus, John becomes a transitional link between the expectation of the Old Testament and the proclamation of the New Testament, for he stands at the end of the age of expectation and points to the beginning of the age of Christ's presence.

14. The Baptism of Christ

At Christ's baptism, the heavens open, the Spirit of God descends like a dove, and a voice comes from heaven saying: "This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased." The scene clearly brings together the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, and therefore it has become one of the important texts in the study of the biblical revelation of the Trinity. The baptism also marks the beginning of Christ's public manifestation in his ministry, where the Father declares God's approval of the Son before Christ begins the path of teaching, miracles, and the cross.

15. The Temptation of Christ in the Wilderness

Christ is led by the Spirit into the wilderness to be tempted by the devil, and there he faces three temptations connected with hunger, authority, and displaying glory in a manner separate from God's will. In each case Christ responds by using the word of God, especially from Deuteronomy, to declare that obedience to God is more important than satisfying desire or obtaining glory in a wrong way. In this way Matthew presents Christ as the true Israel and the obedient Son who triumphs where humanity has fallen, and he proves that the way of the kingdom begins with obedience, not with display.

16. The Sermon on the Mount

Matthew 5–7 constitutes the greatest continuous unit of teaching in the Gospel, in which Christ reveals the nature of the life that the children of the kingdom should live. He begins with the Beatitudes, then speaks about salt and light, the law, anger, adultery, oaths, retaliation, love for enemies, prayer, fasting, money, and judgment. Christ does not merely present a collection of ethical rules; rather, he reveals that the righteousness of God's kingdom reaches the heart and inward motives and is not satisfied with the outward form of conduct.

17. Christ and the Law

Christ clearly declares: "Do not think that I came to abolish the Law or the Prophets. I did not come to abolish but to fulfill." This statement is foundational for understanding the relationship between the New Testament and the Old Testament, because Matthew does not present Christ as separate from the preceding Scripture, but as the person in whom its purpose is fulfilled and in whom it reaches its fullness. Therefore, the Gospel of Matthew is among the most important books for revealing the unity of the biblical story from the Law and the Prophets to Christ.

18. The Miracles of Christ

After the Sermon on the Mount, Matthew moves to a broad collection of miracles that display Christ's authority over disease, nature, demons, and death. He heals the leper, the paralytic, and the centurion's servant; calms the sea; casts out evil spirits; raises the dead; and restores sight, hearing, and movement to the sick. Matthew thus connects Christ's authority in word with his authority in deed: the one who speaks with authority in the sermon is the same one who exercises the authority of the kingdom over the powers of evil, disease, and death.

19. The Calling of Matthew

The calling of Matthew comes amid this proclamation of Christ's work. Jesus says to him, "Follow me," and Matthew rises and follows him. Christ then sits with tax collectors and sinners and declares: "I did not come to call righteous people, but sinners to repentance." Theologically, Matthew's own story becomes a model of the Gospel he writes, because the author is not merely a historian standing outside the events, but a man who personally experienced Christ's call, grace, and the transformation of his life.

20. Sending the Twelve

Christ chooses twelve apostles and first sends them to the lost sheep of the house of Israel, giving them authority over unclean spirits, diseases, and illnesses. This stage reveals that the kingdom proclaimed by Christ is not confined to himself alone, but extends through the community of his disciples who carry his message. By the end of the Gospel, the circle of mission expands from Israel to all nations, revealing a clear movement from the local beginning to the universal end.

21. Peter and the Confession of Christ

Christ asks his disciples: "But who do you say that I am?" Peter answers: "You are the Christ, the Son of the living God." This moment represents a major turning point in the Gospel, because Christ's identity, which had gradually appeared in miracles and teaching, now becomes the subject of a direct confession by the disciples. After this confession, Christ begins to announce the cross, making clear that understanding the title "the Christ" is incomplete without understanding the path of suffering, death, and resurrection.

22. The Transfiguration

Christ goes up a high mountain with Peter, James, and John, and there his appearance is transformed before them, while Moses and Elijah appear. Then the Father's voice comes, saying: "This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased. Listen to him." Thus Christ appears in a unique relationship to the Law and the Prophets. The scene does not place Moses and Elijah in competition with Christ, but indicates that the Law and the Prophets find their purpose in the Son to whom the disciples must listen.

23. The Road to Jerusalem

After Peter's confession and the Transfiguration, the direction of the Gospel turns clearly toward Jerusalem, where Christ knows beforehand that suffering and the cross await him. Christ begins to teach his disciples that true discipleship cannot be separated from self-denial, taking up the cross, and following him. Here Matthew reveals that the kingdom is not a path to easy human glory; rather, the path of the King himself passes through the cross before the resurrection.

24. The Entry into Jerusalem

Christ enters Jerusalem riding on a colt, and Matthew clearly points to the fulfillment of Zechariah's prophecy concerning the king who comes humble and riding on a donkey. The crowd cries out to Christ as the Son of David, so the scene appears as a royal entry into the city. Yet the king who enters Jerusalem does not come with an army or weapons, but comes in humility and then goes directly to the temple, where he will pronounce judgment on corrupt worship.

25. The Cleansing of the Temple

Christ enters the temple and drives out those who buy and sell, then says: "My house shall be called a house of prayer, but you have made it a den of robbers." Here Christ appears as the Lord who has authority over the temple, not merely as a teacher who disagrees with certain religious practices. This scene is powerfully connected with Malachi's prophecy concerning the coming of the Lord to his temple. Therefore, the cleansing of the temple represents a declaration of God's visitation of his people and judgment upon worship that has lost its true nature.

26. Confrontation with the Religious Leaders

The confrontation between Christ and the scribes, Pharisees, and chief priests intensifies, and Christ exposes the contradiction between religious appearance and inward reality. In chapter twenty-three, he announces a series of woes against the leaders who speak but do not act and care more for appearance than for righteousness, mercy, and faith. This confrontation is not merely a personal dispute, but a struggle over who possesses authority and who is the true interpreter of God's will.

27. The Prophecy of Jerusalem's Destruction

Christ speaks about the destruction of the temple and says: "Not one stone will be left here upon another that will not be thrown down." He then speaks about tribulation and the coming of the Son of Man. Matthew places these words within broad teaching about watchfulness, readiness, and not being deceived. The destruction of Jerusalem and the temple in 70 AD remains a very important historical background when studying this section, while it is necessary to distinguish between what concerns Jerusalem's destruction and what is connected with eschatological events and the coming of the Son of Man.

28. The Last Supper and the New Covenant

At the Last Supper, Christ takes the bread and the cup and declares his body and blood, then says concerning the cup: "This is my blood of the new covenant, which is poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins." Here the theme of covenant reaches an important climax, because Christ does not present his death merely as a tragic event, but interprets it himself as an act connected with the covenant and forgiveness of sins. This image is connected with the background of Exodus 24 and other covenant texts, yet in Christ it reaches a new level of revelation and redemption.

29. Gethsemane and the Arrest of Christ

In Gethsemane, Christ prays to the Father, saying: "Not as I will, but as you will," thus displaying his complete obedience before the cup awaiting him. Then Judas comes with the crowd and they arrest him; the disciples flee, and the community that was with him is scattered. Here the reader can see an echo of Zechariah in the image of the shepherd who is struck and the sheep scattered, a connection that deepens our understanding of Matthew's way of reading the events of the cross through the Old Testament.

30. The Trial and the Cross

Christ stands before the chief priests and then before Pilate, and the question turns to his identity as the King of the Jews. He reaches Golgotha and is crucified, and the inscription is placed above the cross: "This is Jesus, the King of the Jews." Thus the great paradox of the Gospel appears: Christ's kingship is proclaimed at the moment when the world thinks that he has been defeated. From this point, the cross in the Gospel of Matthew becomes not merely a tragedy, but a strange throne for the King who came to save his people from their sins.

31. The Death of Christ

When Christ dies, darkness comes over the land, the veil of the temple is torn from top to bottom, powerful earthquake signs occur, and the centurion and those with him confess, saying: "Truly this was the Son of God." The tearing of the veil points to a profound theological significance connected with the relationship between God, humanity, the temple, and holiness. At the moment when Christ appears to have lost all authority, Matthew presents signs revealing that his death itself is part of God's saving work.

32. The Resurrection

On the first day of the week, the women come to the tomb and find it open and empty, and the angel declares: "He is not here, for he has risen, as he said." The resurrection is not an event separate from Christ's earlier words, but the fulfillment of what he had previously declared about his death and resurrection. Here fear and sorrow become testimony, and the disciples are transformed from a defeated community into a community that will be sent to the whole world.

33. The Great Commission

The Gospel of Matthew ends with Christ's declaration: "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me," followed by his command to his disciples: "Go therefore and make disciples of all nations." He commands them to baptize in the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, and to teach the nations all that he commanded them. Here the Gospel reaches its climax, because the King whose manifestation began in the land of Israel concludes by declaring his authority over heaven and earth and his mission to all nations.

34. The Most Important Figures in the Gospel of Matthew

Jesus Christ is the central figure: the Messiah, the King, the Son of God, the Son of Man, the Teacher, and the Savior. All the events of the Gospel move around his person, authority, and mission. Mary and Joseph appear in the birth narrative, while John the Baptist represents the connecting link between Old Testament expectation and Christ's appearance. Peter stands out especially among the disciples, while Judas Iscariot appears as the disciple who betrayed Christ and handed him over. Herod appears as a king fearful of the birth of another king, and Pilate as the governor who handed Christ over for crucifixion, while the religious leaders represent the principal party in the confrontation with Christ.

35. The Central Message of the Gospel of Matthew

The Gospel of Matthew declares that Jesus is the Messiah promised in Scripture, the Son of David and the Son of Abraham, who came to fulfill the Law and the Prophets and proclaim the kingdom of heaven. It reveals that Christ did not come to establish a political kingdom according to the human model, but came to reign over hearts, forgive sins, heal the sick, free humanity from the authority of evil, and gather a people who live according to the righteousness of the kingdom. It then shows that the path of this King passes through the cross, that his death was not a defeat but an act connected with the new covenant and forgiveness of sins, and that his resurrection proclaimed his victory and complete authority. Therefore, he sent his disciples to all nations to make disciples, teach them, and carry the message of the kingdom to the world.

36. The Grand Movement of the Gospel of Matthew

The grand movement of the Gospel of Matthew begins with God's promise to Abraham, David's kingship, and the expectation of the Messiah. It then moves to the birth of Jesus as the Son of David, the Son of Abraham, and Immanuel, which means "God with us." After that, John the Baptist appears to prepare the way, and Christ comes to proclaim that the kingdom of heaven has drawn near, then demonstrates his authority through his teaching, miracles, and call to repentance and faith. From there, the Gospel moves toward Peter's confession of Christ, then Christ's announcement of the cross, then the entry into Jerusalem, the cleansing of the temple, and the confrontation with the leaders, followed by the Last Supper, suffering, crucifixion, and death. After the resurrection, the scene changes completely, because Christ, who appeared to be rejected and defeated, appears as the risen King who has all authority in heaven and on earth. Therefore, he sends his disciples to all nations, and the Gospel concludes with the promise of his abiding presence with his church until the end of the age.

37. The Most Important Links between Matthew and the Old Testament

Matthew is distinguished by building his Gospel upon a coherent theological reading of the Old Testament. He sees in Christ the fulfillment of Abraham's promise, the continuation of David's promise, the fulfillment of the image of the son in Hosea, and the completion of the prophecies of Isaiah, Micah, Zechariah, and Malachi. He also uses texts from Deuteronomy and the Psalms in interpreting Christ's life, temptation, and sufferings. Therefore, studying Matthew in isolation from the Old Testament loses a large part of the depth intended by the author, because many expressions and events in the Gospel become clearer when placed within the background of the Law, the Prophets, and the Psalms. It may be said that Matthew presents Christ as the key to reading the Old Testament and, at the same time, presents the Old Testament as a witness that explains Christ's identity and the message of his kingdom.

38. The Special Relationship between Matthew and Malachi

The relationship between Matthew and Malachi acquires special importance in studying the order of the books, because Malachi ends with the promise of sending Elijah and preparing the way, whereas Matthew begins with the account of Christ's approach and the appearance of John the Baptist. Malachi speaks of the Lord's coming to his temple; then Matthew presents Christ in the temple, cleansing it and exercising his authority over it, so that Christ appears as the fulfillment of prophetic expectation. Thus, the transition from Malachi to Matthew becomes a transition from expectation to fulfillment, from promise to presence, and from the voice of the prophet to the presence of the King.

39. The Relationship between Matthew and Zechariah

The book of Zechariah appears powerfully in the final events of the Gospel of Matthew, especially in Christ's entry into Jerusalem as the humble King riding on a donkey. The background of the thirty pieces of silver also appears in the story of Judas, as does the image of the shepherd who is struck and the sheep scattered on the night of Christ's arrest. These links reveal that Matthew sees the events of the cross as part of the ancient prophetic story of the rejected shepherd and king, rather than merely a series of political or judicial events.

40. Encyclopedic Summary

The Gospel of Matthew presents Christ as the promised King, the awaited Messiah, the Son of David, the Son of Abraham, the Son of God, and Immanuel, God with us. The Gospel begins with the history of Israel and ends with the whole world; it begins with the genealogy and ends with the commission; it begins with promise and ends with fulfillment; and it begins with a vulnerable child pursued by Herod and ends with the risen Christ, who declared that all authority in heaven and on earth had been given to him. Therefore, the Gospel's great message is that Jesus is the center of the entire story of salvation, that his kingdom came in his person, that the cross was the King's path to glory, that the resurrection proclaimed his victory, and that the church is called to carry the Gospel of this King to all nations, while his promise remains: “And behold, I am with you always, to the end of the age.”
