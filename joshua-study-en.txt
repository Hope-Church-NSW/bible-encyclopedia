Book of Joshua
Joshua — יְהוֹשֻׁעַ — Yehoshua
6
1. The Name of the Book and Its Meaning

The Hebrew name of the Book of Joshua is יְהוֹשֻׁעַ — Yehoshua, meaning “the LORD saves” or “Yahweh is salvation.” The name itself contains an important theological key for understanding the book, because the victories it recounts are not presented as an independent military achievement by Israel, but as the fulfillment of God’s promise and the completion of the saving work that began with the call of Abraham.

The Greek and English name Joshua is the same Hebrew name transmitted through Greek and Latin into modern languages. The name also carries particular significance in messianic studies, because the name Joshua/Jeshua comes from the same linguistic root associated with the name Jesus in the New Testament. This does not mean that Joshua is the Christ; rather, his own name conveys the idea of divine salvation.

The Book of Joshua comes directly after Deuteronomy, and therefore it should not be read as an independent story. Deuteronomy ends with Moses standing at the borders of the land, while Joshua begins with the divine word directing the new successor to cross the Jordan. In this way, the narrative moves from promise to the beginning of possession.

2. The author and the meaning of his name

The book does not explicitly state at its beginning that Joshua wrote the entire book, and therefore a distinction should be made between the book’s connection with Joshua’s person and testimony and the matter of its final composition.

The main character is Joshua son of Nun. His Hebrew name is יְהוֹשֻׁעַ — Yehoshua, meaning “the LORD saves.” His original name was Hoshea, according to Numbers 13:16, and Moses named him Joshua.

Joshua was from the tribe of Ephraim and served Moses from an early period. He was one of the two spies who stood firm in faith when the other spies feared entering the land. Therefore, his selection as leader after Moses is not surprising; through years in the wilderness, he had demonstrated that he could see God’s promise as greater than the enemy’s strength.

3. Joshua’s Birth and Death

The Bible does not state the year of Joshua’s birth, and therefore his birth date cannot be determined precisely.

As for his death, the book states that he died at the age of one hundred and ten years and was buried within the boundaries of his inheritance at Timnath-serah in the hill country of Ephraim.

This makes Joshua’s age at death different from Moses’ age, but more importantly, the Bible presents the man’s life within a transitional period: Moses leads the exodus and the wilderness, while Joshua leads the entry and possession.

4. For Whom Was the Book of Joshua Written?

The book was written within the context of the people of Israel, and it presents later generations with an interpretation of the history of possessing the land and distributing it among the tribes.

Therefore, it is not merely a record of wars, but a document affirming that the land in which Israel dwells is not the result of its military strength alone; rather, it is connected to God’s promise to the fathers and to the covenant.

At the same time, the book warns that possessing the land does not mean possessing the right to disobey. The people who entered the land by God’s power could lose the blessing if they violated the covenant.

5. Why Was the Book Written?

The purpose can be summarized in three themes:

Fulfillment of the promise of the land — proclamation of God’s leadership — a call for Israel to be faithful to the covenant.

After Israel had lived forty years in the wilderness, the question arises: Will the new generation be able to enter the land?

The answer presented by the book is yes, because God is faithful to His promise.

But the book adds an important warning: military success does not mean that everything is over; Israel must continue in obedience.

6. The relationship between Deuteronomy and Joshua

The relationship between the two books is very strong.

Deuteronomy ends with Moses on Mount Nebo.

And Joshua begins after the death of Moses.

Deuteronomy ends at the borders of the Jordan.

And Joshua begins with crossing the Jordan.

Deuteronomy ends with the promise of the land.

And Joshua begins with taking possession of the land.

Thus the transition can be summarized as follows:

Deuteronomy: “Cross over.”

Joshua: “Cross over.”

7. The general division of the book

The book can be divided into four major sections:

First: Entering the land

Joshua 1–5

The appointment of Joshua, crossing the Jordan, the setting up of the memorial stones, and circumcision at Gilgal.

Second: Conquering the land

Joshua 6–12

Jericho, Ai, the battle with the Gibeonites, and the major wars in Canaan.

Third: Dividing the land

Joshua 13–21

The distribution of the land among the tribes, the Levitical cities, and the cities of refuge.

Fourth: Renewing the covenant and the end of Joshua

Joshua 22–24

The problem of the altar, Joshua’s final address, the renewal of the covenant at Shechem, then the death of Joshua.

8. Joshua assumes leadership

After the death of Moses, the LORD says to Joshua:

“As I was with Moses, I will be with you. I will not fail you or forsake you.”

These words are the foundation on which the new leadership is built.

God does not tell Joshua that his success will come from his own strength or military expertise, but rather links success to the LORD’s presence with him.

For this reason, the call is repeated in chapter one:

“Be strong and courageous.”

But the strength and courage in Joshua do not mean confidence in oneself alone; rather, they mean trust in the word of God and in his presence.

9. Rahab and the Spies

Before crossing the Jordan, Joshua sends two men to spy out Jericho.

There they meet Rahab, a woman from Jericho, who hides them from the king’s men.

Rahab declares remarkable faith when she says that the people of the land have heard what the LORD has done for Israel, and that she has realized that the LORD is God.

Here we find a very important paradox:

Israel, who saw the works of God, could fear, while Rahab, who had only heard the reports, believed.

For this reason, Rahab becomes an example of faith, and she is mentioned in the New Testament in Matthew 1, Hebrews 11, and James 2.

10. Crossing the Jordan

The people arrive at the Jordan, and the river is in flood, but the priests carry the Ark of the Covenant and go ahead, and when their feet enter the water, the river’s flow stops.

And the people cross to the other side.

Here a clear comparison with Exodus appears:

The Red Sea in Exodus.

The Jordan in Joshua.

In both cases, water that seems to be an obstacle becomes a path because God leads his people.

11. The Twelve Stones

After the crossing, Joshua commands that twelve stones be taken from the middle of the Jordan and set up as a memorial.

The purpose is that the sons will ask in the future:

“What are these stones?”

Then the answer will be that the LORD cut off the waters of the Jordan before Israel.

And this idea is fundamental throughout the whole book:

Do not forget what God has done.

For biblical faith is not founded only on personal experience, but on a communal memory that passes on the works of God from generation to generation.

12. Circumcision at Gilgal

After entering the land, the new generation is circumcised, and then the people celebrate the Passover.

Here there is a strong connection between Exodus and Joshua.

Israel came out of Egypt by the Passover.

Now, after crossing the Jordan, they celebrate the Passover in the land.

Then the manna ceases.

As if the book declares:

The wilderness stage has ended, and the land stage has begun.

13. The Commander of the LORD’s Army

Before the fall of Jericho, Joshua meets a man carrying a sword, and when Joshua asks him, “Are you for us or for our enemies?” the answer comes:

“No, rather, I have come now as the commander of the LORD’s army.”

Then Joshua bows down.

This event is of great importance; it corrects the very concept of war.

Joshua does not treat the LORD as if he were a military commander subordinate to Israel.

Rather, the opposite is true:

Joshua and the army of Israel are the ones who must submit to the LORD’s leadership.

14. The Fall of Jericho

Jericho is the most famous event of the book.

Instead of the Israelites attacking the city in the usual military manner, God commands them to march around the city, and then on the seventh day the walls fall after the shouting of the people.

The goal is not to display military strength, but to proclaim that victory belongs to the LORD.

For this reason, Jericho becomes a foundational picture in the study of faith: what appears humanly impossible can be accomplished when the matter proceeds from God.

15. Achan’s Sin

Immediately after Jericho, disaster occurs at Ai.

The Israelite army initially fails, and Joshua discovers that Achan son of Carmi took from the spoil what had been forbidden.

Here a serious principle becomes apparent:

The sin of one person can affect the community.

The problem was not the amount Achan took, but that the disobedience occurred after a clear divine command.

The story reveals that Israel could not rely on its previous victory at Jericho and assume that it had become immune to the consequences of disobedience.

16. The Fall of Ai

After dealing with the sin, Israel triumphs over Ai.

The sequence is intentional:

Jericho — success.

Ai — failure because of disobedience.

Ai — success after the problem was dealt with.

Thus, the book teaches that spiritual battle is measured not only by human strength, but also by the state of one’s relationship with God.

17. The Gibeonites

The Gibeonites deceive Israel, and Israel enters into a covenant with them without asking the Lord.

This is one of the book’s most important moments because it reveals that failing to consult God can lead to a wrong decision even after great victories.

The mistake was not merely military; it was a decision-making error.

This is a powerful practical lesson: previous experience does not replace seeking God’s counsel in the present decision.

18. The Battle of the Gibeonites and the Halted Sun

After the Gibeonites enter into the covenant, Amorite kings attack them, and Joshua goes out to defend them.

In the context of the battle, the text states that the sun stood still and the moon stopped until the nation had taken vengeance on its enemies.

This text is among those most extensively discussed by interpreters and scholars from literary, historical, and phenomenological perspectives, but the basic message within the context is clear: God granted Israel an extraordinary victory in the battle.

The book uses the event to show that the Lord is sovereign over history, nature, and war.

19. The Kings of Canaan

Victories over a number of Canaanite kings follow one another.

It is important, however, not to understand the book as glorifying military power for its own sake; the text itself connects victory with God’s faithfulness to his promise and with the judgment of the peoples of Canaan within the biblical framework.

The book also does not present a complete and immediate occupation of every inch of the land. Rather, areas that had not been fully possessed appear later, making Joshua’s period the beginning of possession, requiring continued obedience and labor.

20. The Division of the Land

After the wars, the lengthy section concerning the division of the land begins.

This section may seem less exciting than Jericho and the battles, but it is of profound biblical importance.

Why?

Because the promise that began with Abraham has now become a specific land with names, cities, boundaries, and tribes.

The territories of Judah, Ephraim, Manasseh, Benjamin, and others appear.

Thus, the idea of the “Promised Land” changes from a promise heard to a geographical reality that can be identified on a map.

21. Caleb and His Inheritance

Caleb son of Jephunneh appears once again in the book.

He was one of the two spies who believed in God at Kadesh, and after decades he claims his share of the land.

He receives Hebron.

This is one of the book’s most beautiful personal narratives:

A man who believed the promise at forty, then remained steadfast until he reached eighty-five.

Faith here is not a momentary burst of enthusiasm; it is long perseverance until the time comes for the promise to be fulfilled.

22. Cities of Refuge

The book designates cities of refuge, to which anyone who has killed a person unintentionally can flee until he is tried.

This reveals another aspect of the law: justice does not mean immediate revenge, but requires distinguishing between intentional and unintentional killing and establishing due process.

The presence of cities of refuge in the land also shows that God is concerned not only with possessing the land, but with the kind of society that will live within it.

23. Levitical Cities

Cities are given to the Levites throughout the land.

This means that the service of God is not confined to one region; rather, the Levites are present throughout the various tribal territories.

Thus, teaching and worship remain connected with the life of the people throughout the land.

24. The Altar of the Tribes East of the Jordan

In Joshua 22, the tribes of Reuben and Gad and half the tribe of Manasseh build an altar by the Jordan.

The rest of the tribes think that they have rebelled, and the matter nearly turns into a civil war.

But it becomes clear that the altar was not for idolatrous worship; it was a witness that these tribes were part of the community of Israel despite being east of the Jordan.

The story reveals the importance of communication before passing judgment, and it also affirms the centrality of unified worship for the people.

25. Joshua’s Final Address

As Joshua grows old, he gathers the people and warns them against returning to the worship of the gods of the nations.

He reminds them of what the Lord has done.

He places before them the responsibility of preserving the covenant.

Here the book reaches the same theme with which Deuteronomy began:

Obedience.

The people have entered the land, but the real battle is not over; they must now preserve their relationship with God.

26. “But as for Me and My House, We Will Serve the Lord”

In Joshua 24:15, the famous statement appears:

“But as for me and my house, we will serve the Lord.”

This is not merely a beautiful statement about the family; it occurs within the context of covenant renewal at Shechem.

Joshua places a choice before the people:

Whom will you serve?

Then he declares his own choice and that of his household.

The text connects faith with decision and commitment, not merely with nominal affiliation.

27. Shechem and Covenant Renewal

At Shechem, the people gather before God, and Joshua reviews the history of salvation from Abraham to the entrance into the land.

This is very similar to the style of Deuteronomy.

The message is:

God’s history with you must become the foundation of your faithfulness to him.

The people do not worship an unknown god; they worship the God who called Abraham, brought Israel out of Egypt, preserved him in the wilderness, and gave him the land.

28. The Death of Joshua

The book ends with Joshua’s death at 110 years of age.

Here there is a clear parallel with the ending of Deuteronomy:

Moses dies → Joshua leads.

Then:

Joshua dies → a new generation must continue.

The book ends without saying that history has ended, because the great question has become: Will the people remain faithful after the death of the leader?

This question leads directly into the Book of Judges.

29. The Most Important Events in the Book

The major events can be summarized as follows:

The death of Moses.

The appointment of Joshua.

Crossing the Jordan.

The setting up of the twelve stones.

Circumcision and Passover at Gilgal.

The appearance of the Commander of the LORD's army.

The fall of Jericho.

The sin of Achan.

The fall of Ai.

The covenant with the Gibeonites.

The battles of Canaan.

The stopping of the sun in the context of Joshua's battle.

The division of the land.

Cities of refuge.

Levitical cities.

The renewal of the covenant at Shechem.

The death of Joshua.

30. Principal figures
Joshua son of Nun

The principal leader, Moses' successor, and the leader of Israel's entry into the land.

Rahab

The woman of Jericho who believed in the God of Israel and helped the spies, becoming part of the history of God's people.

Caleb

An example of steadfastness in faith and waiting for the fulfillment of the promise.

Achan

An example of the danger of disobedience and its effect on the community.

The Gibeonites

They represent a people who used deception to protect themselves, then entered into a covenant relationship with Israel.

Commander of the LORD's army

A figure who appears before the fall of Jericho to declare that the battle is under the LORD's command.

31. The principal message of the book

The message of Joshua may be summarized as follows:

God is faithful to His promise, and true victory comes through submission to His leadership; yet possessing the blessing must be accompanied by continued faithfulness to the covenant.

For the book does not say that Israel triumphed because it was the strongest people in the region.

Rather, it links victory to God's promise.

Nor does it say that possessing the land means that the people no longer bear responsibility.

Rather, it warns them against disobedience.

Therefore, the entire movement may be summarized as follows:

Promise → Crossing → Victory → Possession → Responsibility.

32. Christ in the Book of Joshua

The first important indication is Joshua's name itself.

The Hebrew name Yehoshua bears the meaning “the LORD saves,” and it is linguistically related to the name that appears in the New Testament for Christ.

Yet linguistic similarity alone is not proof that Joshua is Christ; what is more important is the theological pattern.

Joshua leads God's people from the wilderness into the land.

Christ, however, leads His people into complete salvation.

Joshua brings the people into the land of rest.

Christ, however, offers eternal rest and salvation.

This makes Joshua a preliminary historical type, not Christ Himself.

33. Joshua, Christ, and rest

One of the most beautiful connections with the New Testament is that the Epistle to the Hebrews speaks of Joshua and rest, then says that another rest still remains for God's people.

This means that entering Canaan was not the theological culmination of the entire concept of rest.

The land was a genuine rest for Israel, but it was not the final rest.

Thus Joshua becomes a sign of something greater:

Christ leads into a rest deeper than the rest of the land.

34. Rahab in Christ's genealogy

Rahab does not disappear from the story after the fall of Jericho.

In the Gospel of Matthew, Rahab appears in the genealogy of Jesus Christ.

This bears a powerful theological meaning: a woman who was outside Israel enters the story of salvation, then appears within the line that leads to Christ.

This accords with a broad biblical theme declaring that God's purpose of salvation extends also to the Gentiles.

35. The time of the book's events

The events of Joshua take place after the death of Moses, during Israel's entry into the land of Canaan and the settlement of the tribes there.

In the historical sequence, the book comes before the period of the Judges.

Determining the absolute years precisely, however, is connected with differing chronologies for the Exodus, the wilderness sojourn, and the entry into Canaan; therefore, in an encyclopedic study it is better to adhere to the sequence presented by the text and not to offer a disputed date as definitive.

36. The time of the book's composition

Tradition associates the book with Joshua and with the early phase of Israel's settlement in the land, with the possibility that early materials or records were used in the formation of the text.

Modern studies, however, differ in identifying the stages of the book's compilation and editing, and often associate it with the broader literary history known as the Deuteronomistic History, which in certain scholarly models includes the books of Joshua, Judges, Samuel, and Kings.

Yet this does not negate the historical and theological value of the text; rather, it means that the researcher distinguishes between the events narrated by the book and the process of recording and editing the text.

37. Why is the Book of Joshua so important?

Because Joshua represents a major turning point in the Bible:

From the wilderness to the land.

From promise to the beginning of fulfillment.

From Moses to Joshua.

From journey to settlement.

But the book carries a parallel warning:

Possession does not guarantee faithfulness.

For the land is a gift, but it is also a responsibility.

Victory is a gift, but it does not nullify obedience.

The great leader dies, but God's covenant continues.

Therefore, Joshua's final words lead us directly to the question that the Book of Judges will answer:

What happens when the generation that knew Joshua dies, and God's people begin to forget the LORD?

🔑 Summary of the Book of Joshua

The Hebrew name: יְהוֹשֻׁעַ — Yehoshua — “the LORD saves.”
The Arabic name: Joshua.
The English name: Joshua.
Traditional authorship: Associated with Joshua and his testimony, with scholarly discussion concerning the final composition.
Joshua’s birth: Unknown.
Death: 110 years.
Audience: The people of Israel and subsequent generations.
Setting: Canaan, the Jordan, Gilgal, and Shechem.
Subject: Entering and possessing the Promised Land and renewing the covenant.
Major events: Crossing the Jordan, Jericho, Ai, Rahab, the Gibeonites, the wars, the division of the land, covenant renewal, and Joshua’s death.
Characters: Joshua, Rahab, Caleb, Achan, and the Gibeonites.
Theological key: God fulfills his promise, leads his people into the land, and calls them to faithfulness after taking possession of it.

🔵 The theological trajectory from Genesis to Joshua
Genesis

Promise, covenant, and beginning.

↓

Exodus

Redemption, the exodus from slavery, and the presence of God.

↓

Leviticus

Holiness, atonement, and worship.

↓

Numbers

The wilderness, testing, unbelief, and God’s faithfulness.

↓

Deuteronomy

Covenant renewal and preparation to enter the land.

↓

Joshua

Fulfillment of the promise, crossing the Jordan, possession of the land, and covenant renewal.

And now we have reached a very important point in the history of the Bible:

Moses died.

Joshua led the people.

The land was given.

The covenant was renewed.

But the question was no longer: Can God give the land?

He had proved that he could.

The question became:

Can the people remain faithful to God after settling in the land?

Here a completely different stage begins in Israel’s history, and the Book of Judges introduces a painful and recurring cycle:

Sin → bondage → crying out → deliverance → rest → sin again.

From this point begins one of the most profound stages in the study of human nature and God’s faithfulness.
