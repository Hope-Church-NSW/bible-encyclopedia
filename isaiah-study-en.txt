Introduction to the Book of Isaiah

The central idea of the book: The Book of Isaiah is one of the greatest and theologically most comprehensive prophetic books; it brings together the holiness of God, human sin, the judgment of the nations, the preservation of the remnant, the hope of salvation, the Messiah King, the Servant of the LORD, the new covenant, the restoration of Israel, and the renewal of creation. The book begins with a vision of God seated on the throne: “Holy, holy, holy,” and ends with the scene of new heavens and a new earth; between the beginning and the end, divine revelation moves from judgment to redemption, and from human ruin to the glory of God.

5
1. Name of the book and its meaning

The Arabic name is:

Isaiah.

And the Hebrew name:

יְשַׁעְיָהוּ — Yəšaʿyāhū / Yesha‘yahu

The name consists of two basic elements:

ישע — yasha‘

meaning:

saves / salvation.

And the element:

יהו — yahu

which is an abbreviation connected with the name of the LORD, Yahweh.

Therefore, the name Isaiah bears a profound meaning:

“The LORD is salvation”
or: “Yahweh saves.”

This is not merely the linguistic meaning of the name, but is almost a theological title for the entire book.

For the book begins with the proclamation of judgment because of sin, but it returns continually to the truth that:

Salvation is from the LORD.

2. Who is Isaiah?

Isaiah is the prophet whose ministry was associated with the city of:

Jerusalem.

The Bible provides important information about him, saying:

“The vision of Isaiah son of Amoz, which he saw concerning Judah and Jerusalem in the days of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah, kings of Judah.”

Thus, his ministry extended across several royal reigns.

This makes Isaiah a witness to a highly sensitive period in Judah's history, when the great empires were pressing upon the kingdom:

Aram.

Northern Israel.

Assyria.

Then, later, the threat of Babylon.

3. The Meaning of the Name Amoz

Isaiah was:

the son of Amoz.

The Bible does not say that Amoz, Isaiah's father, was the prophet Amos.

This is a point that must be maintained.

The similarity between the two names in Arabic may cause confusion, but the Bible provides no evidence that they were the same person.

4. The Time of Isaiah's Ministry

The opening verse places the time of his ministry in the days of:

Uzziah.

Jotham.

Ahaz.

Hezekiah.

This period is generally estimated to have been in the eighth century before Christ.

Isaiah's ministry was especially connected with the events preceding the fall of the Northern Kingdom in 722 B.C. and with the great Assyrian crisis during the reign of Hezekiah.

5. The Year of Isaiah's Birth and Death

The Bible does not provide:

the year of Isaiah's birth.

Nor does it provide:

The year of his death.

Therefore, definitive dates must not be assigned.

Later Jewish and Christian traditions concerning his death exist, but these traditions are not part of the biblical text itself.

Therefore, in an encyclopedic study we write:

Isaiah's dates of birth and death are unknown from the Bible, and his prophetic ministry is thought to have extended through the eighth century BC.

6. For whom did Isaiah write?

The primary audience was:

Judah and Jerusalem.

But the book extends beyond Judah to address:

Israel.

The nations.

Kings.

Babylon.

Assyria.

Egypt.

Moab.

Damascus.

And others.

Then the address extends to:

The remnant of Israel.

And the peoples of the world.

Thus Isaiah moves from:

Jerusalem

To:

The nations

Then to:

Universal salvation.

7. Why did Isaiah write?

The purposes of the book can be summarized in six themes:

1. Exposing sin

Isaiah confronts the corruption of the people, leadership, and worship.

2. Proclaiming judgment

God is holy and does not overlook evil.

3. Warning the people

Do not place your trust in political alliances instead of God.

4. Proclaiming the remnant

Judgment will not mean that God's promise will fail.

5. Proclaiming the Messiah

The book presents the clearest and most profound Old Testament portraits of the coming Savior.

6. Proclaiming final salvation

Salvation does not end merely with a political return, but reaches:

New heavens and a new earth.

8. The book's central problem

The problem Isaiah addresses can be formulated as follows:

How does the holy God deal with a sinful people without nullifying the promises of His covenant?

The answer unfolds through three concepts:

Judgment.

The remnant.

Salvation.

God judges sin.

But He does not abandon His promise.

And from within judgment appears:

The Savior.

9. Division of the book

The best practical division of the book is:

Isaiah 1–12

Judgment and hope in Judah and Jerusalem.

Isaiah 13–27

Judgment of the nations and the proclamation of God's universal sovereignty.

Isaiah 28–35

Woes, warnings, and trust in God.

Isaiah 36–39

The Assyrian crisis, Hezekiah, and Sennacherib.

Isaiah 40–48

Comfort, God the Creator, and the fall of Babylon.

Isaiah 49–55

The Servant of the LORD, salvation, and redemption.

Isaiah 56–66

Final salvation, the new Jerusalem, and the renewal of creation.

This division reveals the overarching movement:

Judgment → comfort → redemption → glory.

10. Isaiah 1 — The indictment of Jerusalem

The book opens with a very powerful picture.

The people possess:

The sacrifices.

Feasts.

Prayers.

But their moral life is corrupt.

Here Isaiah presents one of the most important principles of the prophets:

External worship cannot cover internal rebellion.

God does not desire rituals separated from:

Justice.

Truth.

Repentance.

11. Isaiah 2 — The Mountain of the LORD

Isaiah sees a future in which rises:

The mountain of the house of the LORD.

And the nations turn toward it.

He declares:

“He will judge between the nations and will arbitrate for many peoples.”

Here we see a global dimension.

Salvation is not a project reserved for Judah alone.

Rather, God desires that it be:

Jerusalem, the center of the proclamation of truth to the nations.

12. Isaiah 6 — The Greatest Scenes of the Book

Among the most famous scenes in the Bible:

“In the year of King Uzziah’s death, I saw the Lord sitting on a high and exalted throne, and the hem of his robe filled the temple.”

Then:

“Holy, holy, holy, Lord of hosts.”

Here Isaiah sees:

The holiness of God.

Then he sees:

The uncleanness of humanity.

And he says:

“Woe is me! I am ruined, for I am a man of unclean lips.”

And this is an important theological point:

A true vision of God reveals humanity to itself.

13. Purification before Sending

After Isaiah confesses his sin, the seraph comes:

“This has touched your lips; your guilt is taken away, and your sin is forgiven.”

Then he hears:

“Whom shall I send? And who will go for us?”

And he answers:

“Here I am; send me.”

And the order is very important:

The vision of God.

Then:

Confession of sin.

Then:

The purification.

Then:

Service.

And this is a profound pattern for the servant’s calling.

14. Isaiah 7 — Immanuel

In the crisis of Ahaz, the prophecy comes:

“But the Lord himself will give you a sign: Look, the virgin will conceive and bear a son, and will call his name Immanuel.”

And the word:

עִמָּנוּאֵל — Immanuel

means:

“God with us.”

And in the Gospel of Matthew this text is directly connected to the birth of Christ.

Here the child’s very name becomes a theological proclamation:

Salvation is not merely God sending help; it is the proclamation of God’s presence with his people.

15. Isaiah 9 — The Child and the King

One of the greatest texts of the book:

“For to us a child is born, to us a son is given; and the government will be on his shoulders, and he will be called Wonderful Counselor, Mighty God, Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace.”

This verse gathers together:

The birth.

The kingdom.

Sovereignty.

Peace.

and the unique identity of the child.

For this reason it has become one of the most important Christological texts in the Bible.

16. Isaiah 11 — A Shoot Coming Forth from the Stump of Jesse

He says:

“A shoot will come forth from the stump of Jesse, and a branch will grow out of his roots.”

Note the name:

Not:

directly from David alone.

But rather:

From Jesse.

As if the prophet were returning the line to its root.

And when it seems that the house of David has been cut off, there appears:

The branch.

And this presents a picture:

the Messiah-King.

17. The Characteristics of the Messiah in Isaiah 11

The Spirit rests upon him:

the Spirit of the Lord.

the Spirit of wisdom.

the Spirit of understanding.

the Spirit of counsel.

the Spirit of strength.

the Spirit of knowledge.

the Spirit of the fear of the Lord.

And this describes a king unlike the kings of the earth.

He does not rule merely:

by military power.

But rather:

by justice, righteousness, and divine knowledge.

18. The Peace of Creation

In Isaiah 11 we read of:

the wolf with the lamb.

the leopard with the young goat.

the calf and the lion.

Then:

“For the earth will be full of the knowledge of the Lord as the waters cover the sea.”

Here the book moves from:

the Messianic King

To:

the renewal of creation.

19. Isaiah 14 — The Fall of Babylon

Babylon appears in the book as a symbol of arrogant human power.

And the book reveals that, however great empires may become:

They are not eternal.

For God is:

The Lord of history.

And He can raise up a nation.

Then bring it down.

20. Isaiah 36–39 — Sennacherib and Hezekiah

These chapters differ from the preceding poetic section because they present a historical narrative.

The Assyrian king:

Sennacherib

threatens:

Jerusalem.

and sends the Rabshakeh with a message seeking to shatter the people's faith.

But Hezekiah:

goes to the house of the LORD.

And lays the matter before God.

Here, one of the book's greatest principles appears:

The political crisis becomes a test of trust in God.

21. Sennacherib and Assyria

The Assyrian Empire was a formidable power in the eighth century BC.

And the fall of Samaria in:

722 BC.

was a sign of its strength.

Then it threatened Judah.

But Isaiah declared that:

Assyria itself is not God.

And the Assyrian king is not the sovereign ruler.

God can use empires for discipline, but He can also judge them when they become proud.

22. Isaiah 40 — “Comfort, comfort My people”

After long pages of judgment, the voice of the book changes:

“Comfort, comfort My people,” says your God.

Here a new stage begins.

The primary focus is no longer:

“Woe.”

But rather:

“Comfort.”

The God who declared judgment is the same One who declares:

Hope.

23. “A voice crying in the wilderness”

He says:

“A voice crying in the wilderness: Prepare the way of the Lord.”

And the Gospel applies this prophecy to:

John the Baptist.

Here one of Isaiah's most important patterns appears:

The King is coming.

Therefore:

The way must be prepared.

24. Isaiah 42 — The Servant of the LORD

He says:

“Behold! My Servant, whom I uphold; My Chosen One, in whom My soul delights. I have put My Spirit upon Him; He will bring forth justice to the nations.”

The Servant here does not come merely as a military king.

But rather:

Gentle.

Righteous.

Bearing truth.

A light to the nations.

And this image develops remarkably in the following chapters.

25. Isaiah 49 — Salvation for the Nations

The Servant's role is not limited to Israel.

But it says:

“It is too small a thing that You should be My Servant to raise up the tribes of Jacob and to restore the preserved ones of Israel; I have also made You a light to the nations, that You should be My salvation to the ends of the earth.”

Here the universal dimension of the Messiah appears.

Salvation begins in Israel.

But its goal is:

The ends of the earth.

26. Isaiah 52–53 — The Suffering Servant of the LORD

Here we reach the heart of the messianic prophecies in the book.

A Servant appears:

Despised.

Rejected.

Suffering.

Wounded.

But He bears the iniquities of others.

Among the most well-known texts are:

“But He was wounded for our transgressions, He was crushed for our iniquities.”

Then:

“And the LORD has laid on Him the iniquity of us all.”

This text became one of the most important passages understood by the early church as a prior declaration concerning:

The sufferings of Christ.

His death.

His bearing of sin.

27. Why is Isaiah 53 so important?

Because the Messiah in the Old Testament often appears as:

The kingdom.

But Isaiah presents a paradox:

The King is the Servant.

and

The victor suffers.

and

Salvation comes through suffering.

This idea becomes central to understanding the cross in the New Testament.

28. Isaiah 55 — Come to the Waters

After speaking of salvation, he says:

“All you who are thirsty, come to the waters.”

This is a free invitation.

He does not say:

Buy with gold.

Rather, he calls the thirsty.

Here salvation becomes:

Grace.

Not:

A human achievement.

29. Isaiah 60 — Light of the Nations

Among the greatest images:

“Arise, shine, for your light has come, and the glory of the Lord has risen upon you.”

Then:

“Nations shall come to your light, and kings to the brightness of your rising.”

Once again:

Jerusalem.

Light.

The nations.

Glory.

All of these move toward a universal vision of salvation.

30. Isaiah 61 — The Anointed Messiah

He says:

“The Spirit of the Lord God is upon me, because the Lord has anointed me to bring good news to the poor; he has sent me to bind up the brokenhearted, to proclaim liberty to the captives and release to the prisoners.”

In Luke 4, Christ reads this text in the synagogue.

Then it says:

“Today this Scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing.”

Here Christ declares himself in the light of Isaiah's prophecy.

31. Isaiah 65–66 — The New Creation

The book concludes with a grand vision:

“For behold, I create new heavens and a new earth.”

This is very important.

For the Bible's salvation is not merely:

The rescue of the soul from the body.

Rather, it moves toward:

the renewal of creation.

This idea later appears clearly in:

Revelation 21.

32. The Main Characters in the Book
Isaiah

The prophet and witness to God's message.

Uzziah

The king whose death was associated with Isaiah's vision.

Jotham

King of Judah.

Ahaz

King of Judah during the Syro-Israelite crisis.

Hezekiah

The king of Judah who faced the threat of Assyria.

Sennacherib

The king of Assyria who besieged Judah.

The Rabshakeh

The Assyrian spokesman who sought to subdue Jerusalem psychologically and spiritually.

Cyrus

He appears in the later part of the book as the instrument God uses in the context of Babylon's fall and the people's return.

The Servant of the Lord

The central prophetic figure in several passages, reaching its climax in Isaiah 52–53.

33. The Most Important Events in the Book

Isaiah's call.

The vision of the throne.

Judgment upon Judah.

The crisis of Ahaz.

The prophecy of Immanuel.

Prophecies of the royal Messiah.

The fall of the nations.

Sennacherib's threat against Jerusalem.

Hezekiah's prayer.

Deliverance of Jerusalem.

The prediction of the exile and return.

The appearance of the Servant of the Lord.

The prophecy of the suffering servant.

The call to salvation.

Light of the nations.

The new heavens and the new earth.

34. The Great Theological Message

Isaiah's theology can be summarized in a sequence:

God is holy.

↓

Humanity is sinful.

↓

Judgment is real.

↓

But God preserves a remnant.

↓

The Messiah comes.

↓

The Messiah brings salvation.

↓

Salvation reaches the nations.

↓

God renews creation.

This makes Isaiah one of the most comprehensive books in terms of biblical theology.

35. Isaiah and Christ

It is difficult to study Christ in the New Testament without Isaiah.

For the book presents multiple portraits of the Messiah:

Immanuel.

The royal child.

The branch.

The righteous king.

The Servant of the Lord.

Light of the nations.

The one anointed by the Spirit.

The suffering servant.

These images do not all appear in one simple portrait; rather, they gradually come together until the New Testament reads them in the person of:

Jesus Christ.

36. Isaiah and the Gospel

Isaiah's echo can be seen directly in the Gospel:

Matthew 1:23 — Immanuel.

Matthew 3:3 — a voice crying in the wilderness.

Matthew 12:17–21 — the Servant of the Lord.

Luke 4:17–21 — Isaiah 61.

John 12:38–41 — Isaiah 6 and the revelation of Christ's glory.

Acts 8:26–35 — Isaiah 53 and the Ethiopian eunuch's reading.

Thus, Isaiah becomes one of the most important bridges between:

the Old Testament and Christ in the New Testament.

37. Isaiah and the Church

Isaiah does not speak only about Israel.

Rather, he sees:

The nations.

Light.

Salvation.

This helps in understanding the Church's mission to the world.

For the divine purpose was not for God's revelation to remain confined within geographical boundaries.

Rather, that it should reach:

“to the ends of the earth.”

38. What Does the Book of Isaiah Indicate to Us?

It indicates that:

God's holiness reveals human sinfulness.

Judgment does not mean the failure of God's promise.

God preserves the remnant.

Salvation is from the LORD.

The Messiah is the center of hope.

The Messiah is both King and Servant at the same time.

Suffering can be a path to salvation in God's purpose.

Salvation extends to the nations.

History is not random.

Empires are under God's sovereignty.

The end is not destruction but renewal.

39. The Most Important Message the Book Presents

The holy God does not overlook sin, yet he does not abandon his mercy; from the midst of judgment, he preserves a remnant and sends the Messiah, who comes as King and suffering Servant, bears the sins of many, and becomes a light to the nations, until God's purpose reaches its end in the salvation of his people and the renewal of creation.

Element Summary
Name: Isaiah
Hebrew: יְשַׁעְיָהוּ — Yesha‘yahu
Meaning of the name: The Lord is salvation
Author: Isaiah son of Amoz, according to the text and tradition
Period of ministry: the eighth century B.C.
Kings: Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, Hezekiah
Primary setting: Judah and Jerusalem
Addressees: Judah, Israel, and the nations
Problem: sin, judgment, and political and spiritual crisis
Theme: God's holiness and salvation
Central figure: the Lord, then the Messiah and the Servant of the Lord
Major event: the crisis of Sennacherib and Jerusalem
Major vision: Isaiah 6
Major prophecy: Isaiah 53
Major messianic figure: the Servant of the Lord
Universal dimension: light to the nations
The end: new heavens and a new earth
Key word: salvation
Message: “The Lord is salvation”

🔑 The Sentence That Summarizes the Book of Isaiah

“Isaiah is the book of God's holiness and salvation: it begins with an unclean man standing before the throne and crying, ‘Woe is me,’ and ends with a renewed world proclaiming God's glory; between the two scenes, God reveals that human salvation will come neither from kings, nor alliances, nor human strength, but from the Messiah, who is Immanuel, the Branch, the King, the suffering Servant of the Lord, and the light to the nations, who bears sin in order to lead God's people to salvation and the renewal of creation.”
