Book of Deuteronomy
Deuteronomy — דְּבָרִים — Devarim
6
1. The Name of the Book and Its Meaning

The Hebrew name of the Book of Deuteronomy is דְּבָרִים — Devarim, meaning "words" or "sayings," taken from the opening of the book: "These are the words which Moses spoke to all Israel." This Hebrew name is very important because it reveals that the book is, in essence, Moses' final address to his people, not merely a mechanical repetition of what was already stated in Exodus, Leviticus, and Numbers.

As for the Greek name Δευτερονόμιον — Deuteronomion, from which the English name Deuteronomy is derived, it is connected to the meaning "the second law" or "repetition of the law." However, this expression needs to be understood precisely, because Deuteronomy does not present a law different from the law of Moses, but rather re-presents the covenant and the commandments before the new generation under new circumstances.

The generation that came out of Egypt mostly died in the wilderness, and the generation now standing in the plains of Moab did not itself experience all the events of the exodus. Therefore Moses gathers together the history of the past, the commandments of the covenant, and the warning about the future, then sets the people before a clear choice: life and blessing through obedience, or death and curse through disobedience.

2. The author and the meaning of his name

Jewish and Christian tradition ascribes the Book of Deuteronomy to Moses, who appears in the book as the main speaker addressing Israel before its entry into the promised land. The beginning of the book affirms that Moses spoke to all Israel, while the life of Moses concludes in the final chapter with his death on Mount Nebo.

The name of Moses in Hebrew is מֹשֶׁה — Mosheh, the name associated in the Book of Exodus with his being drawn out of the water. By the end of his life, Moses had become more than merely the leader of the exodus; he became the mediator of the covenant, the lawgiver, the prophet, and the teacher who prepares the new generation for life in the land.

Nevertheless, the book makes clear that Moses himself will not enter the land. This paradox is of great significance: the man who led Israel from Egypt to the borders of Canaan will not cross over with them into it, but the word of God and His covenant continue after his departure.

3. The birth and death of Moses

Scripture does not provide a specific calendar date for the birth of Moses, so no certain year of birth can be given. As for his death, the Book of Deuteronomy states that he died at 120 years old, and that his eyes were not dim, nor his natural vigor abated.

The death of Moses took place on Mount Nebo in the land of Moab, after the Lord showed him the land which He had promised to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. Thus Deuteronomy becomes the book that joins together the end of Moses' ministry and the beginning of the new stage in the history of Israel.

4. For Whom Was the Book Written?

The primary audience is the new generation of the children of Israel who were about to cross the Jordan and enter the promised land.

This generation differs from the generation that came out of Egypt, for many of the children of the old generation had died because of unbelief and rebellion. Therefore Moses retells before the new generation the account of past events, not merely to remind them of history, but so that they might learn from the mistakes of their fathers.

Here the value of the book for later generations appears: the history of Israel is not merely a past to be recounted, but a lesson that must influence future obedience.

5. Why was the Book of Deuteronomy written?

The primary purpose is the renewal of the covenant and the preparation of Israel to enter the land.

The wilderness stage had almost ended, and the people were now at the borders of Canaan. Therefore they needed a new understanding of their responsibility: they would no longer be a community traveling in the wilderness around the tent of meeting, but a people who would live in a land with cities, agriculture, property, economy, kings, priests, and judicial systems.

For this reason the book brings together:

History — Law — Worship — Leadership — Land — Covenant — Future.

And the axis that connects them all is:

You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, and with all your soul, and with all your strength.

This statement, found in Deuteronomy 6:5, becomes one of the most important theological texts in the entire Bible.

6. The relationship between Numbers and Deuteronomy

The Book of Numbers ends with Israel in the plains of Moab opposite Jericho, while Deuteronomy begins from almost the same location.

But the difference between the two books is great.

In Numbers we see:

The people walking in the wilderness.

But in Deuteronomy we see:

The people preparing to enter the land.

In Numbers the focus was on testing, rebellion, and judgment.

But Deuteronomy focuses on remembrance, obedience, renewal of the covenant, and preparation for the future.

Thus Deuteronomy becomes, as it were, the theological threshold between the wilderness and the promised land.

7. The general division of the book

The Book of Deuteronomy can be divided into six main units:

First: The first historical address

Deuteronomy 1–4

A review of Israel's journey from Horeb to the plains of Moab, with emphasis on the mistakes of the previous generation.

Second: Renewal of the covenant and the commandments

Deuteronomy 5–11

The Ten Commandments, the Shema, love for God, and the warning against forgetting the Lord.

Third: Legislation for life in the land

Deuteronomy 12–26

Worship, priests, prophets, judgment, kings, war, family, society, and justice.

Fourth: Blessings, curses, and renewal of the covenant

Deuteronomy 27–30

The choice between life and death, and between obedience and disobedience.

Fifth: The last words of Moses

Deuteronomy 31–33

The handing over of leadership to Joshua, the Song of Moses, and the blessing of the tribes.

Sixth: The death of Moses

Deuteronomy 34

Moses' ascent to Mount Nebo, his sight of the land, his death, and his burial.

8. Review of the wilderness journey — Deuteronomy 1–4

Moses begins by reviewing the history of the journey from Horeb, through the wilderness, arriving at the borders.

But Moses does not present history merely as a list of events; rather, he interprets the events theologically.

For when he speaks about the spies and the people's refusal to enter the land, he focuses not only on what happened, but on the fact that the problem was the people's lack of faith in the word of God.

And here the book presents a very important principle:

Biblical history is not only what happened, but why it happened, and what we must learn from it.

9. The Ten Commandments — Deuteronomy 5

Moses proclaims the Ten Commandments again before the new generation.

And the event is important because the covenant is not transferred automatically from one generation to another merely by birth; rather, every generation must know God, His covenant, and His commandments.

For the new generation needs to hear for itself:

"I am the Lord your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery."

And here we notice an important theological order:

Salvation first, then the commandment.

God did not say to Israel: Obey Me so that I may bring you out of Egypt; rather, He brought them out first, and then gave them the commandments of the covenant.

10. The Shema — the Heart of Deuteronomy

In Deuteronomy 6 appears one of the greatest texts in the Bible:

"Hear, O Israel: The Lord our God, the Lord is one. You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your strength."

The Hebrew word שְׁמַע — Shema‘ means "hear," but in the biblical context it carries more than mere audible hearing; it is a call to the hearing that results in response and obedience.

Therefore, the Shema can be considered the heart of the book of Deuteronomy:

God is one → Israel hears → Israel loves → Israel obeys.

And Christ took this very text and placed it at the heart of His answer to the question about the greatest commandment.

11. Do Not Forget the Lord

Among the most frequently repeated warnings in Deuteronomy:

"Take care lest you forget the Lord."

And this warning becomes more important when the people enter a good land, eat and are satisfied, build houses, and their livestock and wealth multiply.

The problem is not only poverty or hunger; sometimes blessing itself is a test of faith.

In the wilderness the people needed God for manna and water, but in the land a person may think that he can rely on his own strength and wealth.

Therefore the book warns that blessing may lead to forgetting the Giver of the blessing.

12. The Worship of God Alone — Deuteronomy 12

The book emphasizes the centrality of the worship of the Lord and the prohibition of idolatrous worship.

For Israel will enter a land full of different religious and cultural practices, and therefore it cannot live in a way that makes the worship of the Lord merely one element among the elements of life.

God demands complete loyalty.

And here appears one of the fundamental concepts in Deuteronomy:

The covenant is not an agreement between two equal parties, but a covenantal relationship in which God demands from His people exclusive faithfulness to Him.

13. Prophets and Leadership

Deuteronomy also addresses the subject of the prophet, the priest, the judge, and the king.

And the book sets limits on human authority; for the king himself does not become above the law, the prophet cannot speak in the name of other gods, and the judge must not judge with bribery or partiality.

And this reveals that the Scripture presents a model of authority in which the ruler, the priest, the prophet, and the people are all subject to the word of God.

14. The King in Israel

Although Israel had not yet appointed a king in the time of Moses, Deuteronomy foretells a situation in which the people will ask for a king.

And it is remarkable that the king, according to Deuteronomy, is not to be absolute in authority. Rather, he is required to read the law, fear the Lord, and keep the commandments.

And this lays an important theological foundation for understanding the history of the kings of Israel later, because the evaluation of the kings in the books of Samuel and Kings will often occur on the basis of their faithfulness to the Lord and His covenant.

15. Love and Social Justice

Deuteronomy is not limited to worship and rituals, but deals with the poor, the widows, the orphans, the stranger, and the slave.

And the theological reason is profound: Israel itself was a stranger and a slave in Egypt, and therefore it must remember the experience of weakness when it exercises authority within its own land.

And here appears a very important principle:

Whoever has experienced the mercy of God must become merciful to others.

For spiritual memory must produce ethical conduct.

16. Choosing Life and Death — Deuteronomy 27–30

The message of the book reaches its climax in the subject of blessing and curse.

The people stand before a clear choice:

Life or death.

Blessing or curse.

Obedience or disobedience.

And the famous statement in Deuteronomy 30:19 says:

"I have set before you life and death, blessing and cursing. Therefore choose life, that both you and your descendants may live."

This is not merely moral advice, but a declaration that Israel's choice of its path will determine its relationship to the covenant, the land, and the blessing.

17. The Covenant and the New Generation

Among the most important ideas of Deuteronomy is that the covenant is not treated as an old memory belonging only to the fathers.

Moses stands before the new generation as if he were saying:

You are now before God.

For the God who dealt with your fathers is the God who demands faithfulness from you as well.

And this makes Deuteronomy a very powerful book on the subject of the transmission of faith between generations.

Faith is not transmitted by genes, nor merely by a name, nor merely by belonging to a community; rather, the new generation must know the Lord and choose to obey Him.

18. Joshua Assumes Leadership

In Deuteronomy 31 Moses hands over the leadership to Joshua son of Nun.

And here appears one of the most beautiful pictures of the transfer of ministry in the Bible.

Moses says to the people:

"Be strong and of good courage. Do not fear nor be afraid of them, for the Lord your God goes with you. He will not leave you nor forsake you."

Then he directs his words to Joshua himself.

And thus the future of Israel does not depend on the continuation of Moses, because the leader may change, but the presence of God does not change.

19. The Song of Moses

In Deuteronomy 32 Moses presents a long song that bears witness against Israel and warns of the future.

The song reviews the faithfulness of God, then describes the people's tendency to stray and turn to idolatry.

And the song carries a prophetic perspective; for it speaks not only about the past, but warns of the future in which the people may forget their God after entering the land.

And thus the song becomes a theological memory that preserves the testimony of the covenant for the generations.

20. Moses' Blessing of the Tribes

In Deuteronomy 33 Moses blesses the tribes of Israel before his death.

And this reminds us of Jacob's way in Genesis 49 when he blessed his sons before his death.

And thus there is an intentional similarity between the end of Jacob's life and the end of Moses' life:

Jacob blesses the tribes before his departure, and Moses blesses the tribes before his entering into rest.

And this makes Deuteronomy a strong link between the story of the patriarchs and the story of the nation.

21. The Death of Moses on Mount Nebo

The book reaches its emotional climax in chapter 34.

Moses ascends Mount Nebo, and the Lord shows him the land from afar.

But he does not enter it.

Then Moses dies in the land of Moab.

The book describes his death in a way that reveals God's honoring of His servant: he was 120 years old, yet his eyes were not dim and his vigor had not departed.

Thus ends the life of the man who confronted Pharaoh, crossed the sea, received the law, and led the people in the wilderness.

But the story does not end.

Moses dies, Joshua leads, and the promise continues.

22. Who Buried Moses?

The text says that the Lord buried him in the valley in the land of Moab, and that no one has known his grave to this day.

This is one of the most mysterious details at the end of the book.

The book does not turn Moses' grave into a center of pilgrimage or worship; rather, it conceals its location.

This accords with the book's overall emphasis: glory belongs to God, not to the human figure.

23. Did Moses Write the Final Chapter?

This is an important question in biblical study.

Deuteronomy ends with an account of Moses' death and burial, and it is natural for the scholar to observe that Moses could not have written the description of his own death himself in the direct sense.

Therefore, Jewish and Christian tradition holds that the final chapter was completed under God's inspiration by a later person, often attributed to Joshua in some traditions, whereas modern scholarship considers the text to have undergone a process of composition and editing.

But this observation does not alter Moses' centrality in the book or his close connection to it.

24. The Main Figures in the Book
Moses

The central figure, prophet, mediator, and teacher, who delivers his final speeches and renews the covenant before the people.

Joshua

Moses' successor, who will lead Israel into the Promised Land.

Aaron

He appears in the recollection of the history of leadership and priesthood, although he died before the final events of Deuteronomy.

The New Generation

Not a single figure, but the book's collective protagonist; it is the generation standing before the decisive question: will it be faithful to the covenant, or will it walk in the way of its ancestors?

The Lord

He is the true central figure throughout the book; for He is the maker of the covenant, the source of the commandment, the leader of the people, and the keeper of the promise.

25. The Most Important Message of Deuteronomy

The central message may be summarized as follows:

Remember what God has done, love Him with all your heart, keep His covenant, and choose life.

Deuteronomy teaches that the most dangerous thing that can happen to the people is not merely facing a powerful enemy, but forgetting the Lord after God gives them rest and blessing.

Therefore, words such as these are repeated:

Remember — hear — keep — do not forget — love — follow — take heed.

These words reveal the heart of the book.

26. Christ in Deuteronomy

Deuteronomy is one of the Old Testament books most prominent in Christ's teaching.

When Christ was asked about the greatest commandments, He quoted Deuteronomy 6:5:

“You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your strength.”

In Christ's temptation in the wilderness, the answers He used before the devil came from Deuteronomy, especially:

“Man shall not live by bread alone.”

This is of great importance; Christ, as the Son of God, enters the wilderness and confronts temptation, whereas Israel had repeatedly failed in the wilderness. Thus, Christ appears in the New Testament as the faithful Israelite who obeys where Israel failed.

27. The Prophet Like Moses

One of the most important prophecies in Deuteronomy is Deuteronomy 18:15:

“The Lord your God will raise up for you a prophet like me from among you, from your brothers. You shall listen to him.”

This text acquired great importance in Christian interpretation, and the New Testament applies it to Christ in the Book of Acts.

Christ is not merely a prophet in the usual sense; rather, He fulfills in a greater way the model of the prophet who speaks God's word, connects the people to God, and leads into the New Covenant.

Here Moses himself becomes a preliminary type of Christ, with the difference that Christ is greater than Moses, as the Epistle to the Hebrews affirms.

28. Deuteronomy and the New Testament

Deuteronomy has a very strong presence in the New Testament.

It is one of the Old Testament books most frequently quoted by Christ and the apostles.

Its influence appears in:

Christ's temptation — the greatest commandment — the concept of covenant — the prophet like Moses — blessing and curse — obedience — love of God.

Therefore, Deuteronomy should not be studied merely as the conclusion of the Torah, but as one of the great keys to understanding New Testament thought.

29. The Time of the Book's Events

The events of Deuteronomy take place in the plains of Moab east of the Jordan, shortly before Israel enters the land of Canaan.

The period is the end of the forty years in the wilderness, after the death of most of the generation that came out of Egypt and before crossing the Jordan under Joshua's leadership.

Thus, the book may be placed in the final stage of Moses' life, when the people stood one step away from fulfilling the promise God had made to the fathers.

30. The Time of the Book's Writing

Jewish and Christian tradition connects the origin of the book with Moses' final speeches in the plains of Moab.

Modern scholarship, however, differs greatly in determining the date of the book's composition and formulation. Some associate certain features with an early stage of Israel's history, while others consider the book in its present form to have passed through long stages of collection and editing, particularly in light of its relationship to the religious reform during Josiah's reign and to the Deuteronomistic traditions in the Books of Kings.

Therefore, in encyclopedic study, it is preferable to distinguish between:

the origin of the speeches associated with Moses,

and the process of composing, editing, and bringing the text to its final form.

This allows the text to be studied faithfully without reducing a complex historical question to an oversimplified and inaccurate answer.

31. Why Is Deuteronomy So Important?

Because Deuteronomy is the book of memory, covenant, and choice.

It says to the people:

Remember Egypt.

Remember the wilderness.

Remember the fall of your fathers.

Remember God's faithfulness.

Remember that you were a slave.

Then it says:

And now choose how you will live in the land.

This makes Deuteronomy more than a book of law; it is a spiritual address to the generations, linking history to the present, the present to the future, blessing to obedience, and identity to the love of God.

🔑 Summary of Deuteronomy

Hebrew name: דְּבָרִים — Devarim — “Words / Sayings.”
Greek name: Deuteronomion — “Second Law / Repetition of the Law.”
Arabic name: Deuteronomy.
Traditional author: Moses.
Moses' birth: historically unknown with precision.
His death: 120 years, according to Deuteronomy 34:7.
Those addressed: the new generation of the Israelites.
Location: the plains of Moab, east of the Jordan.
Period: the end of the forty years, before entering the Promised Land.
Theme: covenant renewal, love, obedience, remembrance, and choice.
Key events: Moses’ speeches, the repetition of the commandments, the Shema, covenant renewal, the appointment of Joshua, the Song of Moses, the blessing of the tribes, and the death of Moses.
Characters: Moses, Joshua, Aaron, and the new generation.
Theological key: Remember the Lord, love him, obey his commandment, and choose life.

Genesis

Promise, covenant, and beginning.

Exodus

Redemption, the exodus from slavery, and God’s dwelling among his people.

↓

Leviticus

Holiness, atonement, and how the people live in God’s presence.

↓

Numbers

The wilderness, testing, rebellion, and God’s faithfulness to the promise.

Deuteronomy

Covenant renewal and preparation to enter the Promised Land.

Thus, the Pentateuch concludes with a truly profound truth:

God began with a promise to Abraham, redeemed the descendants of the promise from Egypt, caused his presence to dwell among them, led them through the wilderness, preserved them despite their failures, and then brought them to stand at the borders of the land he had promised to their fathers.

But the story did not end there.

After the death of Moses, the great historical and theological question arises:

Who will lead the people now? And can the new generation do what the previous generation failed to do and actually enter the Promised Land?

This leads us directly to the Book of Joshua, where the story moves from the promise the people heard to the land the people will begin to possess.
