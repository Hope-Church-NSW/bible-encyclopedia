The Gospel of John
1. Name of the book and its meaning

The name “John” means “the Lord is gracious” or “the Lord shows mercy,” and it is a common name in the New Testament. The Gospel is traditionally associated with John son of Zebedee, one of the Twelve and a disciple of Christ who was especially close to Him, and the book presents a profound theological witness concerning the person of Jesus Christ, His divine identity, and His saving work.

2. The Author and His Character

Christian tradition attributes the Gospel of John to John son of Zebedee, the fisherman of Galilee and one of the sons of Zebedee, whom Christ called together with his brother James. John belonged to the circle closest to Christ, and appeared with Peter and James in important events such as the raising of Jairus’s daughter, the Transfiguration, and Gethsemane. The Gospel refers to “the disciple whom Jesus loved” as a witness to the events, and connects this disciple with the testimony on which the Gospel is based.

3. For Whom Was the Gospel Written?

It appears that John wrote for a wider Christian audience, from Jews and Gentiles, with the aim of strengthening faith in Christ and showing His truth clearly. Therefore, he does not rely on explaining Jewish details in the same way we find in Mark, but uses selected events and dialogues to lead the reader to believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God.

4. The Reason for Writing the Gospel

John himself clearly declares his purpose at the end of the Gospel: “But these have been written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life in His name.” Therefore, the Gospel is not merely a historical biography of Christ, but an intentional testimony that leads to faith and life. John chose a number of signs, works, and discourses that reveal the identity of Christ and call the reader to take a position toward Him.

5. The Time of Writing the Gospel

Many scholars consider it likely that the Gospel of John was written in the late first century AD, with differences over determining the precise date. A large number of studies place it in the period around 90 AD, while other views propose earlier dates. The Gospel itself does not provide a specific date for its writing, so determining the year should be treated as a historical estimate, not an explicit biblical fact.

6. The Main Theme

The central theme in the Gospel of John is Jesus Christ, the Son of God, the eternal Word who became flesh, and who grants eternal life to everyone who believes in Him. John presents Christ as the source of life, light, truth, the way, and the resurrection, and continually leads the reader from the sign to faith, and from faith to life.

7. The Eternal Word

John opens his Gospel with one of the deepest theological statements in the Bible: “In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.” Here John does not begin from Bethlehem, but goes back to before creation, in order to proclaim Christ’s eternity, His relationship with the Father, and His sharing in the divine nature.

Then he says that “the Word became flesh and dwelt among us,” and by this John connects the eternity of the Son with the incarnation. The Christ who appeared in history is Himself the eternal Word who was in the beginning.

8. Christ and Life

Life is one of the central concepts in John; Christ is the source of life, and He grants the human person eternal life that goes beyond the mere continuation of physical existence. This truth appears in His saying, “I am the resurrection and the life,” and in His promise that whoever believes in Him has eternal life.

Therefore, John's miracles are called “signs,” because they aim not only to demonstrate Christ's power, but also to point to his identity and to the life he came to give.

9. Christ and the Light

John declares that the life was the light of humanity, and that the light shines in the darkness. Christ appears as the light of the world, who reveals God and brings human beings out of the darkness of sin and ignorance.

The conflict between light and darkness recurs throughout the Gospel; accepting Christ means coming into the light, whereas rejecting him means remaining in darkness.

10. The Seven Signs

John highlights a number of major miracles as “signs” that reveal Christ's glory. Among the most prominent are the turning of water into wine, the healing of the royal official's son, the healing of the sick man at the Pool of Bethesda, the feeding of the five thousand, walking on the sea, the healing of the man born blind, and the raising of Lazarus.

The purpose does not end with the miracle itself; each sign leads to a deeper revelation about the person of Christ, until the signs reach their climax in the raising of Lazarus, after which the narrative moves toward Christ's death and resurrection.

11. “I Am”

One of the most prominent characteristics of John's Gospel is Christ's use of the expressions “I am.” He declares: “I am the bread of life,” “I am the light of the world,” “I am the gate of the sheep,” “I am the good shepherd,” “I am the resurrection and the life,” “I am the way, and the truth, and the life,” and “I am the true vine.”

These declarations reveal that Christ does not merely offer spiritual gifts, but declares that he himself is the source of bread, light, life, salvation, care, and the way to the Father.

12. The New Birth

In his conversation with Nicodemus, Christ declares that a person must be born from above, or born again, in order to see the kingdom of God. This teaching reveals that entry into God's kingdom does not come through religious affiliation, knowledge, or social status, but through God's work in a person.

The new birth is associated with faith in Christ and the work of the Holy Spirit, whom Christ compares to the wind that blows wherever it wishes.

13. The Samaritan Woman

The story of the Samaritan woman reveals the breadth of Christ's mission beyond the traditional boundaries between Jews and Samaritans. Christ speaks to her about living water and worship in spirit and truth, and then reveals himself to her as the Messiah.

The story ends with the woman's testimony to the people of her town. Many believe because of her testimony, and then others believe because they hear Christ himself. Thus, the story becomes a model for the movement of faith from personal encounter to witness to others.

14. The Bread of Life

After feeding the five thousand, Christ declares: “I am the bread of life.” Here John moves the reader from material bread to the spiritual reality to which the miracle points; human beings need Christ himself in order to live the true life.

The discourse reveals that the greatest gift is not bread that perishes, but the life granted by the Son of God.

15. The Good Shepherd

Christ presents himself as the good shepherd who knows his sheep and lays down his life for them. This image is connected with the shepherd imagery of the Old Testament, especially in Psalm 23 and Ezekiel 34, but in John it reaches a new climax, because the shepherd does not merely lead the sheep; he lays down his life for them.

Thus shepherding is connected with the cross; Christ's love is revealed in his knowledge of his people and in his willingness to die for them.

16. The Raising of Lazarus

The raising of Lazarus represents the greatest of the signs recorded in the Gospel before the cross. Christ arrives at the tomb four days after Lazarus's death, then calls the dead man, and he comes out alive.

Before the miracle, Christ declares: “I am the resurrection and the life.” Therefore, the raising of Lazarus becomes a sign pointing to Christ himself as the source of life, while at the same time preparing for the events that will lead to his death and resurrection.

17. Christ's Love

Christ's love reaches its climax in the thirteenth chapter and what follows, where he loves his own “to the end.” He washes his disciples' feet, then commands them to love one another as he has loved them.

In John, love cannot be separated from the cross; love is not merely a feeling, but the giving of oneself for others.

18. Christ and the Holy Spirit

John presents extensive teaching about the Holy Spirit, especially in Christ's final discourses. He calls him “the Advocate” and declares that he will come to teach the disciples, remind them of Christ's words, bear witness to him, and guide them into the truth.

Thus the Gospel reveals the continuing presence and work of God in the lives of believers after Christ's ascension. The Holy Spirit does not replace Christ, but bears witness to him and glorifies Christ.

19. Christ and the Father

John repeatedly affirms the unique relationship between the Son and the Father. Christ declares: “I and the Father are one,” and also says: “Whoever has seen me has seen the Father.”

This does not mean that the Son is the Father with respect to personhood, but reveals their unique divine unity and the relationship of the Son to the Father. Therefore, Christ's declaration about himself was a primary reason for the escalating conflict with the Jewish leaders.

20. The Last Supper and Foot Washing

John presents the Last Supper differently from the Synoptic Gospels, focusing especially on Christ's washing of his disciples' feet and on the lengthy discourses that preceded the cross.

The foot washing reveals that the Lord and Teacher became a servant, and that leadership in God's kingdom is founded on love and self-giving. Christ then connects this conduct with his new commandment: “that you love one another as I have loved you.”

21. The Way, the Truth, and the Life

Christ declares to his disciples: “I am the way, and the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me.” This is one of the clearest statements of messianic revelation and Christian theology.

Christ is not merely someone who explains the way to God, but is the way itself; he is not merely a teacher of the truth, but is the truth; and he is not merely a giver of life, but is the life.

22. The High Priestly Prayer

In the seventeenth chapter, Christ prays to the Father for himself, his disciples, and all those who will believe in him through their preaching. The prayer reveals the unity of the relationship between the Father and the Son, Christ's concern to preserve his disciples, and his request that believers may be one.

This prayer forms a theological bridge between the Last Supper and the cross, as Christ enters his suffering while carrying his people before the Father.

23. The Cross

John presents the cross in a way that shows Christ as sovereign over events even during his suffering. When the soldiers come to arrest him, he says to them, “I am he,” and they draw back and fall to the ground; then he gives himself up willingly.

In John, the cross appears as the hour of Christ's glorification, because Christ knows that his hour has come. Therefore, the cross is not merely death, but the glorification of the Son and the completion of the work the Father gave him.

24. “It Is Finished”

At the cross, Christ says: “It is finished,” then bows his head and gives up his spirit. This word summarizes a profound theological concept: the work Christ came to accomplish has reached its goal.

Thus, in John, the cross is neither failure nor an event beyond control, but the fulfillment of God's purpose and the completion of the Son's mission.

25. The Resurrection

John presents Christ's resurrection through moving personal encounters, especially with Mary Magdalene, Thomas, and the disciples. The resurrection proclamation reaches a particular climax when Thomas says to the risen Christ: “My Lord and my God.”

This statement reveals that the resurrection not only proves that Christ is alive, but also confirms his divine identity, which he had declared from the beginning of the Gospel.

26. The Purpose of the Gospel and Faith

John reaches his purpose clearly: that the reader may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing, the reader may have life in his name. Therefore, the verb “believed” recurs extensively in the Gospel.

In John, faith is not merely intellectual assent, but a relationship with Christ, accepting him and trusting him; this relationship leads to eternal life.

27. The Most Important Characters in John's Gospel

Alongside Christ, important figures appear, such as John the Baptist, Nicodemus, the Samaritan woman, Lazarus, Martha and Mary, Thomas, Peter, Judas Iscariot, and Pilate. These characters are used to show different ways of responding to Christ: faith, hesitation, rejection, worship, or witness.

28. The Gospel's Central Message

John declares that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and the eternal Word who became flesh, and that faith in him grants a person eternal life. The Gospel moves from the preexistence of the Word to the incarnation, from the signs to faith, from teaching to the cross, from the cross to the resurrection, and from the resurrection to witness.

29. The Great Movement of John's Gospel

John's Gospel begins in eternity, not in Bethlehem, declaring that the Word was in the beginning with God and was God, and then became flesh and dwelt among human beings, so that the glory of God appeared in the person of Jesus Christ. From there, Christ begins to reveal himself to the world through signs, teaching, and dialogue: he turns water into wine, gives life, heals the sick, feeds the crowds, opens the eyes of the blind, and raises Lazarus. Each sign leads to the greatest question concerning his identity. Then the “I am” declarations unfold, proclaiming that Christ is the bread of life, the light of the world, the good shepherd, the resurrection and the life, the way, the truth, and the life, and the true vine, while division over his person increases between those who believe in him and those who reject him. After the raising of Lazarus, events move forcefully toward Jerusalem. There Christ enters the hour of his glorification, washes his disciples' feet, and gives them the commandment of love. He then speaks about the Holy Spirit, abiding in him, and going to the Father, and prays for his disciples and for all who will believe in him. Then comes the cross, but John does not present it as a tragic conclusion; rather, he presents it as the hour of glorification and the completion of the work. Christ says, “It is finished,” and gives up his spirit. After this come the empty tomb and the appearances of the risen Christ. The disciples move from fear to faith and witness, and the Gospel reaches its goal when it calls the reader personally to believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that, by believing, the reader may have life in his name.

30. Links to the Old Testament

Although John differs in style from Matthew and Mark, its covenantal background is profound. Christ appears as the Word who fulfills God's purpose from creation, and is connected with the background of manna in the wilderness, light, the shepherd, water, the temple, the Jewish festivals, the bronze serpent, and images of the Messiah and the King. These connections reach their climax at the cross, where John presents Christ as the lamb who fulfills the purpose of Passover. Thus, the events of the Old Testament become the background for understanding Christ's saving work.

31. Encyclopedic Summary

John's Gospel is the Gospel that leads the reader into the depths of Christ's identity: he is the eternal Word, the Son of God, the light of the world, the bread of life, the good shepherd, the resurrection and the life, and the way, the truth, and the life. John does not merely record what Christ did, but selects the signs, events, and discourses that reveal his glory and lead to faith. From the preexistence of the Word to the incarnation, from the signs to the “I am” declarations, from the raising of Lazarus to the washing of the disciples' feet, and from the final discourses to the cross and resurrection, the entire Gospel moves toward one purpose: that people may know who Jesus is, believe in him, and receive life in his name.
