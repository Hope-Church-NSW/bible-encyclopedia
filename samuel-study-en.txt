First Samuel and Second Samuel

Methodological note: First and Second Samuel were originally a single historical work in the ancient Hebrew tradition, then later divided into two books. Therefore it is better to study them as one unit, while highlighting the distinctiveness of each part. Also, the name "Samuel" does not mean that the prophet Samuel wrote both books in their entirety; the book itself records Samuel's death before the end of First Samuel.

7
First: The Book of First Samuel
1. The Name of the Book and Its Meaning

The Hebrew name is שְׁמוּאֵל — Shemu'el — Samuel, the name by which the prophet Samuel was known, the pivotal figure at the beginning of the book. In Hebrew, the name is connected to the meaning associated with the idea of "God has heard," a meaning very fitting for the story of his birth, because Hannah prayed to the Lord for a son, and the Lord heard her prayer and gave her Samuel.

But the name Samuel is not connected only to his birth; it represents an entire stage in the history of Israel. In his days the nation moves from the system of the Judges to the monarchy, and from intermittent leadership to the emergence of Saul and then David.

In essence, the book is not merely a biography of Samuel, but the story of the transition of leadership from the judge to the king, from Saul to David, and from human choice to divine choice.

2. The Author

The book does not mention the name of a specific author for it in its final form.

In ancient times, some parts of the book were associated with Samuel, especially the material he witnessed, but this cannot mean that he wrote the entire book, because the book itself mentions his death in 1 Samuel 25.

It is most likely that the book, in its final form, resulted from the gathering of various traditions and historical records, then was composed within a historical and theological framework that explains the rise of the Israelite monarchy, the fall of Saul, and the rise of David.

3. The time of the events of First Samuel

The events of the book take place at the end of the period of the Judges and the beginning of the monarchy.

And this is a very important point in the ordering of the books:

Judges → Ruth → Samuel.

Judges revealed the chaos that resulted from the absence of stable leadership, Ruth led us to the family of David, then Samuel comes to present to us the beginning of the Davidic kingdom.

4. For Whom Was the Book Written?

The book was written for the nation of Israel and for the generations that came after the rise of the monarchy.

The goal was for the reader to know how the royal system began, why Saul was rejected, and why God chose David.

The book does not present the monarchy as the magical solution to all of Israel's problems, but reveals that the king himself must be subject to God.

5. Reasons for writing the book

The purpose can be summarized in four points:

Explaining the transition from the Judges to the monarchy.

Showing Saul's failure to obey God.

Announcing the choosing of David.

Confirming that the success of the king does not depend on his appearance or strength, but on his relationship with the Lord.

For this reason, First Samuel is the book of choice, obedience, rejection, and waiting.

6. The division of First Samuel

The book can be divided into four sections:

Samuel the prophet

1 Samuel 1–7

The birth of Samuel, his ministry, the corruption of Eli's two sons, the ark of the covenant, and Israel's victory over the Philistines.

Saul and the monarchy

1 Samuel 8–15

Israel's request for a king, the choosing of Saul, his first victories, then his disobedience and rejection.

David and Saul

1 Samuel 16–31

The anointing of David, the killing of Goliath, the friendship of David and Jonathan, Saul's persecution of David, then the death of Saul.

The transition to David

The book ends with the death of Saul, paving the way for David's rise in Second Samuel.

7. Hannah and a mother's prayer

The book begins with a woman wounded in heart.

Hannah was barren, and was suffering from deep inner bitterness.

She goes to Shiloh and prays before the Lord, asking for a son and vowing to dedicate him to the Lord all the days of his life.

Here the book begins with an idea that will be repeated often:

God hears the prayer and works through a person who appears weak.

8. The birth of Samuel

Samuel is born in answer to Hannah's prayer.

Then Hannah fulfills her vow and dedicates him to the Lord.

This is one of the most important keys to understanding the character; Samuel did not begin his life as a man of authority, but began as an answer to prayer and a child consecrated to God.

From here his entire life becomes bound up with worship, prophecy, and leadership.

9. Eli and his sons

Eli was high priest and a judge, but his two sons, Hophni and Phinehas, were corrupt.

This story constitutes a severe warning:

A religious place does not guarantee holiness.

A person can be in a sacred position while his heart is far from God.

For this reason, the book contrasts between:

Eli's corrupt sons

and

The young Samuel, who grows up before the Lord.

10. "Speak, Lord, for your servant is listening"

The Lord comes to Samuel at night.

At first Samuel thinks that Eli is calling him.

Then Eli understands that it is the Lord who is calling the boy.

So Samuel says:

"Speak, Lord, for your servant is listening."

This statement encapsulates the spirit of the true prophet:

It is not required that human beings make God speak according to their own desires; rather, the person must become prepared to hear what God says.

11. The Ark of the Covenant

Israel goes out to war against the Philistines.

Instead of repentance and seeking the Lord, they take the Ark of the Covenant into battle as though the presence of the Ark guarantees victory.

But they are defeated.

And the Ark is taken.

And Hophni and Phinehas die.

And Eli dies when he hears the news.

Here the narrative gives a powerful theological lesson:

Holy things cannot be turned into magical instruments to guarantee human success.

12. Dagon and the Ark of the Covenant

The Philistines place the Ark of the Covenant in the temple of Dagon.

In the morning they find the statue of Dagon fallen before the Ark of the Lord.

On the following day they find it fallen and broken.

Then the plagues strike the cities of the Philistines.

Here the narrative declares clearly:

The God of Israel does not need anyone to rescue Him.

He is able to manifest His glory even when His people are defeated.

13. Samuel Leads the People

Samuel calls the people to abandon the foreign gods and return to the Lord.

The people gather at Mizpah.

Then Israel defeats the Philistines.

And Samuel sets up a stone and names it:

“Stone of Help” — Eben-Ezer.

This name carries the meaning:

“Thus far the Lord has helped us.”

It is a declaration that the victory came from the Lord.

14. Israel’s Request for a King

Samuel grows old, and his sons become judges, but they do not walk in his way.

Then the people request a king.

Here the problem is not in the idea of kingship itself; the Law had already known in advance the possibility of a king.

The problem lay in the motive and the manner of the request.

For the people want a king:

“Like all the nations.”

Here begins the conflict between:

A king according to the will of God

and

A king to satisfy the desire of the people.

15. Saul

The name Saul — שָׁאוּל — Sha'ul is associated with a Hebrew root meaning “the one asked for” or “the one appointed/entrusted.”

The narrative describes him as handsome and tall, and he was taller than all the people.

Here there is an important paradox:

The people look at outward appearance.

But God looks at the heart.

Yet God gives Saul a genuine opportunity to lead.

16. Saul’s Early Victories

At the beginning of his reign, Saul achieves victories over his enemies.

And he appears to be the king the nation had been waiting for.

But the narrative wants the reader not to judge too quickly.

Because the true test of a king is not:

Whether he can win?

But rather:

Whether he can obey?

17. Saul’s First Sin

In one crisis, Saul waits for Samuel, but he acts improperly and offers the burnt offering.

Samuel declares that his kingdom will not be established.

The problem was not merely in offering a sacrifice.

The problem was that Saul overstepped the divine command because of pressure of circumstances.

Here the principle is made evident:

Obedience is more important than religious activity.

18. Saul’s Rejection

In the story of Amalek, God commands Saul to carry out a certain judgment.

But Saul keeps the best sheep and cattle.

Then he says that the people kept them in order to sacrifice to the Lord.

Then comes Samuel’s famous response:

“Behold, obedience is better than sacrifice, and listening better than the fat of rams.”

This is one of the most important verses in the narrative.

God does not desire worship used to cover disobedience.

19. Anointing of David

After Saul’s rejection, God sends Samuel to the house of Jesse in Bethlehem.

Jesse’s sons pass before the prophet.

But God rejects one after another.

Then David comes, the youngest of the sons, and he was shepherding the sheep.

Then the Lord says:

“Man looks at outward appearance, but the Lord looks at the heart.”

This verse is one of the key openings to the whole narrative.

20. David the Shepherd

Before David was king, he was a shepherd.

And this is very important in studying his character.

God did not begin by educating him in the palace.

Rather, He began him in the wilderness with the sheep.

There he learned responsibility, courage, and dependence on God.

This stage will appear later in his confrontation with Goliath.

21. David and Goliath

The Philistine giant Goliath comes challenging the army of Israel.

Everyone is afraid.

But David sees the matter differently:

Goliath is not only challenging the army of Israel, but also challenging the God of Israel.

And David goes out to him with five stones and a sling.

Goliath falls.

Thus David's victory becomes the great turning point in his life.

22. The meaning of David's victory

The primary goal was not for David to prove that he was the better warrior.

But to prove that:

"The battle is the Lord's."

And this idea will continue throughout David's life.

Strength is not in the sword.

And victory is not in numbers.

And true kingship does not begin from the throne, but from faith.

23. Jonathan and David

Jonathan son of Saul becomes David's closest friend.

He is one of the most beautiful examples of friendship in the Bible.

Even though David would take the kingdom that could have been Jonathan's by human expectation, Jonathan does not fight him.

Rather, he acknowledges God's purpose.

This reveals a rarely humble heart.

24. Saul's jealousy

David succeeds more and more.

And the women's song spreads:

"Saul has slain his thousands, and David his ten thousands."

So Saul becomes angry.

And from here the transformation begins:

Saul, the king whom the people chose,

becomes:

the man who hunts down the man whom God chose.

25. David the fugitive

David flees from Saul.

He lives in caves and the wilderness.

And he gathers men around him.

Yet he refuses to kill Saul when given the opportunity.

This is one of the greatest moments of David's character.

For David believes that God gave Saul the anointing, and therefore he does not want to seize rule by his own hand.

26. David and "the Lord's anointed"

David could have killed Saul.

But his choice was to wait.

This is very important in the study of spiritual leadership:

Not every door opened by God means that a person must enter it in the way he himself wants.

David waits for God's timing.

And he does not want to build the kingdom through treachery.

27. Abigail

Abigail appears as a wise figure who intervenes to prevent David from taking revenge on Nabal.

She is one of the important female figures in the book.

The story reveals that God can use a wise word from a woman to prevent a great leader from committing a bloody mistake.

28. The death of Saul

At the end of the book, Saul fights his final battle against the Philistines.

He is severely wounded.

He dies along with his sons, among them Jonathan.

The book ends with the tragedy of the king who began powerfully but lost the kingdom because of disobedience.

Then Second Samuel begins with the story of David's rise.

🔵 The most important message in First Samuel

God does not choose according to appearance, nor does He measure leadership by outward success, but seeks a heart that hears and obeys; whoever rejects the word of God cannot keep what God gave him, no matter how great his beginning was.

📖 Secondly: The Book of Second Samuel
1. The Main Topic

If First Samuel narrates:

the rise of Saul and his fall, and the rise of David,

then Second Samuel narrates:

David's rise to the throne, the establishment of his kingdom, then his moral, familial, and political downfall.

This makes the book one of the most realistic books in the study of leadership.

David is not an angel.

But he is also not Saul.

The fundamental difference is that when David is rebuked, he returns to God in repentance.

2. The Author

Like First Samuel, the book does not explicitly mention a single author.

The author or final editor used a number of historical materials, records, and ancient traditions.

The book gives a strong impression that it is close to real political and military events, because it provides precise details about places, families, wars, and conflicts within the house of David.

3. The time of the events of Second Samuel

Its events take place during the reign of David.

It begins immediately after the death of Saul.

And it extends until nearly the end of David's life, with a clear introduction to the events of Solomon's rise in the Book of Kings.

4. Division of the book
David as king over Judah

2 Samuel 1–4

David as king over all Israel

2 Samuel 5–10

David's sin

2 Samuel 11–12

The Collapse of the House of David

2 Samuel 13–20

Appendices and Later Events

2 Samuel 21–24

5. David After the Death of Saul

When David hears of the death of Saul and Jonathan, he does not rejoice.

Rather, he laments for them.

And this reveals something great in his heart:

He did not want the kingship as revenge upon Saul.

He wanted the fulfillment of God's promise.

Therefore he begins his reign with grief, not with gloating.

6. David as King over Judah

David is anointed king over the house of Judah in Hebron.

But the rest of Israel follows Ish-bosheth son of Saul.

Thus a period of conflict begins between the house of Saul and the house of David.

And here it becomes evident that God's promise does not mean the path will be easy.

7. David as King over All Israel

After the death of Ish-bosheth, the tribes of Israel come to David.

And they anoint him king over all Israel.

And thus what God had previously announced is fulfilled.

David begins his reign from Hebron, then moves to a new stage.

8. Jerusalem

David seizes Jebus and makes Jerusalem his capital.

This is an event of the utmost importance in biblical history.

Jerusalem was not merely a political city.

It will later become:

The City of David.

The center of kingship.

The center of worship.

And profoundly connected to the history of the Temple and the Messiah.

9. The Ark of the Covenant and Jerusalem

David wants to bring the Ark of the Covenant to Jerusalem.

But when Uzzah stretches out his hand to the Ark, he dies.

So David becomes afraid.

Then the Ark is later brought in the correct manner.

And David enters before the LORD with joy.

And here we see that David wants something important:

That the presence of God be at the center of the kingdom.

10. The Davidic Covenant

This is one of the most important moments in the entire Old Testament.

God tells David through the prophet Nathan that God will establish a house for him.

And the key phrase:

"And I will establish the throne of his kingdom forever."

And here the book moves from the historical story of David to the messianic line.

So the Davidic king becomes part of the promise that will be fulfilled in the Messiah.

11. Why Is the Davidic Covenant Important?

Because God does not promise David merely a temporary kingdom.

Rather, He links the future to his offspring.

And this covenant becomes a foundation for a great number of prophecies and psalms that speak of:

The kingdom.

Son of David.

The throne.

The eternal kingdom.

And in the New Testament the title becomes:

"Son of David"

One of the most important messianic titles of Jesus.

12. David's Sin with Bathsheba

After David's greatest moments comes one of his worst moments.

He sees Bathsheba, the wife of Uriah the Hittite.

Then he takes her.

And afterward he arranges the death of Uriah.

And here the book declares that the greatest king of Israel can fall a horrific fall.

And the Scripture does not conceal David's sin.

And this is one of the most important reasons for the value of the biblical narrative.

13. Nathan the Prophet

Nathan comes to David.

And he does not begin by accusing him directly.

Rather, he tells him the story of a rich man who took a poor man's ewe lamb.

So David becomes angry and pronounces judgment.

Then Nathan says to him:

"You are the man."

And this is a moment of great prophetic confrontation.

The king who rules the people must also accept that the word of God rules him.

14. David's Repentance

David confesses his sin.

And his confession does not cancel the consequences.

And this is a very important distinction:

Forgiveness does not always mean the removal of temporal consequences.

God forgives David, but the house of David will enter into a series of upheavals.

15. Amnon and Tamar

Troubles begin within the house of David.

Amnon assaults his sister Tamar.

Then Absalom kills him.

Thus the effects of David’s sin appear in the house.

And the book shows that personal sin can leave effects that extend beyond the sinner.

16. Absalom

Absalom, son of David, is marked by beauty and an attractive personality.

But he uses these qualities to build political popularity.

Then he rises up against his father.

Here the kingdom becomes a family tragedy:

The father against the son.

17. David Flees from Jerusalem

David flees from Jerusalem.

And this is a deeply painful picture.

The man who entered the city as king comes out of it as a fugitive.

And the one who brought the Ark of the Covenant to Jerusalem finds himself outside the city.

Yet David appears in this crisis as more mature; he accepts discipline and does not cling to the throne at any price.

18. Absalom’s Death

David’s men prevail.

But Absalom becomes caught by his hair in a tree.

And he dies.

And when David hears the news, he cries out:

'My son Absalom! My son, my son Absalom! Would that I had died in your place!'

This is one of the most human moments in the book.

David has an army, a throne, and a kingdom, but at that moment he is a father with a broken heart.

19. Saul and David: The Great Contrast

First and Second Samuel place the two kings before us.

Saul

He is concerned with the king’s image.

David

He is concerned with the heart’s relationship to God.

But David is not without sin.

The crucial difference appears when the two face their sin:

Saul justifies himself.

David confesses and repents.

That is why David becomes the model of the repentant king, not the model of the perfect man.

20. David and the Messiah

This is the most important theological axis in the two books.

David is:

The anointed king.

The shepherd.

The warrior.

The one who comes from Bethlehem.

The covenant keeper.

The one who bears the promise of offspring and throne.

Then the Messiah comes in the New Testament as:

Son of David.

But the Messiah fulfills what David was unable to fulfill.

David sinned.

As for Christ, he is without sin.

David was king for a time.

But the Messiah’s kingdom is eternal.

David died.

But the Messiah rose.

21. The Most Important Figures in the Two Books
Samuel

The prophet who led Israel from the era of the judges to kingship.

Hannah

The mother of Samuel, and a model of prayer and faith.

Eli

The high priest and judge who failed to discipline his household.

Saul

The first king, who began well but refused obedience.

Jonathan

The faithful friend who placed the will of God above his own interests.

David

The anointed king, who prevailed by faith but fell and repented.

Abigail

An example of wisdom that prevents evil.

Nathan

The prophet who confronted the king with the word of God.

Bathsheba

The wife of Uriah and then of David, and the mother of Solomon.

Absalom

The son of David who rebelled against him.

22. The Most Important Message in First and Second Samuel

It can be formulated thus:

God is the One who chooses and establishes the king, and the true king must submit to the word of God; human beings may fall greatly, but genuine repentance remains the way back to God, while the promise of God continues despite human weakness.

“For man looks at the outward appearance, but the Lord looks at the heart.”
