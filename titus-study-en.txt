Paul's Letter to Titus
1. The Epistle’s Name and the Meaning of the Name

It is known as the Epistle to Titus, one of the three Pastoral Epistles alongside First and Second Timothy.

The name "Titus" is a Greek/Latin name common in the Roman world, and the New Testament does not mention any theological meaning for the name. Titus was one of Paul's co-workers, a trusted companion in ministry, especially in matters related to the churches and the Gentiles.

The letter focuses on the relationship between sound teaching and godly living, and on the necessity of ordering the church, choosing elders, and teaching believers that the grace that saves them is the same grace that trains them for a life of holiness.

2. The Author and His Character

The author of the letter is Paul the Apostle, who describes himself as a servant of God and an apostle of Jesus Christ.

As for Titus, he was one of Paul's partners in ministry, and his name appears prominently in the context of Paul's ministry among the Gentiles, particularly in dealing with the church of Corinth.

Titus appears in the letter as a man whom Paul entrusted with completing what remained unfinished and ordering matters in the churches.

3. To Whom Was the Epistle Written?

It was written to Titus, who was serving in Crete.

His task was to set in order the matters that remained unfinished, to appoint elders in the cities, to confront deviant teachers, and to teach the various groups of believers.

4. The Reason for Writing the Epistle

Paul states clearly that the purpose of leaving Titus in Crete was:

"For this cause left I thee in Crete, that thou shouldest set in order the things that are wanting, and ordain elders in every city, as I had appointed thee"
(Titus 1:5).

Thus the letter combines the ordering of the church, the protection of doctrine, the formation of ministers, and the demonstration of godliness in daily life.

5. Date of the Epistle

It is likely that the letter was written in the second half of the sixties AD, in roughly the same period to which the other Pastoral Epistles belong.

The text does not provide a specific date, so dating it remains a matter of historical research rather than a direct scriptural statement.

6. The Main Theme

The central theme of the letter is:

Sound teaching that produces a godly life and good works.

The letter does not separate doctrine from conduct; true faith must be manifested in the home, the church, society, work, relationships, and self-control.

7. Paul, a servant of God and an apostle of Jesus Christ

Paul opens the letter by introducing himself as:

"Paul, a servant of God, and an apostle of Jesus Christ, according to the faith of God's elect, and the acknowledging of the truth which is after godliness"
(Titus 1:1).

From the beginning, Paul links faith with the knowledge of the truth and godliness.

8. The hope of eternal life

Paul says that the apostolic message rests:

"In hope of eternal life, which God, that cannot lie, promised before the world began"
(Titus 1:2).

Thus, Christian teaching begins with God's eternal promise and ends with the hope of eternal life.

9. God Who Does Not Lie

Paul describes God as the One who is above lying.

For Christian hope is not human optimism, but is built on God's faithfulness and His promise.

10. God's Grace and Its Revelation

Paul connects divine revelation with God's work at the appointed time.

For the gospel did not emerge as a human idea, but as a revelation originating from God and proclaimed through the preaching entrusted to Paul.

11. Church Order

Here comes one of the epistle's most important themes: church leadership.

For church order is not a secondary matter, but part of the faithfulness of ministry.

12. Qualifications of an Elder

Paul requires that an elder be blameless, the husband of one wife, having believing children who are not accused of dissipation or rebellion.

Then he clarifies that the overseer must be:

“Blameless as a steward of God, not self-willed, not quick-tempered, not given to wine, not violent, not greedy for dishonest gain,”
(Titus 1: 7).

For Christian leadership is based on character before position.

13. The Minister as a Lover of Good

The minister must be a lover of what is good, sensible, just, devout, and self-controlled.

These qualities reveal that ministry is not merely an ability to speak, but a life submitted to Christ.

14. Holding Fast to the Faithful Word

Paul says that the overseer must be:

“Holding fast to the faithful word according to the teaching,”
(Titus 1: 9).

For the minister cannot guard the church from false teaching unless he himself is firmly established in the word of truth.

15. Resisting False Teachers

Paul warns of many insubordinate individuals who speak empty words and deceive people.

He refers specifically to some from a Jewish background who were mixing Christian teaching with practices and doctrines not in accordance with the gospel.

16. “Silencing Their Mouths”

Paul says concerning deceptive teachers:

“Whose mouths must be stopped, for they subvert whole households, teaching things they ought not for the sake of dishonest gain,”
(Titus 1: 11).

For the church is responsible for protecting believers from teaching that corrupts faith and exploits people.

17. Professing God and Denying the Power

Paul presents a grave diagnosis:

“They profess to know God, but in works they deny Him,”
(Titus 1: 16).

This is one of the epistle's most important themes: verbal profession is insufficient if one's life denies God.

18. Teaching Befitting Godliness

The second chapter begins with an important statement:

“But as for you, speak what is befitting sound doctrine,”
(Titus 2: 1).

For Christian truth must take on a practical ethical form.

19. Older Men

Paul calls older men to be sober-minded, dignified, self-controlled, and sound in faith, love, and patience.

For age in the church ought to be transformed into spiritual maturity and an example.

20. Older Women

He calls older women to reverent behavior, not to be slanderers or enslaved to wine, and to be teachers of good things.

Here emerges the concept of intergenerational discipleship within the church.

21. Younger Women

Paul asks that younger women be taught to love their husbands and their children, and to live sensibly and purely.

For the gospel enters into daily family life.

22. Young Men and Self-Control

Paul summarizes what he requires of young men in a crucial aspect:

“Likewise, exhort the young men to be sober-minded,”
(Titus 2: 6).

That is, self-control and maturity are fundamental elements in the Christian life.

23. Titus as an Example

Paul asks Titus to present himself:

“In all things an example of good works,”
(Titus 2: 7).

For the minister does not teach merely by words, but embodies the doctrine before others.

24. The Grace That Disciplines

The epistle arrives at one of its greatest passages:

“For the grace of God that brings salvation has appeared to all men, teaching us that, denying ungodliness and worldly lusts, we should live soberly, righteously, and godly in the present age,”
(Titus 2: 11–12).

Grace here is not merely forgiveness for the past; it is a teacher and instructor for humanity in the present.

25. Awaiting the Appearance of Christ

Grace connects the present life with the future:

“Looking for the blessed hope and the appearance of the glory of our great God and Savior Jesus Christ,”
(Titus 2: 13).

For the church lives in the present while looking toward the appearance of Christ.

26. Christ Gave Himself

Paul says:

“Who gave Himself for us, that He might redeem us from every lawless deed and purify for Himself a special people, zealous for good works,”
(Titus 2: 14).

Here redemption, purification, possession, and good works unite in the work of Christ.

27. Teaching and Spiritual Authority

Paul asks Titus to speak, exhort, and rebuke with all authority.

For the minister does not offer a personal opinion, but proclaims the teaching entrusted to him.

28. Submission to Authorities

Paul calls believers to be subject to rulers and authorities, to obey magistrates, and to be ready for every good work.

The gospel does not push the believer toward chaos, but toward a responsible life in society.

29. They should slander no one

He calls believers not to blaspheme anyone, and not to be quarrelsome, but gentle, showing all meekness to all people.

For a person who has known the grace of God ought not to live in bitterness and hostility.

30. The believer's past

Paul mentions that the believers themselves were formerly in ignorance, disobedience, and enslavement to lusts.

This reminder prevents spiritual pride: we were not saved because we are better than others, but because God had mercy on us.

31. The appearing of God's kindness and love

One of the most beautiful passages of the letter comes here:

"But when the kindness and love of God our Savior toward man appeared, not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to His mercy He saved us."
(Titus 3:4–5).

Salvation is built on God's mercy, not on human merit.

32. Renewal by the Holy Spirit

Paul continues:

"through the washing of regeneration and renewing of the Holy Spirit"
(Titus 3:5).

Salvation is not merely a legal change in status before God, but includes an inner renewal by the work of the Holy Spirit.

33. Justification by grace

He says:

"having been justified by His grace, we should become heirs according to the hope of eternal life"
(Titus 3:7).

Here Paul brings together grace, justification, hope, and eternal life.

34. Good works

Although salvation is not by the works of righteousness which we have done, Paul affirms the necessity for believers to be concerned with good works.

This is not a contradiction; works are not the price of salvation, but the fruit of salvation.

35. Warning against disputes

Paul warns against foolish disputes, genealogies, contentions, and quarrels about the law.

Argument that does not build up the church must not become the center of ministry.

36. Dealing with the heretic

Paul asks that a divisive person be warned once and twice, then avoided if he continues to reject the truth.

The church combines patience in teaching with discipline in the face of persistent deviation.

37. Titus and practical service

Paul concludes with practical requests concerning travel, workers, and needs.

This reveals that apostolic life was not merely theological teaching, but a network of service, support, and practical partnership.

38. "That they may learn to practice good works"

Paul repeats the idea of good works at the end of the letter.

Faith that refuses to produce good in the world contradicts the practical purpose of the gospel.

39. Christ in the Letter to Titus

Christ appears in the letter as Lord and Savior, in whom the saving grace of God appeared, who gave Himself to redeem His people and purify them, and who will appear in glory as the blessed hope of the church.

Thus Christ is not merely a doctrinal subject, but the foundation of salvation, holiness, hope, and good works.

40. Connections with the Old Testament

The letter is connected to the Old Testament background in the themes of godliness, righteousness, wisdom, the law, leadership, elders, purity, good works, and the hope of salvation.

The concept of the people of God who belong specially to God and are characterized by holiness has its roots in God's calling of His people in the Old Testament, but in Titus it takes on a clear messianic dimension through the redemptive work of Christ.

41. The overall movement of the letter

The letter begins with Paul's declaration of his mission for the faith of God's elect and the knowledge of the truth which accords with godliness, then directs Titus to set the churches in order and appoint elders able to hold fast to the faithful word and confront false teachers. After addressing leadership and teaching, the letter moves to daily life, calling men, women, young people, and servants to conduct befitting sound teaching. Then the letter reaches its theological center in declaring that the saving grace of God has appeared, and that this same grace trains the believer to deny ungodliness and to live with self-control, righteousness, and godliness, while awaiting the blessed hope and the appearing of the glory of Jesus Christ. After that Paul returns to social life, calling for submission to authorities, gentleness, and readiness for every good work, then he lays the foundation of salvation in God's mercy, the renewal of the Holy Spirit, and justification by grace. Thus the letter moves from sound teaching to church order, from church order to holiness of life, from grace to good works, and from present life to awaiting the appearing of Christ.

42. Encyclopedic summary

Paul's letter to Titus is the letter of sound teaching that turns into a sound life. It begins by establishing the church upon mature spiritual leadership that holds fast to the word and resists false teaching, then extends to all categories of believers to declare that the gospel must appear in conduct, family, work, and relationships. At the heart of the letter stands the declaration of grace: "The grace of God that brings salvation has appeared to all men"; but it is not a grace that leaves man as he is, but a grace that teaches him, purifies him, and trains him for a life of godliness. Hope reaches its peak in awaiting the appearing of the glory of Jesus Christ, who gave Himself to redeem His people and purify them and make them a people of His own, zealous for good works. Then Paul makes clear that salvation was not the result of works of righteousness done by man, but the result of God's mercy, the renewal of the Holy Spirit, and justification by grace. Therefore the letter presents an integrated biblical equation: sound teaching → grace → godliness → good works → hope in the appearing of Christ. Thus an organized church, sound doctrine, faithful service, a holy life, and good works all become manifestations of genuine faith in Christ, whose appearing is awaited.
