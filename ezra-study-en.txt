The Book of Ezra
4

The central idea of the book: Ezra is a book of return and restoration: the return of the people from exile, the restoration of worship, the rebuilding of the temple, and then the reshaping of the people’s life around the word of God. Therefore, Ezra should not be read merely as a historical record of the return from Babylon, but as a testimony to God’s faithfulness to His covenant and, at the same time, as a warning that returning to the land is not enough unless it is accompanied by a genuine return to God.

1. Name of the book and its meaning

The name of the book is Ezra, and in Hebrew עֶזְרָא—ʿEzrā; it is a name related to the Hebrew root עזר—ʿ-z-r, which conveys the meaning of help or assistance.

The name is profoundly fitting to the nature of the book, for Israel emerged from exile in a state of political and spiritual weakness. It had neither an independent king, nor the former kingdom, nor the temple that Solomon built; nevertheless, God began to open before it a new path of return and restoration.

Ezra himself becomes, in the second part of the book, one of the instruments of this assistance, for he does not come merely to live in Jerusalem, but to restore the people to the law of the LORD, combining priesthood, scribal work, teaching, and reform.

2. The Author

The book does not directly identify the name of its full author; therefore, it is not proper to say without qualification that Ezra is the certain author of the entire book.

However, beginning with Ezra 7, large sections appear in the first person, especially Ezra 7:27–9:15, making Ezra’s connection to the original material very clear. Other sections appear in the third person; therefore, some scholars hold that Ezra combined his memoirs with other historical sources, or that a later historian compiled these materials into the final form of the book.

Ancient Jewish tradition also associated Ezra with this work, and there is a clear literary and historical resemblance among Ezra, Nehemiah, and Chronicles. Therefore, a number of scholars consider it possible that this literary collection had a common author or editor, while the question of the final author remains inconclusive.

3. Ezra: The Scribe and Priest

Ezra was not merely an administrative figure sent by the Persian king to Jerusalem.

The book describes him as a priest and a skilled scribe in the Law of Moses. This description is very important for understanding the entire book, because the mission for which he came was not merely to rebuild stones, but to restore the people to the knowledge and practice of God’s will.

Here an important threefold pattern appears in Ezra’s character:

Studied the word → practiced the word → taught the word.

This principle explains why Ezra became a pivotal figure in the history of Judaism after the exile, and why the book moves from the subject of rebuilding the temple to the subject of rebuilding the people’s life around the Law.

4. Ezra’s Birth and Death

The Bible provides neither the date of Ezra’s birth nor the date of his death.

Therefore, according to the approach agreed upon in this series, a date not found in the biblical sources or reliable historical sources should not be invented.

What is certain is that Ezra was alive and active during the reign of the Persian king Artaxerxes I, and that his arrival in Jerusalem is usually placed in the middle of the fifth century B.C., most often dated to about 458 B.C. according to the traditional chronology adopted by a number of studies. There is, however, scholarly discussion concerning the dating of Ezra’s mission and its sequence in relation to Nehemiah.

5. The Time of the Book’s Events

The history of the book is divided into two major stages separated by several decades.

The first stage begins around 538 B.C., after Babylon fell before the Persians, when Cyrus issued a decree permitting the Jews to return to Jerusalem and rebuild the temple. This stage ends with the completion of the temple during the reign of Darius, around 515 B.C.

The narrative then jumps to the second stage in the fifth century B.C., when Ezra comes to Jerusalem during the reign of Artaxerxes and begins a somewhat different work, consisting of teaching the Law and reforming the life of the community.

Thus, the Book of Ezra covers events extending over a long historical period and presents before us two fundamental stages: rebuilding the house of God, then rebuilding the people of God.

6. The Time of the Book’s Composition

It is difficult to determine one certain year for the composition of the book, because its historical material covers events extending from 538 B.C. to Ezra’s time, and because some parts of the book are written in Hebrew and others in Aramaic.

Many scholars believe that the final form of the book took shape in the Persian period, perhaps during the fifth century B.C., with the possibility that some materials and memoirs predate the final compilation process.

More important than determining the exact year is recognizing that the book was born in a post-exilic setting—that is, in a community attempting to understand how to live with God after the experience of judgment and destruction.

7. For Whom Was the Book Written?

The book was written primarily for the Jewish community that returned from exile and for subsequent generations seeking to understand the meaning of their existence in the Promised Land without the former Davidic kingdom as it had been in the past.

They faced a profound question:

If God permitted the destruction of Jerusalem and the temple and the exile of the people, do His promises still stand?

The Book of Ezra answers that the exile was not the end of Israel’s story, because God Himself moved political events, brought back the people, restored the temple vessels to them, and opened the way for the restoration of worship.

8. The Purpose of Writing the Book

The purposes of the Book of Ezra can be summarized in a set of interconnected themes.

First, to record the return of the remnant from exile to the Promised Land.

Second, to record the rebuilding of the temple as the center of the worship of the LORD.

Third, to demonstrate God’s faithfulness to His promises despite the people’s former sin.

Fourth, to demonstrate God’s sovereignty over kings and empires, as He uses Cyrus, Darius, and Artaxerxes to accomplish His purposes.

Fifth, to call the people to holiness, obedience, and the Law so that the tragedy of exile may not be repeated again.

9. General Outline of the Book of Ezra
Ezra 1–2

The decree of Cyrus and the first return from exile.

Ezra 3

The erection of the altar and the laying of the temple foundation.

Ezra 4

Opposition and the cessation of the work.

Ezra 5–6

The resumption of construction, the completion of the temple, and its dedication.

Ezra 7–8

Ezra’s arrival in Jerusalem and his mission to teach the Law.

Ezra 9

The discovery of the problem of intermarriage and the confession of the people’s sin.

Ezra 10

Repentance and the adoption of reforming measures within the community.

This division reveals the book’s internal structure:

Return → altar → temple → Law → repentance.

10. The Fall of Babylon and the Rise of Cyrus

The Babylonian exile appeared, from a human perspective, to be a tragic end.

Yet God was at work in the political history of empires.

In 539 B.C., Babylon fell before the Persians under the leadership of Cyrus, and in approximately the following year permission was given to the Jews to return and rebuild the temple.

Here one of the greatest ideas of the book appears:

Empires change, but God’s sovereignty over history does not change.

Cyrus, who was not an Israelite king, becomes an instrument in God’s hand to fulfill a promise He had previously declared through the prophets.

11. Cyrus and Isaiah

When we read the Book of Ezra in the light of the prophets, the appearance of Cyrus becomes more significant.

Isaiah had previously announced Cyrus by name in the context of speaking about the fulfillment of God’s purpose.

Ezra then presents the event historically.

Thus we find a remarkable connection between:

Prophecy → history → fulfillment.

This is one of the very important characteristics of biblical study: prophecy does not remain an abstract idea, but enters history.

12. Zerubbabel

Zerubbabel is among the most important figures of the first stage.

He is of Davidic descent and appears as the leader of the first return.

The presence of a man of Davidic descent in the leadership of the post-exilic community has great theological significance, because the exile removed the independent kingdom, but did not erase David’s line from history.

Here the thread of the Davidic covenant continues even after the collapse of the kingdom.

13. Joshua the High Priest

Joshua son of Jehozadak the high priest also appears.

Thus, two important figures appear before us:

Zerubbabel represents the Davidic line of leadership.

Joshua represents the line of priesthood and worship.

This convergence of kingship, priesthood, and temple becomes one of the keys to understanding the post-exilic period.

14. The Altar before the Temple

One of the most beautiful theological details in the book is that the people erected the altar before the construction of the temple was completed.

This means that worship began before the building project was completed.

Returning to God does not begin when construction ends; rather, it begins when a person returns to God.

This is a point that can be applied to the concept of restoration in general:

God is concerned first with the people's relationship with Him; then buildings and systems take their proper place.

15. Laying the Foundation of the Temple

When the foundation of the temple was laid, a powerful emotional response occurred.

Some shouted and rejoiced.

Others among the elders who had seen the first temple wept when they compared the past with the present.

This is one of the most human scenes in the book.

It is a moment that brings together:

The memory of former glory

and

Hope for the future

and

The pain of loss

and

The joy of restoration.

16. Opposition to Rebuilding

The path of return was not easy.

Opposition arose from the surrounding peoples.

Letters and complaints were sent to the Persian authorities, and the work stopped for a period of time.

But this opposition was not stronger than God's purpose.

Haggai and Zechariah appear to encourage the people to complete the work; then the royal decree is confirmed, and construction continues until it is completed.

17. Haggai and Zechariah

The presence of the two prophets Haggai and Zechariah in the background of the book is very important.

Haggai confronts a people who had begun to care for their own houses while the house of the Lord still lay in ruins.

Zechariah gives the people a broader vision of God's work and the restoration of Jerusalem.

Thus, we see that God used not only political leadership but also the prophetic word to move the people to action.

18. Completion of the Temple

The temple is completed during the reign of Darius.

Then the people hold a celebration, sacrifices are offered, and they celebrate Passover.

This event represents a great victory.

The temple that the Babylonians destroyed stands once again.

The people who were exiled worship in Jerusalem once again.

But the writer does not allow us to regard this as the end of the story.

For the next question is:

Will the people themselves live differently?

19. Ezra Comes to Jerusalem

After many years, Ezra appears.

The difference between the period of Zerubbabel and the period of Ezra is exceedingly important.

Zerubbabel was associated with rebuilding the temple.

Ezra, however, is associated with rebuilding the life of the people around the Law.

Here the book shifts from stones to hearts.

The temple can be built in years, but building the character of a people faithful to God requires teaching, repentance, and spiritual discipline.

20. Ezra's Mission

Ezra had an official commission from the Persian king.

He comes to Jerusalem with resources and authority to organize matters connected with the Law of Moses, and he begins to teach the people and order life according to the Law.

But Ezra's greatness is not shown primarily in the authority the king gave him.

Rather, it is shown in the fact that he prepared his heart to seek the Law of the Lord, to practise it, and to teach it.

This makes his life a very important model for the scholar, minister, and teacher.

21. The Real Problem after the Return

The great surprise in the second part of the book is that the problem is no longer:

“Will the temple be built?”

But rather:

“Will the people remain faithful to God?”

The greatest danger was no longer the Babylonian army.

Rather, it was internal deviation.

This is a very profound theological idea in the book:

A person may return physically from exile, but his heart also needs to return to God.

22. The Crisis of Intermarriage

Ezra discovers that some of the people, including some leaders and priests, had entered into marriages with the peoples of the region in a context connected with the danger of turning away from the worship of the Lord.

Ezra collapses before God and confesses the sin of the community.

Here the text must be read in its covenantal and historical context; the issue is not merely ethnic or racial difference, but the protection of the identity of the covenant community from returning to the idolatrous practices that had originally been among the causes of the exile.

23. Ezra's Prayer

Ezra's prayer in chapter 9 is one of the profoundest texts of corporate repentance in the Old Testament.

He does not stand apart from the people and say:

“They have sinned.”

Rather, he includes himself with them in the confession.

In the sense of the prayer, he says:

We have sinned.

This reveals the heart of the true spiritual leader:

He does not use the sin of the people to exalt himself; rather, he bears the sin of the community before God.

24. Repentance

The people respond.

A painful and difficult process of reform begins.

This reveals that biblical repentance is not merely a feeling of regret.

True repentance means:

Confession.

Brokenness.

Change.

Taking practical steps to cut off sin.

And this is why the Book of Ezra does not end merely with celebration, but ends with a process of purification and reformation.

Cyrus

The Persian king who opened the way of return and issued the order to rebuild the temple.

Zerubbabel

Leader of the first return and from the line of David, connected to the rebuilding of the temple.

Joshua son of Jozadak

The high priest who participated in the restoration of worship.

Haggai

The prophet who encouraged the people to complete the building of the temple.

Zechariah

The prophet who encouraged the community and announced hope of restoration.

Ezra

The priest and scribe who led the reformation based on God's law.

Darius

The Persian king who confirmed the decree of Cyrus and aided the continuation of temple building.

Artaxerxes

The Persian king who sent Ezra and gave him authority and resources to carry out his mission.

The fall of Babylon.

Cyrus’s decree.

The first return.

The arrival of Zerubbabel.

The establishment of the altar.

The laying of the foundation of the temple.

Opposition and cessation.

The ministry of Haggai and Zechariah.

The resumption of building.

The completion of the temple.

The celebration of Passover.

The arrival of Ezra.

The teaching of the law.

The discovery of sin.

Ezra's prayer.

Repentance and reformation.

27. The Most Important Theological Message in the Book

The Book of Ezra presents three interconnected truths.

First: God is faithful to His promises.

Exile was not able to annul God's promise.

Second: God is Lord of history.

Cyrus and Darius and Artaxerxes, despite being pagan kings, appear within the narrative as instruments in the accomplishment of God's purpose.

Third: True restoration requires holiness.

The return of the people to the land and the building of the temple are insufficient if the people returned to the same sins that led to exile.

28. Ezra and the Temple

The temple in Ezra is not merely a building.

It is a sign that God did not abandon His people.

When the temple returns to Jerusalem, the sacrifices and Passover and the service of the priests and Levites return.

However, the book simultaneously places the temple under the authority of the law, because true worship is inseparable from a life of obedience.

29. Ezra and the Word of God

It can be said that there is an important transition within the book:

Chapters 1–6: Rebuilding the house of God.

Chapters 7–10: Rebuilding the life of God's people.

And this transition from stone to heart is one of the most beautiful theological structures in the book.

For the project is not complete when the temple becomes established, but the people need themselves to become a holy community living in obedience to God.

30. The Messianic Dimension in the Book of Ezra

Ezra does not present direct messianic prophecy like some prophetic books.

But the book falls within the line of salvation history that leads to the Messiah.

For the return from exile preserves the remnant of the people of Israel, and preserves with it the historical chain that will lead to the fulfillment of the earlier promises.

And most importantly, the problem of the book reveals something profound: even after the return and the building of the temple, the human heart remains in need of a work deeper than mere external reformation.

And this leads us in Christian reading to the promises that spoke of a new heart and a new spirit, and then to the Messiah who accomplishes ultimate salvation and restores God's presence to His people in a manner far greater than the earthly temple.

31. What Do We Learn from the Book of Ezra?

That God does not forget His promises even when history appears to contradict them.

That God is able to use political forces and empires to accomplish His purposes without losing His sovereignty over history.

That reconstruction sometimes begins with small possibilities but becomes great when it is in God's hand.

That worship does not begin with the building, but with the heart that returns to God.

That the spiritual leader needs to seek God's word and practice it before teaching it to others.

That true repentance requires practical steps and is not merely an emotional response.

That the most dangerous enemy of the people is not always from without; the drift may come from within.

That exile was not the end of the story, but a phase within God's plan for restoration.
