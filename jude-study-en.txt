The Letter of Jude
1. The Epistle’s Name and the Meaning of the Name

The Epistle of Jude is a very short letter, yet it is one of the New Testament’s most forceful books in defending the apostolic faith and warning against corrupt teachers. The name “Jude” is the Arabic form of the Greek name Judas, a common name in the Jewish environment of the first century, and in the New Testament it is associated with more than one person. The author introduces himself as “Jude, a servant of Jesus Christ and brother of James,” thereby defining his identity through his service to Christ and his relationship to James.

2. The Author and His Character

The author introduces himself by saying: “Jude, a servant of Jesus Christ, and brother of James” (Jude 1: 1). Christian tradition understands this to refer to Jude, the brother of the Lord Jesus and the brother of James, who appears in the Gospel lists of Christ’s brothers. It is noteworthy that Jude does not introduce himself as “the brother of Jesus,” but as “a servant of Jesus Christ”; this choice carries spiritual and theological significance, since his relationship with Christ had come to rest on faith and submission to him as Lord and Savior, not merely on physical kinship.

3. To whom was the epistle written?

Jude wrote to believers whom he describes as “those who are called, beloved in God the Father and kept for Jesus Christ” (Jude 1: 1). He does not identify a particular group or city, which suggests that the letter had a broader character and could be read in more than one Christian community. These believers needed a serious warning about people who had entered their midst and attempted to turn grace into a license for sin and to deny the lordship of Christ.

4. The Reason for Writing the Epistle

Jude initially wanted to write about the common salvation, but he found it urgently necessary to warn against the danger of false teaching, saying: “I found it necessary to write appealing to you to contend for the faith that was once for all delivered to the saints” (Jude 1: 3). Therefore, the letter becomes a call to defend the apostolic faith entrusted to the church and not to allow doctrinal and moral corruption to infiltrate the community.

5. Date of the Epistle

The text does not provide an explicit date of composition, and therefore scholars differ in determining its date. Some scholars associate the letter with the second half of the first century AD, while others place it closer to the end of the first century. A specific year should not be treated as a biblical fact, but as an estimate based on the possible relationship between the letter and the developments faced by the early church.

6. The Main Theme

The central theme of the Epistle of Jude is the defense of the faith and the warning against the spiritual and moral corruption that enters the church under the guise of grace. The letter powerfully combines doctrine and ethics, because Jude sees no possibility of separating sound teaching from holy living. Consequently, deviation from the truth about God leads to deviation in conduct, and the church is called to resist both together.

7. The Faith Once Delivered to the Saints

Jude uses a highly significant expression: “the faith that was once for all delivered to the saints” (Jude 1: 3). This does not refer merely to the personal faith of each believer, but to the content of the Christian faith that the church received from the apostles. Therefore, defending the faith does not mean inventing a new doctrine, but preserving and faithfully transmitting the apostolic revelation.

8. Grace Turned into Immorality

Jude warns about people who “pervert the grace of our God into sensuality” (Jude 1: 4). Here one of the most dangerous distortions confronting the church appears: using God’s grace as an excuse to persist in sin. Biblical grace does not give a person permission to rebel; rather, it frees him from sin’s dominion and leads him into a life of holiness.

9. Denying the Only Master

Jude continues describing these people, saying that they “deny our only Master and Lord, Jesus Christ” (Jude 1: 4). The connection between moral deviation and denial of Christ’s lordship is extremely important: when Christ loses his place as Lord and Master, grace becomes an excuse for justifying conduct that rejects his commands.

10. Examples of Judgment in the Old Testament

Jude draws on well-known historical events in Scripture to affirm that God does not overlook rebellion. He mentions the people whom the Lord saved from the land of Egypt and then destroyed because of unbelief, the angels who did not keep their own domain, and Sodom and Gomorrah, which were made an example of judgment. In this way, he uses salvation history itself to warn the church against believing that belonging to God’s community prevents judgment if a person continues in rebellion.

11. The People Who Came Out of Egypt

Jude says: “I want to remind you, although you once fully knew it, that Jesus, who saved a people out of the land of Egypt, afterward destroyed those who did not believe” (Jude 1: 5).

This example places a clear principle before the church: previous experience of God’s gifts does not replace continuing faith and obedience. The exodus from Egypt was a great work of salvation, but unbelief led to falling in the wilderness.

12. The Fallen Angels

Jude also mentions: “And the angels who did not stay within their own position of authority, but left their proper dwelling, he has kept in eternal chains under gloomy darkness until the judgment of the great day” (Jude 1: 6). This example affirms that rebellion against the order established by God is not a trivial matter, and that even spiritual beings are not above God’s authority and judgment.

13. Sodom and Gomorrah

Jude presents Sodom and Gomorrah as a third example of judgment, saying that they “serve as an example by undergoing a punishment of eternal fire” (Jude 1: 7). He connects sexual deviation with rebellion against God’s purpose. He uses the story to affirm that moral sin is not separate from faith, but is part of a person’s attitude toward God’s authority.

14. The Archangel and Moses

Jude mentions a unique incident: “But when the archangel Michael, contending with the devil, was disputing about the body of Moses, he did not presume to pronounce a blasphemous judgment, but said, ‘The Lord rebuke you’” (Jude 1: 9). This is one of the letter’s most debated passages because of its connection with a Jewish tradition outside the canonical books of the Old Testament. In Jude’s context, the important point is that Michael himself does not deal with the devil independently of God’s authority, but leaves final judgment to the Lord.

15. Cain, Balaam, and Korah

Jude compares the deceivers with three figures from biblical history: Cain, Balaam, and Korah. Cain represents the way of jealousy and hostility; Balaam represents using spiritual matters to obtain gain; and Korah represents rebellion against the authority established by God. Jude brings these images together to reveal that the problem is not merely an intellectual error, but a spirit of rebellion, corruption, lust, and self-interest.

16. Love Feasts and Christian Fellowship

Jude describes these people as “hidden reefs at your love feasts” (Jude 1: 12). Love feasts were associated with the communal life of the early church, so the presence of these people within them reveals the danger of corruption infiltrating the heart of Christian fellowship. Jude portrays them as dangerous elements that do not nourish the community but threaten it.

17. Images of Spiritual Corruption

Jude uses a powerful series of natural images to describe these people: “waterless clouds, swept along by winds; fruitless trees in late autumn, twice dead, uprooted” (Jude 1: 12). The central image is appearance without fruit; a teacher may appear spiritual, but if he bears no fruit of holiness and truth, he is inwardly empty.

18. Wandering Stars

He also describes them as “wandering stars, for whom the gloom of utter darkness has been reserved forever” (Jude 1: 13). The image points to a loss of direction; instead of leading others along the way of truth, the teacher himself becomes a source of error. This is a grave warning about the danger of spiritual leadership when it becomes detached from truth and holiness.

19. Enoch and Judgment

Jude cites a prophecy attributed to Enoch: “Behold, the Lord came with ten thousands of his holy ones, to execute judgment on all and to convict all the ungodly of all their deeds of ungodliness that they have committed in such an ungodly way, and of all the harsh things that ungodly sinners have spoken against him” (Jude 1: 14–15).

The important point here is that Jude uses material known in the Jewish environment to affirm a basic biblical truth: God’s judgment on evil. His mere quotation from a literary tradition does not mean that everything contained in that source became part of biblical revelation.

20. Grumbling and Craving

Jude describes the deceivers as “grumblers, malcontents, following their own sinful desires” (Jude 1: 16). This reveals that corrupt teaching ultimately manifests itself in corruption of the heart and conduct; the person who makes his desires the standard of truth will end up reinterpreting the faith to suit his cravings.

21. The Words of the Apostles

Jude moves believers from warning to remembering apostolic teaching: “But you must remember, beloved, the predictions of the apostles of our Lord Jesus Christ” (Jude 1: 17). Here once again the centrality of apostolic testimony appears as the criterion for distinguishing sound teaching from distorted teaching.

22. Scoffers in the Last Days

Jude says that the apostles warned of “scoffers, following their own ungodly passions” (Jude 1: 18). This image connects mocking the truth with the desire to escape God’s authority; a person may mock judgment in order to justify continuing on his own path.

23. Those Who Cause Division

He says: “It is these who cause divisions, worldly people, devoid of the Spirit” (Jude 1: 19). The problem is not merely intellectual disagreement, but the creation of divisions within the body of Christ. False teaching does not remain at the level of ideas; it produces factionalism, division, and the weakening of fellowship.

24. Build Yourselves Up in the Faith

After the lengthy section of warning, Jude turns to the responsibility of believers: “But you, beloved, building yourselves up in your most holy faith and praying in the Holy Spirit” (Jude 1: 20).

Here he presents an integrated spiritual strategy for confronting error: building oneself up in the faith, praying, remaining in God’s love, and waiting for Christ’s mercy.

25. Keep Yourselves in the Love of God

He says: “Keep yourselves in the love of God, waiting for the mercy of our Lord Jesus Christ that leads to eternal life” (Jude 1: 21). The letter does not end with fear of deceivers, but by returning the believer to God’s love and the hope of eternal life.

26. Mercy for Those Who Doubt

Jude says: “And have mercy on those who doubt” (Jude 1: 22). This is very important: rejecting error does not mean rejecting every person who is struggling with uncertainty. There is a difference between the deliberate deceiver and the person who doubts or needs instruction and mercy. Therefore, the church needs discernment, not blind reactions.

27. Save Others from the Fire

He says: “Save others by snatching them out of the fire; to others show mercy with fear, hating even the garment stained by the flesh” (Jude 1: 23). Here the evangelistic and pastoral dimension of the letter appears: the church does not merely defend itself, but seeks to rescue those affected by error while maintaining holiness and avoiding participation in sin.

28. Distinguishing Between the Person and the Sin

This passage reveals a profoundly important balance: the church can show mercy to a person and seek to rescue him, while at the same time rejecting sin and false teaching. This establishes an important pastoral principle: love does not mean participating in sin, and discernment does not mean abandoning mercy.

29. Divine Preservation

After calling believers to keep themselves, Jude concludes by declaring God’s preserving power: “Now to Him who is able to keep you from stumbling, and to present you faultless before His glory with exceeding joy” (Jude 1: 24). Thus, the believer’s confidence does not rest only on his own ability, but on God’s ability to preserve and establish.

30. Glory to God

Jude concludes with a great doxology: “To the only wise God our Savior, be glory and majesty, dominion and power, both now and forever. Amen” (Jude 1: 25). After the warning against error and judgment, the book ends with the believer’s eyes fixed on God, not on the danger; because God is the Savior, the Preserver, and the One who possesses final authority.

31. Links with the Old Testament

The Epistle of Jude is very rich in biblical background; it evokes the exodus from Egypt, the rebellion of the people in the wilderness, Sodom and Gomorrah, Cain, Balaam, and Korah, and it is grounded in the concepts of judgment, holiness, and obedience that fill the books of the Old Testament. Thus Jude places the church in continuity with the history of God’s people, showing that the God who judged rebellion in the Old Testament is the same God who demands holiness and steadfastness from the church.

32. The Relationship to Apostolic Teaching

Jude places the faith once delivered to the saints at the heart of the epistle, then asks believers to remember the words of the apostles. Therefore, the church’s authority in confronting deceptive teaching does not rest on personal opinions or changing cultural trends, but on the apostolic revelation that the church received. This gives the epistle special importance in the matter of preserving doctrine.

33. The Epistle of Jude and the Second Epistle of Peter

There are clear similarities between Jude and 2 Peter, especially in the warning against false teachers, the use of examples of judgment, the description of moral corruption, and the warning against scoffing in the last days. Many scholars hold that there is a literary or traditional relationship between the two epistles, but defining the precise nature of that relationship remains a matter of debate. What is important theologically is that the two epistles testify to the early church’s concern over teaching that uses Christianity to justify corruption.

34. The Major Movement of the Epistle

The epistle begins with a greeting focused on calling, sanctification, and preservation, then quickly moves to the necessity of contending for the faith delivered to the saints. After that, Jude exposes the entry of corrupt people into the community, and demonstrates from the history of the exodus, the angels, and Sodom and Gomorrah the certainty of God’s judgment. He then uses the images of Cain, Balaam, Korah, clouds without water, trees without fruit, and wandering stars to reveal the nature of the deceivers. After that, he returns to the teaching of the apostles and calls believers to build themselves up on the faith, to pray, to remain steadfast in God’s love, and to wait for the mercy of Christ, while practicing mercy and rescue toward those who have been affected by error. In the end, he lifts the church’s gaze from the danger to God, who is able to keep it from falling and to present it before His glory without blemish.

35. The Most Important Theological Message

The Epistle of Jude reveals that preserving the faith and preserving holiness are inseparable; apostolic faith is not a doctrinal system without moral effect, grace is not a permit for sin, and love is not acceptance of error. The church is called to defend the truth, practice discernment, and warn against corrupt teaching, while at the same time showing mercy to the doubtful and seeking to rescue those who have fallen under the influence of error. All of this ultimately takes place under the authority of God, who is able to preserve His people.

36. Encyclopedic Summary

The Epistle of Jude is the epistle of defending the faith amid the infiltration of corruption into the Christian community. Jude begins by declaring believers to be called, sanctified, and preserved in Christ, then reveals the necessity of contending for the faith once delivered to the saints, because people had entered the church and turned the grace of God into a license for sin and denied the sovereignty of the Lord Jesus Christ. He draws on the history of Scripture to affirm that God is not lenient toward unbelief, rebellion, and corruption, and he evokes the examples of Israel in the wilderness, the angels, Sodom and Gomorrah, Cain, Balaam, and Korah. He then portrays the deceivers as fruitless, wandering, and dangerous to the community of believers. Yet he does not leave the church in a passive defensive posture, but calls it to build itself up on the faith, to pray in the Holy Spirit, to remain steadfast in the love of God, and to wait for the mercy of Christ, while exercising mercy, discernment, and rescue toward those who have fallen within the sphere of error. The epistle ends with praise to God, who is able to keep believers from stumbling and to present them before His glory without blemish. Thus the epistle moves from danger to discernment, from discernment to steadfastness, from steadfastness to divine preservation, and from preservation to glory to God.
