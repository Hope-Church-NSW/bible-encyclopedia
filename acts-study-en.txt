The Book of Acts
1. The Name of the Book

The book is known as Acts, a name that reflects its general theme: the extension of Christ's work after His resurrection and ascension through the apostles and the church by the power of the Holy Spirit. Nevertheless, the book does not record the deeds of all the apostles equally; rather, it focuses especially on Peter's ministry in the first section and on Paul's ministry in the larger portion of the second section.

The deeper name that expresses the book's content is that it is the record of the risen Christ's work through the Holy Spirit in the church. Therefore, the apostles are not independent heroes of the book, but instruments in God's hand for the completion of the gospel message.

2. The Author

The author of the book is Luke, the same author as the third Gospel. The Acts is directly related to the Gospel, for it begins by referring to the first book Luke wrote concerning the life and teaching of Christ.

The author's unity is evident in style, language, and theological structure. The author's occasional shift from the third person to the first person plural in the "we" sections also indicates his participation in some of Paul's journeys, especially in the later stages of his ministry.

3. The Time and Place of Writing

It is generally held that the book was written in the second half of the first century AD, and many scholars connect its ending to Paul's period of residence in Rome, since the narrative ends while Paul is still alive and preaching in Rome, without mentioning his final trial or death.

This silence is not necessarily conclusive evidence of a precise date, but it is consistent with Luke's intention to end his account when the gospel reached the heart of the Roman Empire, leaving open the continuing mission of the church.

4. The Audience and Purpose

The book is addressed to Theophilus, as was the Gospel according to Luke. At the same time, however, it speaks to the church and to subsequent Christian generations who need to understand how the message of Christ passed from Jerusalem to the world.

The purpose is not merely to record the history of the early church, but to show that Christ's resurrection and ascension did not end His work; rather, they ushered the church into a new phase in which Christ works through the Holy Spirit and guides the apostles in bearing witness to the gospel to the ends of the earth.

5. The Central Theological Idea

The theological center of the book is the work of the Holy Spirit in the extension of God's kingdom from Jerusalem to the nations.

The Holy Spirit is not merely a power that gives the apostles courage, but the divine presence who guides the church, empowers it for witness, reveals God's will, opens the doors to the nations, and directs the apostles in their decisions and ministry.

For this reason, the theological movement of the book can be summarized in the words of Christ:

"You will be My witnesses in Jerusalem, and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the ends of the earth."
(Acts 1: 8).

This verse does not merely offer a promise; it also provides the geographical and theological map of the journey.

6. The Main Themes
The resurrection of Christ, His ascension, and the continuation of His work.
The outpouring of the Holy Spirit and the founding of the church.
Witness to the gospel in the power of the Holy Spirit.
The growth of the church amid resistance and persecution.
The transition of the proclamation from the Jews to the nations.
Salvation through faith in Christ.
The unity of the church between Jews and Gentiles.
God's sovereignty over the course of the proclamation despite opposition.
Peter's ministry, then the widening of Paul's ministry.
The arrival of the gospel in Rome, the center of the Roman world.
7. The Major Characters

Peter appears prominently in the first half, as he moves from the disciple who denied Christ to a bold witness who proclaims the resurrection before the authorities and the crowds.

Then the center of the narrative gradually shifts to Paul, who had persecuted the church but became the apostle of Christ to the nations. His missionary journeys, imprisonment, and trials become the path by which the gospel reaches Rome.

Other figures also appear, such as John, Stephen, Philip, Barnabas, James, Agabus, Silas, Timothy, Lydia, Aquila and Priscilla, along with Gentile and Roman characters through whom God reveals that the circle of the gospel continues to widen.

8. The Major Movement of the Book

Acts begins in Jerusalem, where the risen Christ appears to His disciples, confirms the reality of His resurrection, promises them the Holy Spirit, and then ascends to heaven. He does not leave them without a mandate, but makes them His witnesses and begins the church's waiting for the outpouring of the Holy Spirit.

On the fiftieth day, the Holy Spirit descends, and the church is born in its public form. Peter proclaims that Jesus who was crucified is the Christ and the Lord. Thus the narrative begins in Jerusalem, and it becomes clear that the resurrection is not an event confined to the past, but a living reality that creates a new community and forms a new witness.

The church then faces resistance and persecution, yet the central paradox of the book is that persecution does not stop the spread of the word; rather, it becomes one of the means God uses to widen the circle of witness. With the martyrdom of Stephen and the scattering of believers, the message reaches new regions, and Samaria begins to receive the proclamation.

After this comes the great turning point in the acceptance of the Gentiles, especially in the story of Cornelius, where God tells Peter that what God has made clean should not be called unclean by him. Here the church expands not only geographically, but also undergoes a profound theological shift: the Gentiles also enter the people of God by faith in Christ and the receiving of the Holy Spirit.

Then Paul appears more and more prominently, and the gospel moves with him from Judea to Asia Minor, then to Macedonia and Greece, until it reaches major cultural, intellectual, and commercial centers such as Philippi, Thessalonica, Corinth, and Ephesus. In each city, the same conflict repeats itself: the proclamation of the gospel, the acceptance by some, the resistance by others, and then the continued advance of the word.

In the final phase, Paul's own journey becomes a path for the gospel. He is arrested in Jerusalem, tried before the authorities, and then appeals to Caesar, going to Rome. Even on the sea voyage, in the storm, and in the shipwreck, God remains sovereign over events; Paul and those with him are saved, and he finally reaches Rome.

The narrative ends strikingly: Paul is in Rome, imprisoned, yet he proclaims the kingdom of God and teaches about the Lord Jesus Christ with all boldness, without hindrance. Here is the deep message of the end of the book: the apostle may be bound, but the word of God is not bound.

9. The Book's Relationship to the Old Testament

The Book of Acts does not present a gospel separate from the story of the Old Testament, but declares that what happened in Christ is the extension of God's promises to Israel. Peter and the apostles use the Psalms and the prophets to establish the resurrection, the kingdom, and the new covenant, and the sermons of the book show that God works according to a plan that began with the fathers and the prophets.

Thus the church in Acts becomes part of the great biblical story, not a community that suddenly appeared, but a community that sees in Christ the fulfillment of God's promises and in the entrance of the nations an extension of the promise that God's blessing reaches all peoples.

10. The Main Message

The central message of Acts is that the risen Jesus Christ is Lord, and that His work continues in the world through the Holy Spirit and the church.

The church does not spread by its human power, and neither political, religious, nor cultural opposition can stop God's purpose. As the word moves from Jerusalem to Judea and Samaria and then to the nations, it becomes clear that God's saving purpose is directed toward the whole world.

11. Key to understanding the book

The entire book can be understood through three interconnected words:

Christ — the Holy Spirit — the testimony.

Christ is the one who sends the message, the Holy Spirit is the one who empowers and guides the church, and the apostles and believers are the witnesses who carry the gospel to the world.

12. Conclusion

The Book of Acts is a bridge between the Gospels and the Epistles; it explains how the proclamation of the risen Christ moved from the events of the Gospel in Jerusalem to the establishment of churches to which many of the New Testament letters would later be addressed.

If the Gospel according to Luke declares what Jesus began to do and teach, then the Acts of the Apostles declares how this work continued after the ascension by the Holy Spirit in the life of the church. Therefore, the book is not merely 'the Acts of the Apostles,' but can be seen theologically as the acts of the risen Christ through the Holy Spirit in and through his apostles and church, until the gospel reaches the ends of the earth.
