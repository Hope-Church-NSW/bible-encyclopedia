Introduction to the Book of Malachi

The Central Idea of the Book: Malachi is the last of the Old Testament books in the order of the Christian Bible, and therefore stands as a gateway between the age of the prophets and the expectation of the Lord’s coming. It addresses a people who had returned from exile, rebuilt the temple, and apparently restored worship, but the problem had become deeper: the heart itself had turned away from God. Therefore, Malachi confronts not so much overt idolatry as spiritual coldness, formal worship, corrupt priesthood, marital unfaithfulness, injustice, doubt concerning God’s justice, and the theft of what belongs to him. The book ends with a twofold promise: the Lord will come to his temple, and before the coming of the great day of the Lord, “Elijah the prophet” will come to prepare the way. Thus Malachi is directly connected with the ministry of John the Baptist and the expectation of Christ’s coming.

1. Name of the book and its meaning

The name of the book is Malachi — מַלְאָכִי — Mal’akhi, a Hebrew name that directly means “my messenger,” “my angel,” or “my messenger,” from the Hebrew word מַלְאָךְ — mal’akh, meaning “messenger” or “angel,” with the first-person suffix “-i.”

This meaning is especially important because the word “Malachi” appears in the book itself in the statement:

“Behold, I send my messenger, and he will prepare the way before me.”

For this reason, scholars have debated whether “Malachi” is the name of an actual prophet or a functional description meaning “my messenger,” but ancient Jewish and Christian tradition treated it as the prophet’s name.

2. Who Was Malachi?

The Bible provides little information about the person of Malachi. It does not mention his father, his city, or details of his personal life, as we find in some other prophetic books.

But the style of the book reveals a prophet working in the post-exilic period, when Jerusalem had returned to life and the temple was standing, but the people and the priests had entered a state of lukewarmness and gradual deviation.

Herein lies the power of Malachi: he does not address a people far removed from religion, but a people who practice religion without having their hearts devoted to God.

3. The Year of Malachi’s Birth and Death

The Bible does not record the year of Malachi’s birth or the year of his death. Nor is there reliable historical information that allows them to be determined precisely.

But most studies place his ministry in the Persian period after the rebuilding of the temple, and many scholars consider it likely that his ministry came in the first half of the fifth century B.C., perhaps around 450–430 B.C.

4. Date of writing the book

The book does not mention the name of the king who was ruling in Malachi’s days, and therefore the dating depends on the social and religious conditions it describes.

The temple had been rebuilt, the priests were serving, sacrifices were being offered, and the people were living in Judah, but religious and social corruption had spread.

For this reason, many scholars see the book as close in time to the era of Ezra and Nehemiah, although the precise relationship between Malachi and Nehemiah’s reforms remains a matter of debate.

5. To Whom Did Malachi Write?

Malachi wrote to the people of Israel in Judah after the exile, and to the priests in particular.

The message often begins with dialogue between God and the people, then directs a direct accusation against the priests, because the corruption of spiritual leadership was leading to the corruption of worship and public life.

6. Reason for writing the book

The problem was not that the people had completely stopped offering sacrifices, but that the sacrifices were being offered in a way that revealed inward contempt for God.

They were offering the sick or lame animal, the priests were permitting this, the people were asking why God did not respond, and then they began to doubt God’s love and justice.

Therefore Malachi came to put the picture back in order:

God loves you, but you no longer love him as you should.

7. Malachi’s Distinctive Style

One of the most important characteristics of the book is its style of dialogue, objection, and answer.

God declares something, and the people respond with a question such as:

“How have you loved us?”

Then:

“How have we defiled you?”

Then:

“How have we wearied him?”

Then:

“Where is the God of justice?”

Thus the book reveals not only the sins of the people, but also the way of thinking of the heart that had begun to justify itself before God.

8. The Division of the Book of Malachi

The book may be divided into six major dialogical units:

God’s love for Israel — Malachi 1:1–5.
The corruption of the priests and the sacrifices — Malachi 1:6–2:9.
Marital unfaithfulness and covenant violation — Malachi 2:10–16.
Doubt concerning God’s justice and the coming of the Lord — Malachi 2:17–3:5.
Robbing God and returning to him — Malachi 3:6–12.
The distinction between the righteous and the wicked and the day of the LORD — Malachi 3:13–4:6.
9. The First Message — “How Have You Loved Us?”

God opens the book with a declaration:

“I have loved you, says the LORD.”

But the people answer:

“How have you loved us?”

This reveals the dangerous spiritual condition they had reached. The question was no longer: Does God love us? Rather, in practice they had begun to doubt the reality of his love.

10. Jacob and Esau

God responds by reminding them of the relationship between Jacob and Esau.

The aim here is not for the book to present a detailed lesson in the doctrine of election, but to remind Israel that the history of its very existence is a witness to God’s grace, election, and care.

The people who had forgotten God’s love needed to remember how their history originally began with God.

11. “My Name Is Great among the Nations”

The book moves to the subject of worship.

The LORD says that his name is great among the nations, but his people, who bear his name, have come to treat worship with contempt.

Here the paradox appears:

The nations will know the greatness of God, while the covenant people despise his name.

12. “Offer It to the Governor”

The people were offering defective animals to the LORD.

So God says:

“When you offer the blind as a sacrifice, is that not evil? And when you offer the lame and the sick, is that not evil? Offer it to your governor; will he be pleased with you or show you favor? says the LORD of hosts.”

The problem is not only the value of the animal, but the value of God in the heart of the one offering the sacrifice.

13. Worship Reveals a Person’s View of God

A person was able to offer the best to a human ruler, yet he offered what was inferior to God.

Here Malachi reveals a spiritual principle of profound depth:

The way you worship reveals the extent of your perception of God’s greatness.

Worship is not merely a ritual, but a practical declaration of who God is in a person’s eyes.

14. The Corruption of the Priests

The LORD says to the priests:

“And now, this commandment is for you, O priests.”

The priest who was supposed to lead the people into holiness had become part of the problem.

Therefore true reform does not begin only with the masses; it must also begin with spiritual leadership.

15. “From the Rising of the Sun to Its Setting”

The LORD declares that his name will be great among the nations.

Here one of Malachi’s greatest themes appears: the greatness of the LORD’s name is not confined to Israel.

God wants his name to be known among the peoples, and true worship is connected with this universal proclamation of his glory.

16. Malachi 2 — The Covenant of Levi

The book speaks about God’s covenant with Levi and about the priest’s responsibility to preserve knowledge and instruction.

Then it says:

“For the lips of a priest preserve knowledge, and from his mouth they seek the law, because he is the messenger of the LORD of hosts.”

Notice the connection between the priest and the word “messenger.”

The true priest does not live for himself, but carries the knowledge of God to the people.

17. The Corruption of Teaching

If the priest becomes corrupt, the problem does not stop with the sacrifices.

Corruption reaches teaching, discernment, the application of the law, and the example set before the people.

Therefore Malachi regards spiritual leadership as a very serious responsibility, because error in it can lead an entire nation into deviation.

18. Marital Unfaithfulness

The book moves to another problem:

“You deal treacherously, each man with his brother.”

Then it speaks about betrayal of the marriage covenant.

This is very important because Malachi connects faithfulness to God with faithfulness in marriage.

19. Marriage and Covenant

The book does not present marriage as merely a social relationship, but connects it with covenant.

For this reason, marital unfaithfulness belongs in the same category as covenant unfaithfulness toward God.

A person who learns to betray his covenant with those closest to him may thereby reveal a deeper problem in his understanding of faithfulness and commitment.

20. “Did He not make them one?”

Malachi connects marriage with God's creational and covenantal purpose.

This text is very important in studying the Bible's concept of marriage, because it connects the marital relationship with unity, offspring, covenant, and responsibility before God.

21. “I Hate Divorce”

Malachi 2:16 contains a well-known statement:

“For I hate divorce,” says the LORD, the God of Israel.”

This verse should not be isolated from its context. The book speaks of betrayal and treachery against the covenant, rather than issuing a simplistic judgment that ignores all the circumstances addressed elsewhere in the Bible.

22. “Where Is the God of Justice?”

The people arrive at a serious question:

“Where is the God of justice?”

They saw the wicked prospering and therefore began to question God's justice.

This issue directly prepares the way for the book's final subject: the day of the LORD, judgment, and the distinction between the righteous and the wicked.

23. “Behold, I Send My Messenger”

A great declaration follows:

“Behold, I send my messenger, and he will prepare the way before me.”

This verse is especially important in the study of the New Testament, because the Gospels connect the idea of preparing the way with the ministry of John the Baptist.

24. “The Lord Will Suddenly Come to His Temple”

Then it says:

“The Lord whom you seek will suddenly come to His temple, and the Messenger of the covenant, in whom you delight; behold, He is coming,” says the LORD of hosts.”

This is among the most important Messianic verses in the book.

The awaited person comes to the temple, but He comes not merely to comfort the people; He comes to begin a process of purification and judgment.

25. Why Does the Lord Come to the Temple?

The theological question here is important:

Is the temple a place where a person can hide from judgment?

Malachi answers: No.

The Lord who comes to the temple comes as a refiner's fire, as soap that cleanses, and as a judge who examines.

26. “Like a Refiner's Fire”

He says:

“But who can endure the day of His coming? And who can stand when He appears? For He is like a refiner's fire and like launderers' soap.”

The image is not merely one of punishment, but of purification.

Fire removes impurities from metal, and the washer removes filth from a garment.

27. “He Will Sit as a Refiner and Purifier”

He says:

“He will sit as a refiner and purifier of silver, and He will purify the sons of Levi.”

God does not merely want to destroy what is corrupt; He wants to purify worship itself.

Here, the coming of the Lord becomes an event that bears two aspects:

Judgment of evil.

And purification of God's people.

28. Social Injustice

After this, the book mentions sorcery, adultery, and falsehood, then:

“Against those who deprive the hired worker of his wages, and the widow and the fatherless, and who turn aside the sojourner.”

This confirms that true religion cannot be separated from social justice.

29. “I, the LORD, Do Not Change”

The Lord says:

“For I, the LORD, do not change; therefore you, O sons of Jacob, have not been consumed.”

This statement has a twofold meaning.

God's constancy means that He is faithful to His covenant; therefore, the people of Jacob were not completely destroyed despite their sin.

But God's constancy also means that His moral standards do not change.

30. “Return to Me, and I Will Return to You”

The call comes:

“Return to Me, and I will return to you,” says the LORD of hosts.”

This statement strongly echoes the book of Zechariah:

“Return to Me ... and I will return to you.”

The post-exilic prophets were not constructing a new, separate message; rather, they were calling that very generation to a genuine return to God.

31. “How Shall We Return?”

Once again, the people reply:

“How shall we return?”

This question reveals that they do not see the problem at all.

And God answers:

“Will a man rob God? Yet you have robbed Me.”

32. “In Tithes and Offerings”

God identifies the issue:

“In tithes and offerings.”

In the Old Testament context, tithes and offerings were part of the system of worship, the support of ministry, and care for the temple and the poor according to the ordinances of the law.

Therefore, the text should not be reduced to an automatic financial promise; rather, it must be understood within the relationship of covenant, worship, and obedience.

33. “Bring the Whole Tithe”

He says:

“Bring the whole tithe into the storehouse, that there may be food in My house, and test Me in this,” says the LORD of hosts.”

This verse has become one of the most widely used texts in Christian teaching on giving.

But a careful reading places it within the context of Israel, the temple, and the old covenantal system, while allowing spiritual principles concerning faithfulness, giving, and placing God at the center to be drawn from it.

34. “I Will Open the Windows of Heaven for You”

The Lord promises blessing:

“If I will not open the windows of heaven for you and pour out for you a blessing until there is no room enough.”

But we should not turn the verse into a commercial formula: give money to God in order to receive more money.

The context speaks of a covenant people, land, harvest, worship, and faithfulness; therefore, the central message is the restoration of faithfulness to God.

35. The Book of Remembrance

In the third chapter, an entirely different scene appears.

Those who fear the LORD speak with one another; the LORD listens and writes before Him a book of remembrance for those who fear Him.

Here we find an image that contrasts with the people who said that serving God was of no benefit.

God does not forget the faithful.

36. “They Shall Be Mine, My Treasured Possession”

The Lord says:

“They shall be Mine,” says the LORD of hosts, “on the day when I make them My treasured possession.”

That is, God knows those who are His, even when the wicked appear to be better off.

37. “Distinguish Between the Righteous and the Wicked”

He says:

“Then you will again distinguish between the righteous and the wicked, between one who serves God and one who does not serve Him.”

And this verse directly addresses the problem of doubt concerning God's justice.

The distinction may not be clear now, but the Day of the Lord will reveal the truth.

38. Chapter Four — the Day of the Lord

He says:

"For behold, the day is coming, burning like a furnace, and all the arrogant and all evildoers will be chaff."

This is a picture of the final judgment.

But the journey does not end with fear alone; it also gives a glorious picture of the faithful.

39. "The Sun of Righteousness, with Healing in Its Wings"

He says:

"But for you who fear My name, the Sun of Righteousness will rise, with healing in its wings."

This is one of the most beautiful images in Malachi.

In contrast to the fire of judgment that burns the wicked, there is the Sun of Righteousness that bears healing for the faithful who fear the name of the Lord.

40. "You shall go out and leap like calves from the stall"

He says:

"You shall go out and leap like calves from the stall."

The image expresses freedom, joy, and strength after salvation.

The faithful do not live forever under the shadow of evil; rather, the day will come when the light of righteousness will shine.

41. "And you shall trample the wicked"

The Lord declares that the wicked will be like ashes under the feet of the faithful on the day He makes.

This does not call the believer to personal vengeance, but proclaims that the final judgment belongs to God, and that evil will not prevail forever.

42. "Remember the Law of Moses"

Before the voice of prophecy falls silent, Malachi says:

"Remember the Law of Moses My servant."

This is a highly important conclusion to the Old Testament.

The prophets did not come to abolish the Law, but to restore the people to the covenant with God and to obedience.

43. "Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophet"

Then come the final words:

"Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophet before the coming of the great and dreadful day of the Lord."

This statement opens directly into the New Testament.

44. "The heart of fathers shall be turned to their children"

He says:

"And the heart of fathers shall be turned to their children, and the heart of children to their fathers."

This is not merely a call to improve family relationships, but a picture of restoring the covenant, the community, and the hearts to God.

45. Malachi and John the Baptist

When John the Baptist came, the New Testament used Malachi's language to describe his mission.

Luke announces that John goes before the Lord "in the spirit and power of Elijah."

This shows that the New Testament saw in John's ministry the fulfillment of the expected line of Elijah, not in the sense that John was literally Elijah returned from the dead, but that he bore the prophetic role, power, and message associated with Elijah.

46. Malachi and the Messiah

It may be said that Malachi stands at the threshold of the New Testament.

He closes the Old Testament in expectation of:

a messenger who prepares the way;

Then:

the Lord who comes to His temple;

Then:

The day of the Lord.

Then:

the Sun of Righteousness;

Then:

Elijah who prepares the people.

This makes the book seem like an arrow of prophecy directed toward the beginning of the Gospels.

47. "The Angel of the Covenant"

The phrase "Angel of the Covenant" is one of the passages around which extensive interpretive debate has revolved.

Some interpreters understand it within the framework of the divine angel associated with the covenant, while others see it as referring to the messenger or mediator associated with the proclamation of the covenant. In Christian reading, the text has become strongly connected to the idea of the coming of Christ to His temple.

Therefore, the text should be studied while preserving the distinction between the direct historical meaning and the Christian reading in the New Testament.

48. Malachi and the Temple

The temple in Malachi already exists, but the existence of the building did not solve the problem.

This is a point of great importance: it is possible for the temple to stand and worship to continue, while the heart remains far from God.

For this reason, the Lord Himself comes to the temple to purify worship.

49. Malachi and Formal Worship

Malachi's problem is not the absence of ritual but the presence of ritual without reverence.

The people offer worship, but in the wrong way.

The priests serve, but without faithfulness.

The people speak of God, but they doubt His justice.

For this reason, the book poses a question before every believing community: are we worshiping God because we know Him, or because we are accustomed to practicing worship?

50. Malachi and the Fear of God

The concept of the fear of the Lord recurs throughout the book.

The fear of the Lord is not merely fear of punishment, but a deep reverence for God's holiness and authority.

In the end, the book distinguishes between:

those who serve God.

and those who do not serve Him.

51. Malachi and Spiritual Leadership

One of the strongest messages of the book is that the corruption of religious leadership can corrupt worship as a whole.

The priest is responsible for:

knowledge, teaching, example, sacrifice, and discernment.

Therefore, he must be a faithful servant of God and not merely a religious official.

52. Malachi and Marriage

Malachi links the covenant with God to the marital covenant.

God, who demands faithfulness in worship, also demands faithfulness in marriage.

This is a very important moral unity: a person cannot claim faithfulness to God while living a life built on betrayal and infidelity.

53. Malachi and Justice

The question:

“Where is the God of justice?”

finds its answer in the Day of the Lord.

The judgment may be delayed, but delay does not mean absence.

The wicked may prosper for a time, but their success is not the final word.

God will come and distinguish between the righteous and the wicked.

54. Malachi and the Time between the Covenants

From the Christian perspective, the historical importance of Malachi is immense because it is the last prophetic voice in the order of the Old Testament.

After it comes a relatively long period in which no similar biblical prophecy appears until the coming of John the Baptist.

Therefore, Malachi becomes a literary and theological bridge between the expectation of the Old Testament and the proclamation of the New Testament.

55. Key Persons in the Book
Malachi

The prophet who confronted religious apathy and called for renewed faithfulness to God.

The priests

The spiritual leaders who faced severe rebuke because of the corruption of worship and teaching.

The people of Israel

The community that questioned God's love and justice and was called to repent and return.

James

Represents the covenant people in the discussion of God's love and election.

Esau

Appears in the comparison God uses to remind Israel of His relationship with Jacob's family.

Elijah

The prophetic figure associated with preparing the way before the day of the Lord.

The Lord / Messenger of the Covenant

The expected figure who comes to the temple and with whom an important messianic interpretation is associated.

56. Key Events in the Book

The declaration of God's love for Jacob; the people's questioning of God's love; the rebuke of the priests; corrupt sacrifices; dishonoring the name of the Lord; discussion of the covenant with Levi; corruption of priestly teaching; marital unfaithfulness; doubt concerning God's justice; the promise to send the messenger; the promise of the Lord's coming to the temple; the declaration of purification and judgment; the call to return; the subject of tithes and offerings; the Book of Remembrance; the distinction between the righteous and the wicked; the declaration of the day of the Lord; the Sun of Righteousness; then the promise to send Elijah the prophet before the day of the Lord.

57. The Main Message of the Book

The Book of Malachi reveals that the most dangerous spiritual condition is not for a person to abandon religion completely, but to continue practicing worship while losing the fear of God in the heart. The temple was standing, sacrifices were being offered, and the priests were serving, but God saw in all this apathy and dishonor to His name. Therefore, He confronted the people with a series of questions that reveal that the problem was not the absence of religion but the absence of the heart. He called them to return to Him and to reform worship, leadership, marriage, giving, and justice. Then He declared that the coming of the Lord would reveal everything, that the Lord would come to His temple to purify and refine, and that the day of the Lord would come, bringing judgment upon evil and healing for those who fear Him. Therefore, the book ends not by closing the story but by opening the door of expectation: the messenger will come, the Lord will come, the Sun of Righteousness will rise, and God will finally declare who truly serves Him.

58. The Major Movement of the Book of Malachi

The book may be summarized in this sequence:

“I have loved you.”

Then:

“How have You loved us?”

Then:

Corrupt worship.

Then:

Corrupt priesthood.

Then:

Marital unfaithfulness.

Then:

“Where is the God of justice?”

Then:

“Behold, I send My messenger.”

Then:

“The Lord will suddenly come to His temple.”

Then:

The refining fire.

Then:

“Return to Me.”

Then:

“How shall we return?”

Then:

Faithfulness in tithes and offerings.

Then:

The Book of Remembrance.

Then:

The distinction between the righteous and the wicked.

Then:

The day of the Lord.

Then:

“The Sun of Righteousness, with healing in its wings.”

Then:

“Remember the Law of Moses.”

Then:

“Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophet.”

Thus, the book ends with the silence of the prophets and the expectation of the coming of the Lord.

🔑 The Sentence That Summarizes the Book of Malachi

“The Book of Malachi is the final call in the succession of Old Testament prophets to awaken a people who know God by name but have lost the fear of Him in their hearts. Therefore, God begins by declaring His love, then exposes the corruption of worship, priesthood, marriage, giving, and justice, and confronts the questions that arose from the heart of a weary people, saying, ‘How have You loved us?’ and ‘Where is the God of justice?’ Then He declares that His apparent silence does not mean His absence, because ‘the Lord’ will come to His temple and will come like a refining fire to purify worship and distinguish between truth and evil. He then opens before the faithful the hope of the ‘Sun of Righteousness,’ which brings healing, and ends the book with the promise of sending Elijah before the day of the Lord. Therefore, Malachi does not close the Old Testament in despair, but in expectation: expectation of the messenger who prepares the way, expectation of the Lord who comes, expectation of the Sun of Righteousness that rises, and expectation of the day on which the difference will appear between the one who serves God and the one who does not serve Him.”
