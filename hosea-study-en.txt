Introduction to the Book of Hosea

The central idea of the book: The Book of Hosea is the book of faithful divine love in the face of human betrayal, the covenant that was broken, the judgment that comes because of spiritual adultery, and then the mercy that opens the door to return, healing, and restoration. Hosea uses his own married life as a painful prophetic sign, making the relationship of the prophet with Gomer an image of the relationship of the Lord with Israel; the people whom God chose left him after other gods. Nevertheless, the book does not end with rejection, but with the call to return. From here the book moves from betrayal to discipline, from discipline to repentance, from repentance to healing, and from healing to fruitfulness, until Hosea becomes one of the deepest books of the Old Testament in revealing God's heart toward his people.

1. Name of the book and its meaning

The name of the book is Hosea, and the Hebrew name is הוֹשֵׁעַ — Hōshēaʿ, and it is connected with the Hebrew root ישע — y-sh-ʿ, which carries the meaning of salvation and deliverance. Therefore the name Hosea is connected with the meaning "salvation" or "the Lord saves," according to the etymological context of the name. The beautiful paradox is that the prophet who bears the name of salvation carries a message that begins by exposing Israel's betrayal, but ends by declaring that true salvation does not come from Baal, nor from political alliances, nor from human strength, but from the Lord alone.

2. Who Is Hosea?

Hosea son of Beeri was a prophet who served primarily in the northern kingdom, Israel, during a period in which the kingdom was experiencing relative political and economic prosperity, but this prosperity was accompanied by deep spiritual and moral deviation. The book presents Hosea as a prophet who lives his message in a very personal way, since God used his marriage and family life as a living sign before the people. Therefore Hosea does not merely present a sermon about betrayal, but makes his own life a prophetic stage that reveals God's relationship with Israel.

3. The Year of Hosea's Birth and Death

The Bible does not record the year of Hosea's birth or the year of his death, and therefore they cannot be determined with certainty. But the book identifies the time of his ministry through the names of the kings in whose days he lived, namely Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah, kings of Judah, and Jeroboam son of Joash, king of Israel. This places his ministry in general in the eighth century BC, the same period that witnessed the ministry of prophets such as Isaiah, Amos, and Micah.

4. Date of writing the book

The historical information within the book indicates that Hosea served some time before the fall of the northern kingdom, and that the timeline continued until the days of Hezekiah. Therefore the book is connected with a stage that preceded the fall of Samaria in approximately 722 BC at the hand of Assyria. A distinction must be made between the time of the prophet's ministry and the stage in which the material was collected or written down in its final form, which is a matter scholars have discussed, but the basic historical framework of the book is clearly connected with the last century of the history of the northern kingdom.

5. For Whom Did Hosea Write?

The primary audience was Israel, that is, the northern kingdom, and therefore the expressions "Israel" and "Ephraim" recur in the book. Nevertheless, the message also reaches Judah, because the prophet sometimes compares the conduct of the two kingdoms and warns that deviation is not a problem belonging to the north alone. The people needed to understand that their real crisis was not only the coming Assyrian danger, but the collapse of the relationship with God from within.

6. Reason for writing the book

Hosea wrote in order to expose Israel's spiritual betrayal, because the people left the Lord and turned to the worship of Baal and idols, and mixed the worship of God with pagan practices. The book also exposes the corruption of the leadership, the priests, and the social classes, and connects religious corruption with moral and political corruption. At the same time, however, it declares that God does not desire the destruction of his people, but wants them to return to him; therefore the book brings together holiness, judgment, and mercy in a moving way.

7. Division of the Book of Hosea

The book is divided into two main sections. Chapters 1–3 focus especially on the marriage of Hosea and Gomer and on the symbolic relationship between the prophet and his wife, and between the Lord and Israel, while chapters 4–14 present a series of prophetic pronouncements against the sins of Israel and call the people to return. The entire structure can be summarized in four words: betrayal, judgment, mercy, restoration.

8. Hosea 1 — Marriage as a Prophetic Message

God commands Hosea to take for himself a woman of prostitution, and his marriage becomes a sign of the Lord's relationship with Israel. This text is among the texts that most require careful reading, because the intent is not to present marital betrayal as something acceptable, but to turn the prophet's personal pain into a prophetic symbol of the people's betrayal of the covenant. Just as marriage represents a covenant between a man and a woman, so the Lord's covenant with Israel was a real relationship of loyalty and love; therefore adultery in the book becomes an image of spiritual betrayal.

9. Gomer

Gomer daughter of Diblaim is mentioned as Hosea's wife, and her story is connected with the image of Israel, which went after other gods. Gomer's behavior becomes a visible symbol of the betrayal with which God accused his people, but what is striking is that the story does not stop at betrayal. God uses it to reveal that human sin is very painful to him; nevertheless, his love does not turn into indifference, but drives him to discipline and to the call to return.

10. The Names of Hosea's Children

The book uses the names of Hosea's children as prophetic messages, and they are Jezreel, Lo-ruhamah, and Lo-ammi. These names are not merely family names, but carry spiritual and historical judgments upon Israel. Here the skill of Hosea's prophetic writing appears, as the family itself becomes a prophetic tableau walking before the people.

11. Jezreel

The name Jezreel — יִזְרְעֶאל is connected to the Hebrew root זרע — z-r-ʿ which means sowing or seed, and can also be related to the idea that God sows. However, the name carries in the context of the book a historical connection to the city of Jezreel and to the violence that was connected to the house of Jehu, especially in the events of the blood of Jezreel. Therefore, the name becomes dual in meaning: a historical place connected with blood, and at the same time a term that can later carry the meaning of sowing and restoration.

12. Lo-Ruhamah

The name Lo-Ruhamah — לֹא רֻחָמָה means fundamentally 'not pitied' or 'no mercy'. The name represents a judicial declaration that Israel will face the consequences of its betrayal, and that the period of tolerance with sin has ended. But what is striking is that the book itself will reverse the meaning later, when the promise comes that God will have mercy on the one who was not shown mercy, and thus judgment is transformed into hope.

13. Lo-Ammi

The name Lo-Ammi — לֹא עַמִּי means 'not my people'. It is one of the harshest expressions in the book, because it reflects a reversal of covenant language, for the people whom God said were his people have come to live in a way that makes the relationship as though it had been broken. But even this name does not represent the final word, because God promises that those to whom it was said 'you are not my people' will be told 'you are children of the living God'.

14. Hosea 2 — The Marital Trial

The second chapter presents a dramatic picture of the relationship of the Lord with Israel, as if God enters into a trial with an unfaithful wife. It speaks of the sins of the people and of attributing the blessings to Baal instead of to the Lord, because Israel would take the gifts of God and then attribute them to pagan gods. And here the book reveals a very deep sin: that a person would enjoy the gifts of God and then attribute their source to someone other than God.

15. Baal

Baal was a Canaanite god associated with fertility, agriculture, and rain, and therefore Israel's reliance on Baal was connected to attempting to ensure crops and prosperity apart from the Lord. But Hosea declares that rain, grain, oil, and wine are not gifts of Baal, but gifts of God. Thus the prophet fights not merely a wooden idol, but the erroneous theological idea about the source of life and blessing.

16. 'Therefore, behold, I will hedge up your way with thorns'

Here appears the picture of divine discipline, as God sets barriers before Israel so that it will not continue on its path. This is a very important point in understanding Hosea: sometimes prevention, obstruction, and crisis are a divine means of stopping a person from the path of destruction. Discipline is not always a sign of God's absence, but can be evidence that he is still dealing with the person and wants to restore him.

17. 'Therefore, behold, I will allure her'

One of the most beautiful transformations in the book is that God, after speaking of judgment, says that he will allure her and bring her into the wilderness and speak to her heart. The wilderness, which in Israel's history was a place of testing, becomes here a place of courtship renewal. It is as if God says that the relationship that was corrupted in a land of abundance will be remade in a place where the person cannot rely on the abundance of blessings, but learns to depend on the Lord himself.

18. 'And I will betroth you to myself forever'

The text reaches a stunning promise:

'And I will betroth you to myself forever; and I will betroth you to myself in righteousness and in justice and in steadfast love and in mercy; I will betroth you to myself in faithfulness. And you shall know the Lord.'

This text is among the deepest texts about covenant, love, and faithfulness in the Old Testament. God does not merely restore Israel to its former state, but speaks of a new relationship based on justice, righteousness, steadfast love, mercy, and faithfulness. Therefore, salvation in Hosea is not merely the removal of punishment, but the rebuilding of the relationship.

19. Hosea 3 — The Purchase of Gomer

God commands Hosea to love a woman beloved of her master and an adulteress, so he purchases her for himself again. This act becomes a shocking picture of God's love for Israel despite its betrayal, but at the same time it places the relationship within a framework of discipline and waiting, because Gomer does not immediately return to a normal life unchanged. The message is that God's love does not mean ignoring sin, but means that judgment is not the final word in God's relationship with his people.

20. 'I will go and return to my place'

The Lord speaks of his withdrawal so that the people will feel their need for him. This picture reveals that God may allow a person to experience the emptiness of separation so that he will recognize the value of the relationship he rejected. And here appears a deep idea in Hosea: sometimes true return begins only when a person discovers that all the alternatives he trusted in cannot give him life.

21. 'Come, let us return to the Lord'

Among the most beautiful expressions in the book:

'Come, let us return to the Lord; for he has torn us, that he may heal us; he has struck us down, and he may bind us up.'

This is not a call to despair, but to return. The hand that disciplines is the same hand that heals, and the Lord who revealed the wound is the same one who can bind it up. Therefore Hosea does not regard repentance as flight from God, but as a return to God himself.

22. 'I desire mercy, not sacrifice'

Hosea says:

'For I desire steadfast love and not sacrifice, the knowledge of God rather than burnt offerings.'

And this verse is among the most important verses of the book, and Christ used it in the New Testament in Matthew 9:13 and 12:7. The meaning is not the abolition of worship or the sacrifices that God commanded, but the exposure of worship that has become external form without faithful heart. God desires a genuine relationship with him that shows itself in mercy, knowledge, and obedience, not merely religious rituals separated from life.

23. The Corruption of the Priests

Hosea attacks the priests strongly because they no longer led the people to the knowledge of God, but became part of the apostasy. And this is a dangerous point in the book, because the corruption of spiritual leadership leads to the spread of ignorance, paganism, and injustice among the people. Therefore Hosea says that his people are destroyed by lack of knowledge, that is, lack of the knowledge of God, the knowledge that produces obedient life.

24. 'My people are destroyed for lack of knowledge'

This verse does not speak of mere lack of information, but of covenant knowledge, relationship, and obedience. A person can know many religious words without knowing God in a way that changes his life, and can practice worship while his heart is far from him. Therefore Hosea places the knowledge of God at the center of true reform.

25. Ephraim

The name Ephraim is repeated often in the book, because Ephraim was the largest and strongest of the tribes of the northern kingdom, and therefore the name sometimes became used to refer to the entire northern kingdom. And Hosea reveals that Ephraim's political and economic power did not protect it from spiritual collapse. And here the paradox appears: the more external power increases without spiritual depth, the greater the danger of internal fall.

26. Political Alliances

Israel sometimes tried to rely on Assyria and Egypt instead of returning to the Lord. And Hosea describes this behavior in a way that resembles a foolish dove going back and forth between political powers. And the message is clear: when a person loses his trust in God, he begins to seek his salvation in changing powers, but these very powers cannot save him from the judgment caused by his separation from God.

27. Hosea 10 — The Empty Vineyard

The book says about Israel that he is 'a luxuriant vine that yields its fruit'. This is a very powerful image, because the people had received many gifts but used them for themselves rather than to bear fruit for God. And therefore the problem is not that Israel was not fruitful, but that the fruit was directed to self rather than to God.

28. 'Sow for yourselves righteousness'

Hosea calls the people to return saying:

'Sow for yourselves righteousness; reap steadfast love; break up your fallow ground, for it is the time to seek the Lord, that he may come and rain righteousness upon you.'

The agricultural image here corresponds to the nature of agricultural society in Israel, but it carries a deep spiritual principle: moral and spiritual life has fruit, and what a person sows has results. And at the same time the text opens the door of hope, because the land that the people neglected can be plowed again, and the heart that hardened can return to seeking the Lord.

29. Hosea 11 — A Father's Heart

The book reaches one of the deepest pictures of God's love:

'When Israel was a child, I loved him, and out of Egypt I called my son.'

Then God portrays himself as a father who taught his child to walk and carried him and fed him, but the child left him. And here the relationship between God and Israel becomes more than a relationship of king to people; it is a relationship of father to son, and therefore the pain of betrayal is greater.

30. 'How can I give you up, O Ephraim?'

The Lord says:

'How can I give you up, O Ephraim? How can I hand you over, O Israel? ... My heart recoils within me; my compassion grows warm and tender.'

These words reveal the deep tension between God's holiness and his judgment on the one hand, and his mercy and love on the other. The text does not mean that God is hesitant or unable to make a decision, but uses deep human language so that he may reveal to the person that his judgment does not stem from harshness or indifference, but from a heart that loves his people and hates their sin.

31. 'For I am God and not man'

The Lord says:

'I will not execute my fierce anger; I will not again destroy Ephraim; for I am God and not man, the Holy One in your midst.'

And here appears the difference between a person's reaction and God's reaction. A person may take revenge to the end, but God gathers together holiness that does not tolerate evil and mercy that opens the door to return. Therefore God's holiness is not the opposite of his love, but is what makes his love genuine and not superficial.

32. Hosea 12 — Jacob's History

Hosea goes back to Jacob's story, recovering events from the history of the ancestors to explain Israel's current condition. And he mentions Jacob's struggle with the angel and his tears and his plea for mercy, as if he calls Israel to return to the roots of its faith rather than continue deceiving itself. History here becomes a mirror to the present, because the people that came from Jacob need to learn from Jacob's own life.

33. Hosea 13 — Death and Judgment

In the thirteenth chapter, judgment reaches a severe level, because Israel made power, wealth, and pagan worship the source of its trust. And the Lord declares that the worship of Baal cannot give life, and the kingdom will face the consequences of its rebellion. But even in this context a later promise appears related to redemption from the dominion of death, which is what made some texts of Hosea of special importance in Messianic and apostolic reading.

34. Hosea 13:14

The text says:

'Shall I ransom them from the power of Sheol? Shall I redeem them from Death? O Death, where are your plagues? O Sheol, where is your sting? Compassion is hidden from my eyes.'

And this verse is of exceptional importance because it appears in the New Testament in 1 Corinthians 15:55 in the context of declaring the victory of the resurrection. It is important to note that Hosea's original context is connected to judgment and redemption, while Paul uses the text in proclaiming Christ's victory over death. Thus, the text becomes part of the grand biblical narrative that culminates in the defeat of death.

35. Hosea 14 — Return and Healing

The book closes with a clear call:

'Return, O Israel, to the Lord your God, for you have stumbled because of your iniquity.'

The book does not end with exile or destruction, but with a call to return. Then God promises that he will heal their faithlessness, will love them freely, and Israel will become like the lily, like the cedar, like the olive tree, like the vine, that is, the ending moves from a picture of adultery and dryness to life, stability, and fruit.

36. 'Who is wise that he may understand these things?'

Hosea closes with words:

'Who is wise, that he may understand these things? Who is discerning, that he may know them? For the ways of the Lord are right, and the upright walk in them, but transgressors stumble in them.'

And this is a very wise conclusion because it does not present the book as merely a historical story, but calls on the reader to take a stand. The same ways of God can be a path of life for the faithful and a path of stumbling for the rebellious, because the problem is not with the Lord's way but with the person's response to him.

37. The Most Important Figures in the Book
Hosea

The prophet who made God his married and family life a living sign of the relationship of the Lord with Israel.

Gomer

The wife of Hosea, and her story became a prophetic symbol of Israel's betrayal of the Lord and its going after other gods.

Beeri

The father of Hosea, the person to whom the prophet is attributed in the opening of the book.

Jeroboam the son of Joash

King of Israel during whose time Hosea served, and his reign was a period of political and economic prosperity for the northern kingdom despite spiritual apostasy.

Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah

Kings of Judah through whose reigns the book establishes the time of Hosea's service.

38. The Most Important Events in the Book

Hosea's call to marry Gomer; the birth of Jezreel, Lo-Ruhamah, and Lo-Ammi; the declaration of Israel's betrayal; the exposure of Baal worship; the call to return; the promise of an everlasting betrothal; the purchase of Gomer again; the condemnation of priests and leaders; the exposure of Israel's reliance on Assyria and Egypt; the comparison of Israel to a vine; the reminder to the people of Jacob's history; the proclamation of judgment on Ephraim; the proclamation of redemption from death; and then the final call to return, healing, and restoration.

39. The Most Important Message the Book Presents

God does not deal with his people's betrayal with indifference, nor does he allow sin to continue without judgment, but at the same time he does not abandon his people easily; he disciplines so that he may restore, wounds so that he may heal, exposes betrayal so that he may lead to repentance, and in the end declares that the relationship that was broken by human sin God is able to renew through his grace.

40. What does the Book of Hosea indicate to us?

First, the book indicates that sin is not merely a legal violation, but a betrayal of a relationship of love and covenant. Second, it indicates that a person can replace God with other sources of blessing, receiving God's gifts and then attributing them to his own strength, resources, or idols. Third, it indicates that divine discipline can be a path to restoration, because God disciplines not with the aim of revenge, but with the aim of bringing a person back. Finally, the book reveals that true knowledge of God must be manifested in mercy, faithfulness, and obedience, not in religious practices alone.

41. Hosea and Christ

The Book of Hosea contains very important messianic threads, especially in the text, “Out of Egypt I called My son,” which Matthew uses in Matthew 2:15 in speaking of Christ. The themes of the shepherd, the people, return, redemption, and victory over death are also all connected to the biblical line that leads to Christ. We should not regard every verse in Hosea as a direct prophecy about Christ, but the book provides a broad theological framework within which Christ's work is understood as the fulfillment of God's salvation, the renewal of His covenant, and His mercy.

42. Hosea and the Church

Among the most important themes that influenced the New Testament reading of Hosea is the use of the expressions “Not My People” and “sons of the living God” in Romans 9:25–26, as well as the references connected with mercy. This opens an important field in biblical theology for studying how the New Testament uses Hosea's texts to understand the calling of the Gentiles into the people of God. Here we see that God's mercy, which begins in the book with the restoration of Israel, expands in biblical revelation to reveal the riches of His grace in Christ.

43. Hosea and Mercy

The word mercy is very central in the book, not merely as an emotional feeling, but as the foundation of the covenant relationship. Therefore, the Lord's statement, “I desire mercy and not sacrifice,” reveals that true worship must produce a merciful and faithful heart. Thus, Hosea becomes a book of great importance in studying the relationship between worship and ethics.

44. Hosea and the Knowledge of God

Hosea does not present knowledge as merely knowing information about God, but as relational knowledge that leads to faithfulness. Therefore, a person may possess the temple, sacrifices, priests, and religious language, yet remain far from God if he does not know Him with a knowledge that changes his life. This message remains fundamental: sound theology is complete only when it is transformed into sound living.

45. The Major Movement of the Book of Hosea

The book can be summarized in this path:

Love.

Then:

Betrayal.

Then:

Warning.

Then:

Judgment.

Then:

The wilderness.

Then:

Return.

Then:

Betrothal anew.

Then:

Healing.

Then:

Fruit.

Therefore, the Book of Hosea begins with the image of an unfaithful woman and ends with the image of a flourishing people; it begins with the name “Not My People” and ends with a restored relationship; it begins with judgment and ends with a call to life.

🔑 The sentence that summarizes the Book of Hosea

“The Book of Hosea reveals that human sin is not merely the breaking of a commandment, but betrayal of a relationship; therefore, God appears in it as a husband wounded by the betrayal of his bride, as a father who suffers from the departure of his son, as the Holy One who cannot ignore evil, as a shepherd who desires to restore His people, and as a physician who heals their apostasy. While the book declares that Israel will reap the consequences of its betrayal, the voice of grace remains louder than the voice of judgment, until God reaches His promise: ‘I will betroth you to Myself forever.’ The book then ends with the call to return, healing, and fruit, declaring that the end God desires for His people is not destruction, but that they may know the LORD, abide in Him, and bear fruit for Him.”
