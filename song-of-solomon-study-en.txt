Introduction to the Song of Songs

The central idea of the book: Song of Songs is a poetic book celebrating pure marital love, covenant, mutual longing, and the beauty of the relationship between man and woman. At the direct literary level, the book presents a poetic dialogue between the lover and his beloved; yet, across the history of biblical interpretation, it has also carried a profound spiritual reading that sees in human love an image that can help us understand God’s love for His people and Christ’s relationship to His church. The strength of the book is that it does not shy away from speaking of beauty, emotion, and desire, but it places love within the framework of dignity, faithfulness, belonging, and covenant.

1. Name of the book and its meaning

The Arabic name is:

Song of Songs.

It is a Hebrew expression in the superlative form.

The text begins:

“Song of Songs, which is Solomon’s.”

And the Hebrew name:

שִׁיר הַשִּׁירִים — Shir ha-Shirim

That is, literally:

“Song of the Songs.”

And this form means:

The greatest song.

Or:

The most exalted of songs.

Just as some similar Hebrew forms mean the highest degree of something.

Therefore, the title can be understood as:

The supreme song, or the greatest of songs.

2. The Author

The book attributes itself to Solomon.

And his name appears at the beginning of the book:

“Song of Songs, which is Solomon’s.”

As Solomon’s name appears elsewhere in the book.

Therefore, ancient Jewish and Christian tradition attributed the book to Solomon.

But, as with the other wisdom books, modern scholarship raises questions concerning:

The date of the text’s composition.

The nature of its relationship to Solomon.

Was Solomon the direct author of the entire text, or was the book associated with his name within a literary tradition?

Therefore, we should distinguish between:

The traditional attribution to Solomon

and

Modern critical discussions concerning the date of the final composition.

3. Solomon and the Meaning of His Name

The Hebrew name:

שְׁלֹמֹה — Shelomoh

It is related to a root:

שָׁלוֹם — Shalom

That is:

Peace.

Well-being.

Wholeness.

The association between Solomon and “Song of Songs” is important literarily; for Solomon appears in biblical tradition as a figure associated with:

Wisdom.

Kingship.

Wealth.

The building of the temple.

And with love and marriage, even though Scripture itself later records the failure of his many wives and his spiritual apostasy.

This final detail is very important, for the book should not be used to justify all that Solomon did in his life.

4. Solomon’s year of birth and death

Scripture does not give a specific year for Solomon’s birth.

It is generally thought that his reign lasted approximately:

970–931 BC.

His death, however, came after roughly forty years of reign.

But again, Solomon’s date does not necessarily equal the date of the book’s final composition, which is an important point in scholarly study.

5. For whom was the book written?

The book is not a message directed to the people of Israel in the form of direct commandments.

Rather, it is a lyrical poetic song.

And it can be read on several levels:

Love between man and woman.

Marital love.

Joy in the relationship.

Faithfulness and belonging.

Beauty.

Longing.

Then, throughout Jewish and Christian history, a spiritual symbolic reading emerged that sees in the relationship between the lover and the beloved an image of the relationship between:

God and His people.

In Christian interpretation, the lover and the beloved are sometimes read in the light of:

Christ and the church.

But we must not erase the book’s original literal meaning in favor of symbolism.

6. Reason for writing the book

The purposes of the book can be understood on several levels.

First:

The celebration of marital love.

Second:

The display of the beauty of the relationship between man and woman.

Third:

The highlighting of mutual longing and belonging.

Fourth:

The affirmation of the value of the body and beauty within the marital framework.

Fifth:

Presenting love as a personal relationship rather than mere physical desire.

And sixth:

Giving the church and Judaism rich material for spiritual reflection on the love of God.

7. The Time of the Book's Writing

If we take the traditional attribution to Solomon, we place the origin of the book in:

The tenth century BC.

But modern studies discuss the dating of the text because of:

Language.

Vocabulary.

Poetic structure.

The historical development of Hebrew.

For this reason some researchers propose a later date.

There is no complete academic consensus on a single date.

Therefore it is best that the encyclopedia record:

Tradition attributes the book to Solomon in the tenth century BC, while a number of researchers hold that the final literary composition may be later.

8. Structure of the book

The book can be divided thematically into several poetic panels:

Song 1–2

Longing and the beginning of the relationship.

Song 3

The search for the beloved and the meeting.

Song 4

Praise of the beloved's beauty.

Song 5

Separation and the search for the beloved.

Song 6

Restoration of the meeting and mutual praise.

Song 7

The beauty of the beloved and longing.

Song 8

The power of love and the conclusion of the song.

This division is not a formal division within the text, but rather a helpful way to understand the development of the poem.

9. The Beloved (Male) and the Beloved (Female)

The two main characters are:

The beloved (male).

The beloved (female).

And the text moves between their two voices.

This is very important, because the woman in the book is not a silent character.

Rather she speaks.

And seeks.

And longs.

And describes.

And calls out.

And expresses her desire.

This makes the woman's presence in the Song of Songs strong and not marginal.

10. "I am my beloved's, and my beloved is mine"

Among the most important recurring ideas:

Mutual belonging.

Love here is not:

One party possessing the other.

But rather:

"I am my beloved's, and my beloved is mine."

This is one of the deepest images in the book.

The relationship is based on:

Knowledge.

Belonging.

Trust.

Longing.

Joy in the other.

11. The Body and Beauty

The Song of Songs is not ashamed to speak of:

The eyes.

The hair.

The teeth.

The lips.

The neck.

The body.

The perfume.

The gardens.

The fruits.

But this language should not be read as pornographic material.

It is ancient poetic language that celebrates the beauty of the beloved man and woman within the context of love and relationship.

This gives the book great importance in the Bible because it declares that the body is not an enemy of the spirit.

12. Love and the Body

In biblical thought, the body is part of creation.

The Bible does not begin with the idea that the body is evil.

Rather in the book of Genesis we read about the creation of man as male and female.

Then the Song of Songs comes to present the poetic side of:

Beauty.

Attraction.

Love.

Joy.

Belonging.

Thus the book can be read as a literary extension of the idea that marriage is a gift from God.

13. Love Is Not Merely Desire

This is a central point.

If the Song of Songs spoke only of physical desire, it would not have needed all this talk of:

Searching.

Separation.

Waiting.

Faithfulness.

Knowledge.

Praise.

Belonging.

For love in the book is broader than the body.

It is a relationship between one person and another.

14. “Do not arouse or awaken love”

Among the important recurring phrases:

“Do not arouse or awaken love until it pleases.”

This is an extremely important phrase for understanding sexual ethics in the book.

Love is not something to be aroused without limits.

Rather, it has:

A time.

A context.

Boundaries.

Maturity.

This makes the book not only a celebration of love, but also an implicit teaching about respecting love’s timing and boundaries.

15. The Garden

The following images appear:

The garden.

The orchard.

Springs.

Perfumes.

The fruits.

The garden powerfully reminds the reader of:

The Garden of Eden.

In the Book of Genesis, we find the man and the woman in a garden.

In the Song of Songs, images of the garden, fertility, beauty, and love appear.

Therefore, there is a beautiful literary relationship between:

Creation.

The garden.

The marriage.

Love.

16. The Beloved as a Shepherd

In some passages, the beloved appears associated with flocks and pastures.

These agricultural and pastoral images reflect the ancient environment of Israel.

Love is not found only in an isolated palace.

Rather, it moves among:

The mountains.

Fields.

Vineyards.

The gardens.

Flocks.

This connects the book with the ancient Palestinian landscape.

17. The Vine

Vineyards appear repeatedly.

The vine has a long symbolic history in the Bible.

It is associated with:

Fertility.

The land.

The people.

Joy.

Fruit.

In the Song of Songs, the vine becomes part of the language of love and fertility.

18. The Seal

Among the greatest statements in the book:

“Set me as a seal upon your heart, as a seal upon your arm, for love is strong as death.”

This statement brings the book to a theological and literary summit.

True love desires:

Stability.

Belonging.

Continuity.

Closeness.

The comparison with “death” reveals the power of love.

19. “Love Is Strong as Death”

This statement is among the most famous texts in the book.

Death is one of the most powerful forces known to humanity.

The author says that true love possesses tremendous power.

However, in a Christian reading, we should not say that human love is stronger than the death of Christ.

Rather, we can see in the statement a poetic image of love’s power and permanence, then read it in the light of the Gospel’s proclamation of the divine love revealed in Christ.

20. “Many Waters Cannot Quench Love”

The poem reaches:

“Many waters cannot quench love, neither can floods drown it.”

Here love becomes like fire.

Water attempts to extinguish it.

But it is not extinguished.

It is an image of:

Stability.

Faithfulness.

Depth.

The strength of attachment.

21. The Major Events in the Book

The book is not a historical book; therefore, it does not contain events in the sense found in the Book of Joshua or Samuel.

Yet a poetic movement can be traced:

The beloved woman longs for the beloved man.

The beloved man praises the beloved woman.

The beloved woman searches for him.

A separation occurs.

A search occurs.

A meeting occurs.

Praise is renewed.

Belonging deepens.

The book reaches a declaration of the power of love.

22. Jerusalem and the Daughters of Zion

The phrase recurs:

“O daughters of Jerusalem.”

This phrase serves an important literary role.

It establishes the emotional relationship within a community and people and city.

This is important because biblical love is not always a secret relationship separated from the community.

Rather, marriage and love are connected to the social and family structure of the Old Testament.

23. Jerusalem in the Song

Jerusalem appears in the background.

The city is not merely a geographic location.

It is the city of the King.

and the city of David.

and the city of the temple.

In biblical literature it is connected to Israelite identity.

Therefore the presence of love in the context of Jerusalem becomes part of the cultural and political picture of the book.

24. Is the Song a marriage story?

There is disagreement among interpreters about the precise structure.

Some see it as:

a marital love poem.

Some ancient readings saw it as connected to:

Solomon's marriage.

But the text itself does not present a complete historical narrative in the narrative sense.

Therefore it is better to study it as:

a collection of interconnected love poems in a single literary work.

25. Are Solomon and a particular woman intended?

There are multiple readings.

Some traditions linked it to:

Solomon and the beloved woman.

But the book does not provide us a clear historical biography of them.

Therefore we should not construct historical details not present in the text.

What is most important is the poetic message itself:

love and longing and faithfulness and beauty.

26. The Jewish Reading

The Jewish tradition read the Song of Songs with a strong allegorical reading.

For the beloved and the beloved woman can symbolize:

God and Israel.

In this way the love between the two becomes a picture of the covenant.

This connects the book with a series of prophetic texts that used marriage as a language for God's relationship with his people.

27. The Relationship with the Prophets

In the Prophets we find the comparison of God's relationship with Israel to the relationship of:

the bridegroom and the bride.

We see this particularly in:

Hosea.

Jeremiah.

Ezekiel.

Isaiah.

This provides important background for the allegorical reading of the Song of Songs.

28. The Christian Reading

The New Testament uses the image of:

the bridegroom and the bride

in speaking of:

Christ and the church.

This makes the Song of Songs especially valuable in Christian tradition.

But the reading must be balanced:

The direct literary meaning speaks of love between a man and a woman.

Then this love can be read as a picture helping us understand the covenantal relationship and spiritual love.

29. Christ and the Bridegroom

In the New Testament John the Baptist declares:

He who has the bride is the bridegroom.

Christ himself used the image of:

the bridegroom.

In Ephesians 5 the marriage relationship becomes a teaching picture of the relationship of:

Christ and the church.

Therefore the language of the Song of Songs can find a deep Christian extension in the concept of:

Christ the Bridegroom.

30. The Most Important Message the Book Presents

Love is a mighty gift from God, and true love combines beauty and longing and faithfulness and belonging, but it needs the right timing and proper boundaries, and when understood within the framework of the covenant it becomes a powerful picture that can help us understand the faithfulness of divine love.

31. What Does the Book Point to for Us?

It indicates that:

Love is not a fault.

The body is not evil in itself.

Marriage is not merely a social obligation.

Beauty can be a subject of joy.

Longing is part of the human relationship.

Faithfulness is more important than mere attraction.

Love needs boundaries.

A healthy relationship is based on mutual belonging.

True love is not consumption of the other.

God is the source of the order that makes love a gift, not slavery.

32. The Relationship Between the Book of Proverbs and the Song of Songs

There is a very important literary relationship between the two books.

In Proverbs we find a warning against:

The strange woman.

And in the Song of Songs we find:

pure love and faithfulness.

Proverbs warns against love separated from wisdom.

And the Song of Songs celebrates love when it becomes:

a deep relationship and belonging and faithfulness.

In this way the two books can be placed side by side to understand sexual and emotional wisdom in Scripture.

33. The relationship between the Song of Songs and Ecclesiastes

The relationship is also beautiful.

Ecclesiastes asks:

What remains in a world that dies?

And the Song of Songs says:

Love has a deep power that cannot be reduced to things.

Ecclesiastes confronts:

Death.

And the Song of Songs says:

"Love is as strong as death."

As if the two books present two different sides of human experience:

Ecclesiastes searches for the meaning of existence.

And the Song celebrates the gift of love within existence.

34. The main characters
The Beloved (male)

Represents the loving man who expresses the beauty of the beloved (female) and his longing for her.

The Beloved (female)

A character with a strong voice and presence, expressing her desire, her search, and her belonging.

The daughters of Jerusalem

A group that appears in the poetic background and participates in some of the dialogues and scenes.

Solomon

He appears in the titles and scenes of the book, but he should not be made the historical hero of every scene without textual evidence.

35. Key words in the book

Love.

The beloved (male).

The beloved (female).

Beauty.

Longing.

The seal.

The garden.

The vineyard.

The perfume.

The meeting.

Searching.

Faithfulness.

Belonging.

These words reveal the emotional structure of the book.
