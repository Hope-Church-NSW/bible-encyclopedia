Introduction to the Gospel of Mark
1. Name of the book and its meaning

The name “Mark” is associated with the Latin name Marcus, a Roman name that was common in the ancient world. In the New Testament, a person appears who bears the name “John who was called Mark.” He is associated with the house of Mary, the mother of John, in Jerusalem; he later appeared in ministry with Barnabas and Paul and afterward became especially associated with Peter, so that Peter mentions him in his letter as “Mark, my son.” Early Christian tradition preserved the name Mark as the author of the second Gospel and connected his writing especially with Peter's testimony. This tradition is supported by early ecclesiastical sources, although the author's name does not explicitly appear within the text of the Gospel itself.

2. The Author and His Character

Ancient Christian tradition attributes the Gospel of Mark to John Mark, the person who appears in the Book of Acts and the New Testament epistles. Mark was associated with Barnabas and Paul at the beginning of ministry, then became clearly associated with Peter. At the end of his ministry, Paul refers to Mark's usefulness in ministry after an earlier period of disagreement concerning his accompanying them. Peter mentions Mark affectionately as “Mark, my son,” which led ancient tradition to associate him especially with Peter's testimony. The testimony of Papias, preserved by Eusebius, states that Mark was Peter's interpreter or translator and that he faithfully recorded what he remembered of the sayings and deeds of Christ, even if the order of his narrative was not strictly chronological.

It is important to distinguish between tradition and internal evidence: the Gospel itself does not name its author, and therefore its attribution to John Mark depends to a great extent on early Christian testimony. Nevertheless, this testimony is sufficiently ancient and early to give the attribution of the Gospel to Mark significant historical weight, even as academic discussion continues concerning the extent of Peter's direct relationship to the material contained in the Gospel.

3. Mark's Birth and Death

The Bible provides no information concerning the year of Mark's birth or the year of his death; therefore, no precise date can be determined for these two events. What can be known from the New Testament is that his name was John, that he was the son of a woman named Mary, that her house in Jerusalem was a place where believers gathered, that he accompanied Barnabas and Paul during a stage of ministry, and that he was later associated with Peter and with Paul's ministry. The multiple references to him reveal that his life was not marginal in the history of the early church; rather, he was part of the network of apostolic ministry that extended between Jerusalem and other regions of the early Christian world.

As for later accounts concerning the end of his life and his ministry in particular places, their details cannot be presented as certain biblical facts. They should therefore remain within the framework of ecclesiastical tradition rather than that of established biblical history.

4. For Whom Was the Gospel Written?

The Gospel of Mark shows a clear concern for a Gentile audience, or at least for an audience that needs explanations of certain Jewish customs and terms. When Mark uses Aramaic words such as “Talitha cumi,” “Ephphatha,” and “Eloi, Eloi, lama sabachthani,” he provides the reader with an explanation of their meanings. He also explains certain Jewish customs relating to purity and the washing of hands, as well as some geographical and cultural expressions. This style makes it likely that Mark's audience included a large number of Gentiles and may have been associated with a Roman setting. This is consistent with the ancient tradition that connected the writing of the Gospel with the city of Rome, although identifying the place of writing does not reach the level of historical certainty.

Mark's audience should not be reduced categorically to a single community, because the Gospel spread among the early churches and its readers likely included Jews, Gentiles, and different social groups. Yet it is clear that Mark writes in a way that makes the message of Christ understandable to the reader who does not live directly within the Jewish setting.

5. Reasons for Writing the Gospel

The primary purpose of the Gospel of Mark is to proclaim Jesus Christ, the Son of God, through a vivid and rapid account of His life, ministry, suffering, and resurrection. Mark does not begin with the birth narrative or genealogy; instead, he begins directly with the ministry of John the Baptist and then the appearance of Christ, as though the reader is driven from the first sentence to confront the central question: Who is Jesus? The miracles, teaching, casting out of demons, healing, and authority over nature follow as successive testimonies to Christ's authority and identity.

At the same time, Mark does not allow the reader to understand Christ through miracles alone, because the path gradually moves toward the cross. The Christ who appears as the one possessing authority is Himself the Son of Man who “must suffer many things” and who came “to serve and to give Himself as a ransom for many.” Thus, Mark brings together authority, service, the cross, and the resurrection in one story.

6. Date of Writing of the Gospel

The Gospel of Mark does not specify an explicit date for its composition; consequently, scholars differ in determining the precise year. The broad academic view places the writing of the Gospel in the sixties or near 70 A.D., while some studies connect the text to the events of the Jewish-Roman War and the destruction of the temple in 70 A.D. The discussion depends especially on the interpretation of chapter thirteen, where Christ speaks about the destruction of the temple, persecution, tribulations, and the coming of the Son of Man.

The chronological question remains debated; therefore, it is not appropriate to present a single year as an established fact. However, placing Mark in the second half of the first century, especially in the sixties or around 70 A.D., accords with a substantial body of modern scholarship. The tradition that connects the Gospel with Rome and with a period of persecution of believers also provides an important background for understanding Mark's concern with suffering, perseverance, and discipleship.

7. The Main Theme of the Gospel

The central theme of the Gospel of Mark is Jesus Christ, the Son of God, who came to proclaim the kingdom of God, serve humanity, and give Himself as a ransom for many. Mark presents Christ as possessing authority over disease, demons, nature, and death, yet he also presents this authority within the framework of service, humility, and obedience unto death.

Here one of the Gospel's most important paradoxes appears: Christ is declared to be the Son of God not only in moments of power and miracles, but with exceptional clarity at the cross, when the centurion says after His death, “Truly this man was the Son of God.” Thus, the cross becomes an essential part of the revelation of Christ's identity, rather than merely a tragic end to His life.

8. The Gospel of the Kingdom

Christ begins His ministry in Mark by declaring that “the time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand.” Therefore, the kingdom provides an essential framework for understanding His ministry. Yet Mark does not present the kingdom as an abstract idea; rather, he makes it present in Christ's deeds, teaching, and authority over the powers of evil, disease, nature, and death. The kingdom is at hand because the King Himself has come, and God's authority is manifested concretely in the ministry of Jesus.

The parables of the fourth chapter explain the nature of this kingdom, for they show that the work of God may begin in a way that appears small and unnoticed, yet it grows powerfully until it reaches the goal God desires. Thus Mark brings together the hidden beginning of the kingdom and its final power, and the word of God that is sown and the authority of God that accomplishes His purpose.

9. The Declaration of Christ, the Son of God

Mark opens his gospel with the phrase, 'The beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God,' and therefore the title 'Son of God' is not a marginal addition, but the key to understanding the whole narrative. The voice of the Father at the baptism declares that Jesus is the beloved Son, and this declaration appears again at the Transfiguration, then the paradox reaches its climax at the cross when the centurion bears witness to Him.

And between these declarations the entire story moves around the question of Jesus' identity, for the demons know something of His identity, the disciples grasp part of it, the crowds question Him, the authorities reject Him, yet the full meaning of His identity does not become clear until we place the Son of God before the suffering, crucified, and risen Christ.

10. John the Baptist and the Beginning of the Way

Mark begins with the ministry of John the Baptist, showing him in the wilderness calling for repentance and the baptism of repentance for the forgiveness of sins, fulfilling the prophecy that speaks of preparing the way before the Lord. Mark does not present John's childhood or Jesus' childhood, but shortens the distance between prophecy and the appearance of Christ in order to place the reader directly before the beginning of the gospel.

John thus becomes the connecting link between the expectation of the Old Covenant and the appearing of Christ. He is not the Christ, but he prepares the way for Him, proclaiming that the One who comes after Him is greater than he, and that He will baptize with the Holy Spirit.

11. The Baptism of Christ

When Jesus is baptized in the Jordan, the heavens open and the Spirit descends upon Him like a dove, and the voice from heaven says, 'You are My beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased.' Mark presents here a clear declaration of the Father's relationship with the Son and the Spirit at the beginning of Christ's public ministry.

The baptism does not represent merely an introductory rite for the ministry, but a moment of divine declaration regarding Jesus' identity before He enters the conflict with Satan and before His ministry begins in Galilee. The One who begins the ministry is the Son whom the Father bears witness to and upon whom the Spirit rests.

12. Christ's Temptation in the Wilderness

After the baptism, the Spirit leads Christ into the wilderness to be tempted by the devil, but Mark narrates the account in a highly compressed manner compared with Matthew and Luke. This brevity confirms the rapid nature of the gospel, for it moves directly from baptism to temptation and then to the proclamation of the kingdom.

The temptation reveals that the path of Christ toward the kingdom does not pass through the use of power independently of the Father's will, but begins with obedience. Christ enters the conflict from the beginning, yet He does not retreat before Satan, and thus the gospel begins by showing that the authority of Jesus is bound to His complete obedience to God.

13. The Calling of the Disciples

Jesus passes along the shore of the Sea of Galilee and calls Simon and Andrew, James and John, and the call comes directly: 'Follow Me, and I will make you fishers of people.' The men immediately respond, leaving their nets and their work to follow Him.

This call reveals the nature of discipleship in Mark: the disciple is not merely a person who receives information from Christ, but a person who leaves his old way and enters the way of Christ. The disciples will later become witnesses to the ministry, but Mark will also reveal their weakness of understanding and slowness of perception, showing that discipleship itself needs continual formation under the leadership of Christ.

14. The Authority of Christ

From the beginning of His ministry, Jesus appears in Mark as One with unique authority, for He teaches in a way that astonishes people, casts out demons, heals the sick, forgives sins, and commands nature so that it obeys Him. This authority is especially evident in chapter one when an unclean spirit cries out and Christ commands it to leave, and the people are astonished, saying, 'This is teaching with authority.'

Thus Mark links the word of Christ with His authority; He does not merely present religious ideas, but speaks and the powers of the spiritual and natural world respond to Him. Yet the authority possessed by Christ does not become a display, but is always directed toward the service of humanity and the proclamation of the kingdom of God.

15. The Healing of the Paralytic and the Forgiveness of Sins

When men bring the paralytic to Jesus, He does not begin by saying that He will heal his body, but says to him, 'Son, your sins are forgiven.' The scribes object because the forgiveness of sins belongs to God alone, and Jesus uses the physical miracle to declare that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins.

This event gains deep theological significance because it reveals that the authority of Christ is not limited to the visible realm. He does not merely treat a person from the outside, but deals with the problem of sin that separates humanity from God; thus the miracle becomes a sign of the identity and authority of Christ.

16. The Calling of Levi

Jesus calls Levi son of Alphaeus while he is sitting at the tax booth, and he rises and follows Him. Then Jesus enters Levi's house and sits with tax collectors and sinners, and the scribes and Pharisees object, so Jesus answers them that the healthy do not need a physician, but the sick do, and that He did not come to call the righteous, but sinners.

The story of Levi reveals the heart of Christ's message: the kingdom does not open its doors only to those with an acceptable religious record, but reaches the person who needs grace and repentance. It appears here that the ministry of Christ is not merely teaching the righteous, but seeking the sinners and restoring those whom society regards as outside the circle of acceptance.

17. The Sabbath and Christ

Confrontations over the Sabbath recur in Mark, and Christ declares, 'The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath,' and then He says, 'The Son of Man is also Lord of the Sabbath.' Here Christ does not abolish the purpose of the law, but reveals that the law cannot be used against the person whom God has come to do good.

Christ appears as One who possesses authority over the interpretation and purpose of the Sabbath; therefore the confrontation with the religious leaders is deeper than a dispute over custom. It is a confrontation over the authority of Christ to interpret the will of God and to declare the purpose of the law.

18. Parables of the Kingdom

In chapter four, Mark presents a group of parables of the kingdom, beginning with the parable of the sower. These parables reveal that the word of God is cast upon different kinds of hearts, and that the response to the word determines its fruitfulness in a person's life.

Then come the images of the lamp, the growing seed, and the mustard seed, proclaiming that the kingdom of God works in a way that may not be fully understood at first. The work may appear small or hidden, but God is the One who gives the growth; therefore the success of the kingdom should not be measured by human appearances alone.

19. The Authority of Christ over Nature

When a storm rises on the sea and the disciples seem helpless before it, Jesus is asleep in the boat, then He wakes up and rebukes the wind and the sea, and they become calm. After that the disciples say, 'Who is this? Even the wind and the sea obey Him.'

This question represents one of the great questions in Mark. The miracle is not only rescue from danger for the disciples, but a gradual revelation of the identity of Christ, because authority such as this over the sea and nature carries within it the echo of the authority of God Himself in the Old Testament.

20. The Authority of Christ over Demons

In the region of the Gerasenes, Christ confronts the man possessed by an unclean spirit, and the text reveals that his power was immense and that he had been isolated from society. But Jesus commands the evil spirits to come out, and the man's life is freed; he sits there, clothed and in his right mind.

This event reveals that the kingdom of God enters directly into confrontation with the kingdom of darkness. Christ does not negotiate with evil or submit to it, but exercises authority over it, showing that the liberation of humanity from spiritual bondage is an essential part of the message of the kingdom.

21. The Raising of Jairus's Daughter and the Healing of the Woman with the Issue of Blood

In Mark, the story of the woman with the issue of blood overlaps with the story of Jairus's daughter, and in both cases Christ faces a human situation that appears hopeless. The woman had suffered for twelve years, and the girl was dying while Jesus was on His way to her house, yet Christ declares His authority over both disease and death.

When He reaches the girl, He says, 'Talitha, get up.' She immediately rises. The scene becomes a declaration that Christ possesses authority not only over sickness, but over death itself, which prepares the way for the victory of the resurrection.

22. The Feeding of the Five Thousand

When Jesus sees the large crowd, He has compassion on them because they were like sheep without a shepherd, and He begins to teach them at length, then feeds the five thousand with five loaves and two fish. Here Christ appears as the shepherd who does not see humanity's spiritual need apart from its physical need.

The scene is linked to the image of the shepherd in the Old Testament and to the story of manna in the wilderness, but here Christ is the One who takes the bread and gives it to the disciples to distribute to the crowd. Thus the miracle becomes a declaration about the person of Christ and the nature of His ministry, which combines teaching, care, and satisfaction.

23. Walking on the Water

After feeding the crowd, Jesus goes up to the mountain to pray, while the disciples find themselves in the middle of the sea under pressure from the wind. Then Jesus comes walking on the water and appears to them, and they are amazed because they had not understood the meaning of the miracle of the loaves.

The scene reveals that the disciples saw the works of Christ, but they did not fully grasp His identity. This is a recurring point in Mark, because the problem is not a lack of evidence, but the slowness of the heart to understand the meaning of the evidence.

24. Peter and the Confession of Christ

In Caesarea Philippi, Jesus asks His disciples, 'Who do people say that I am?' Then He asks them, 'But who do you say that I am?' Peter answers, 'You are the Christ,' and this confession becomes a decisive turning point in the gospel.

But Mark places immediately after the confession Jesus' announcement of His suffering, death, and resurrection. This means that the title 'Christ' cannot be understood in Mark in a merely political or national sense, but must be understood through the cross; for the Christ is the King who goes to death willingly in order to fulfill the purpose of God.

25. The Transfiguration

Jesus takes Peter, James, and John up a high mountain, and His appearance is transformed before them; Moses and Elijah appear, and then a voice from the cloud says, 'This is My beloved Son. Listen to Him.' The scene reveals the glory of Christ in an extraordinary way before three of His disciples.

The importance of Moses and Elijah is evident in the connection of the scene with the Law and the Prophets, but the heavenly voice does not direct the disciples to them, but to the Son. Thus the Transfiguration declares that Christ is the center of the proclamation to which the disciples must listen, and that the glory revealed on the mountain is linked to the path that will lead Him to the cross.

26. The Way to the Cross

After Peter's confession and the Transfiguration, Mark begins to intensify Jesus' teaching about suffering and discipleship. Jesus declares that the Son of Man must suffer, be rejected, be killed, and then rise, and He calls His disciples to deny themselves, take up the cross, and follow Him.

Here the concept of human glory is overturned; the true path with Christ is not a path of power and comfort, but a path of service and self-giving. Thus the cross becomes not only the path of Christ, but the model that defines the meaning of Christian discipleship.

27. Christ and Service

The teaching of Christ about service reaches its climax when He says that the Son of Man 'did not come to be served, but to serve, and to give His life as a ransom for many.' This statement is a fundamental key to understanding the whole Gospel of Mark.

All the authority seen in the miracles is ultimately directed toward self-giving, and all the power Jesus demonstrated does not end in subjugating others, but in giving Himself for them. Here the greatness of the kingdom of God is revealed: the true King reigns through service and achieves victory through self-sacrifice.

28. The Entry into Jerusalem

Jesus enters Jerusalem riding on a donkey, and the crowd shouts, 'Blessed is He who comes in the name of the Lord,' so the scene appears as a royal entry. But Mark presents the same paradox that will lead to the cross; the King welcomed by the crowds will face rejection, trial, and death within a few days.

Jesus goes directly to the temple, making His entry into Jerusalem connected with the work of judgment and cleansing. Thus the King enters His city, but His first confrontation is not with the Roman army, but with the corruption of worship within the house of God.

29. The Cleansing of the Temple

Jesus enters the temple and drives out those who were buying and selling, declaring that the house of God must be a house of prayer for all nations, but they had made it 'a den of thieves.' Here Christ appears with the authority of the Lord over the temple, and declares that worship which has lost its justice and mercy does not fulfill the purpose of God.

The mention of 'all nations' gains special importance in Mark, because the message of the temple is not closed to a particular national community, but is tied to the presence of God and His call to the world. From this point, the cleansing of the temple becomes a sign of the approach of judgment and the necessity of returning to true worship of God.

30. Confrontation with the Religious Leaders

The confrontation between Christ and the chief priests, scribes, Pharisees, and Sadducees intensifies, and He is questioned about His authority, about tribute, about the resurrection, and about the greatest commandment. In each confrontation, Christ reveals the depth of His understanding of the law and the inadequacy of His opponents in grasping the purpose of God.

The confrontation reaches its climax when the question shifts from the validity of His teaching to the issue of disposing of the person Himself. Thus it becomes clear that the rejection of Christ was not due to obscure teaching, but because His authority and His word exposed the corruption of a religious system that had separated itself from the purpose of God.

31. The Woman Who Anointed Him

Before the cross, a woman comes to Bethany and pours an expensive perfume on the head of Jesus; some of those present are angry because they regard it as waste, but Jesus declares that she has done a good deed to Him and that she has anointed His body in advance for burial.

Her story becomes part of the memory of the gospel itself, for Jesus says that what she has done will be proclaimed wherever the gospel is preached. In the midst of the approach of the cross, it becomes clear that true love recognizes the value of Christ even when others do not understand what is happening.

32. The Last Supper and the Covenant

At the Last Supper, Jesus takes the bread, blesses it, breaks it, and gives it to the disciples; then He takes the cup and declares that it is His blood of the new covenant, which is poured out for many. Here Christ interprets His death before it occurs, so that the cross is not understood as a political accident or an unforeseen tragedy.

Christ links His blood with the covenant, and thus His death enters the heart of the biblical story of salvation. The cross is the way of establishing the new covenant, and the forgiveness of sins is bound to the self-offering of Christ.

33. Gethsemane and the Arrest of Christ

In Gethsemane, Christ begins to bow beneath the weight of the coming hour, and He says to the Father, 'Abba, Father, all things are possible for You; take this cup away from Me. Yet not what I will, but what You will.' Here the genuine humanity of Christ is revealed, as He faces suffering without turning away from the Father's will.

Then Judas comes with the crowd, and they seize Him, the disciples flee, and the company is scattered. Mark presents the scene with striking humanity, highlighting the fear and weakness of the disciples while Christ remains steadfast on the path He has chosen.

34. The Trial

Christ is led to the chief priest, and the authorities seek testimony against Him, but their testimonies do not agree. When He is asked about His identity, Jesus answers in a way that links Himself to the Son of Man seated at the right hand of power and coming with the clouds of heaven.

The declaration of Christ about Himself becomes the basis of the accusation, and He is condemned to death. Here the paradox reaches its climax: the Christ who possessed authority over demons, disease, and nature is now handed over to the authority of human beings, but He does so willingly within the obedience of the Father.

35. Peter and the Denial of Christ

Peter follows Christ from afar, then denies Him three times, just as Christ had previously announced. Mark presents this account without attempting to embellish Peter's image, because the Gospel does not conceal the weakness of the disciples, even when they are leaders in the early Church.

However, Peter's fall does not represent the end, because Mark's narrative will lead him to the announcement of the Resurrection and the call to meet the disciples. Thus, the grace of Christ is revealed—a grace that does not stop at the failure of the disciple, but restores him to the path of ministry.

36. The Cross

Christ is led to Golgotha and crucified between two robbers, and above the Cross an inscription is placed: 'The King of the Jews.' And the central paradox of the Gospel appears: Christ is the King, but His throne is the Cross, His crown is thorns, His wreath is mockery, and His kingdom is proclaimed in His death.

And when Christ dies, the veil of the temple is torn from top to bottom, and the centurion says: 'Truly this man was the Son of God.' Thus the Cross becomes the place where the mystery of Christ's identity is revealed in a way that cannot be ignored.

37. The Death of Christ

Christ cries out with a loud voice and yields up the spirit, and the veil of the temple is torn. Mark does not present the death as the end of the story, but as the moment that opens the way for the proclamation of the Resurrection.

The tearing of the veil carries deep theological significance, as it points to a major shift in humanity's relationship with God's presence and in the meaning of access to the sanctuary. With the centurion's confession that Jesus is the Son of God, the proclamation with which the Gospel began reaches a climax at the Cross.

38. The Resurrection

On the first day of the week, Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James, and Salome come to the tomb, and they find the stone rolled away and find a young man telling them that Jesus of Nazareth, who was crucified, has risen and is not here. Thus the Resurrection confirms that death was not the final word in the story of Christ.

The announcement sends the women back to Galilee, where Christ had begun His ministry and where He had called His disciples. Thus the Resurrection turns into a new beginning, because the path that seemed to have ended at the Cross is opened anew by the message of the risen Christ to His disciples.

39. The Most Important Characters in the Gospel of Mark

Jesus Christ is the central figure, the Messiah, the Son of God, the Son of Man, the Servant, and the Savior, and all events revolve around His identity, His authority, and His path to the Cross. John the Baptist stands out as the forerunner who prepares the way, and Peter appears prominently among the disciples as the one who confessed Christ and then as the disciple who denied Him, while James, John, and the rest of the Twelve appear as witnesses to Christ's works and students learning from Him despite their weak understanding. Judas Iscariot appears as the disciple who betrayed Christ, while the religious leaders represent the main party opposing His authority, Pilate appears as the governor who handed Christ over to be crucified, and the centurion appears at the end of the road with his striking confession of Christ's identity.

40. The Most Important Message Presented by the Gospel of Mark

The Gospel of Mark proclaims that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that the kingdom of God has drawn near in His person and work. Christ appears possessing authority over sickness, demons, nature, and death, but He does not use this authority to establish human glory; rather, He directs it toward serving, liberating, and restoring humanity. Mark reveals that Christ cannot be understood through miracles alone, because the true path to understanding Him passes through acknowledging that He is the suffering Messiah who must be rejected, killed, and then rise. Therefore, true discipleship does not mean seeking personal glory, but self-denial, bearing the cross, and following Christ, because the Son of Man Himself came to serve and to give His life as a ransom for many. In the end, the Resurrection proclaims that Christ, who entered the path of death, was not defeated, but triumphed over death, and that the Gospel which began with the proclamation 'Jesus Christ, the Son of God' reaches its climax at the Cross and the empty tomb.

41. The Grand Movement of the Gospel of Mark

The grand movement in the Gospel of Mark begins with the proclamation of 'the beginning of the Gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God,' then John the Baptist appears to prepare the way, and Christ comes to the Jordan, where the Father testifies of Him and the Spirit descends upon Him. Then He enters the wilderness victorious over temptation and begins proclaiming that the kingdom of God is at hand. From there, the Gospel moves rapidly from Galilee to the cities and villages, as Christ teaches with authority, casts out demons, heals the sick, forgives sins, calms the sea, and raises the dead, while questions about His identity grow, alongside the disciples' misunderstanding and the resistance of the religious leaders. The narrative then reaches Caesarea Philippi, where Peter confesses that Jesus is the Christ, but Christ announces that the path of the Messiah passes through suffering, rejection, death, and resurrection, and from here a clear focus begins on the Cross, discipleship, and service. After the Transfiguration, Christ sets out toward Jerusalem, entering the city as a gentle King, cleansing the temple, confronting the religious leaders, and proclaiming judgment. Then come the Last Supper, Gethsemane, the arrest, the trial, the denial, and the crucifixion. At Golgotha, the proclamation reaches its climax, for Christ, whom the Gospel proclaimed as the Son of God, dies on the Cross, the centurion confesses His identity, and the veil of the temple is torn. After that comes the first day of the week, when the women find the tomb empty and it is proclaimed to them that Jesus of Nazareth, who was crucified, has risen. Thus the story of the suffering Servant is transformed into a proclamation of the risen Christ, and the Cross, which seemed a defeat, becomes the way through which the victory of the Son of God was revealed.

42. The Most Important Connections Between Mark and the Old Testament

Mark builds his Gospel on a strong Old Testament background, despite his conciseness and his lesser use of the fulfillment formula compared to Matthew. He begins by connecting the appearance of John the Baptist with the texts of Isaiah and Malachi, and then echoes of Deuteronomy, the Psalms, and the Prophets appear in Christ's temptation and ministry. Furthermore, Christ's authority over the sea, demons, sickness, and death opens before the reader the Old Testament background that proclaims God's authority over creation and the powers of evil. Christ also appears in the image of the Shepherd who cares for the crowds, and as the Son and the suffering Servant, while the events of the Cross are linked to the Psalms and the texts of the covenant and sacrifice. Thus Mark, despite his brevity and rapid style, does not present a story detached from the Old Testament, but presents Jesus as the meeting point of God's ancient promises with the work of salvation that reaches its climax in the Cross and Resurrection.

43. The Relationship Between Mark and Isaiah

Isaiah occupies an important place at the beginning of the Gospel of Mark, because the voice of John in the wilderness is linked to preparing the way before the Lord. This connection leads to reading Christ's ministry as a fulfillment of the work of God, who promised to come to His people and lead them to salvation.

Additionally, the image of the suffering Servant in Isaiah helps in understanding the path that Christ walks in Mark, because Christ does not appear as a triumphant king in the political sense, but as a servant who gives Himself for others. Therefore, reading Mark in light of Isaiah reveals that service and the Cross are not a departure from Christ's identity, but are part of His identity and mission.

44. The Relationship Between Mark and Psalms

The Psalms appear in the background of Mark especially in the events of the Cross, notably Christ's cry: 'My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?', which is the beginning of the Twenty-Second Psalm. The use of this phrase reveals that Christ's sufferings are not understood merely as psychological or physical pain, but enter into the language of prayer and hope known to the people of the Old Testament.

Moreover, the image of the suffering, rejected, and then victorious King helps in understanding the trajectory of the entire Gospel. Mark does not separate glory from suffering, but presents a path in which the King passes through rejection and death before the appearance of victory.

45. The Relationship Between Mark and Peter

The figure of Peter occupies an important place in the Gospel of Mark, and reports about him appear frequently and with great candor, including his weakness and his denial of Christ. Early Christian tradition linked the Gospel to Peter's testimony, and Papias mentioned that Mark was Peter's interpreter and that he recorded what he remembered of Christ's words and deeds.

If this tradition is correct, Peter's strong presence in the narrative becomes understandable, especially since the Gospel does not present an idealized picture of him, but retains even the events of his failure. This gives special importance to the Gospel as a testimony that does not hide the witness's weakness, but focuses on the faithfulness and power of Christ.

46. The Relationship Between Mark, Matthew, and Luke

The Gospel of Mark is the shortest among the four Gospels, and it is one of the three Synoptic Gospels alongside Matthew and Luke. A strong literary relationship exists among these Gospels, and many scholars hold that Mark was the earliest written Gospel and that Matthew and Luke used his framework and extensive material from him, with differing detailed interpretations regarding the question of sources.

Mark is characterized by rapid movement and frequent transitions between events, with a strong focus on Christ's deeds, His miracles, and His conflict with the powers of evil and the religious leaders, whereas Matthew expands the teaching content and highlights the fulfillment of prophecies and the kingdom of heaven, and Luke gives special attention to history, salvation, mercy, and marginalized groups. Therefore, studying Mark independently is important even when reading it in parallel with Matthew and Luke.

47. The Encyclopedic Summary

The Gospel of Mark is the Gospel that presents Jesus Christ as the Son of God, the Messiah, and the suffering Servant who entered history to announce the kingdom of God, confront the authority of evil, liberate humanity, heal the sick, and raise the dead. The Gospel begins rapidly and directly from John the Baptist to the appearance of Christ, then moves across Galilee in a successive series of teaching, miracles, and confrontations, while the central question unfolds: Who is Jesus? The answer emerges gradually, but it does not reach its fullness until we see Christ willingly walking toward the Cross, accepting death, and giving His life as a ransom for many. At the Cross, all human standards are inverted, because the King appears in the form of the crucified One, the Son of God is revealed at the moment of death, the veil of the temple is torn, and then the empty tomb comes to proclaim that death has not triumphed. Therefore, the grand message of the Gospel of Mark is that Christ is known not only through the power of His miracles, but through the path of His service, His Cross, and His Resurrection, and that whoever desires to follow Him must accept the path of self-denial and bearing the cross, because the kingdom brought by Christ reveals the glory of God through service, self-giving, and victory over death.

48. Basic References

The primary biblical text is the Gospel of Mark 1–16, with special reference to Isaiah 40, Psalms 2, 22, 23, 69, Deuteronomy, Malachi 3, Zechariah, Hosea, and other Old Testament texts that help in understanding the background of Christ's appearance, ministry, and sufferings. Among the important commentary and research references are: R. T. France, The Gospel of Mark; James R. Edwards, The Gospel According to Mark; William L. Lane, The Gospel According to Mark; Robert A. Guelich, Mark 1–8:26; Robert H. Stein, Mark; Mark L. Strauss, Mark; Craig S. Keener, The IVP Bible Background Commentary: New Testament; William Hendriksen, Exposition of the Gospel According to Mark; Matthew Henry Commentary; Pulpit Commentary; Ellicott's Commentary; Jamieson-Fausset-Brown Commentary; The Expositor's Bible Commentary; Word Biblical Commentary; New International Commentary on the New Testament; New Bible Dictionary. Issues such as the date and place of writing and Mark's relationship with Peter remain topics that should be presented with an appropriate degree of scholarly caution, because the sources do not provide a decisive historical consensus at every point.
