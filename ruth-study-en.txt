Book of Ruth
Ruth — רוּת — Rūt
6
1. The Name of the Book and Its Meaning

The Hebrew name of the book is רוּת — Rūt — Ruth, the name of a Moabite woman who became one of the most prominent female figures in the Bible, although she was not of Israelite origin.

The Old Testament does not offer a certain linguistic meaning for the name "Ruth", and therefore caution should be exercised against giving it a definitive, unestablished meaning. Some studies have suggested that the name is connected to a Semitic root denoting friendship or companionship, but this interpretation is not settled, so it is best, from an encyclopedic standpoint, to say that the origin of the name's meaning is uncertain.

The significance of the name lies more in the person who bore it and the story that became associated with it; Ruth enters, from a Gentile background, into the people of Israel, moving from poverty and widowhood to the house of David, and from Moab to Bethlehem, becoming part of the line that would lead to King David and then to Christ according to the genealogy in Matthew.

2. The Author

The Book of Ruth does not explicitly name its author.

Ancient Jewish tradition attributed the book to Samuel the prophet, but this attribution cannot be proven from within the text itself.

The book, in its present form, presents a complete story with an extremely precise literary structure, beginning with death and famine and ending with life, offspring, and the name of David; therefore, what matters in biblical study is to note that the story serves a clear historical and theological purpose: showing how God works in small details to shape the history of salvation.

3. The Time of the Events of the Book

The book explicitly states that the events took place:

"In the days when the judges ruled".

This phrase directly links Ruth to the preceding book.

But the contrast is striking:

Judges portrays a society collapsing spiritually and morally.

Ruth, however, presents to us, in the midst of this darkness, a story of faithfulness, mercy, and redemption.

And while the phrase in Judges was:

"Every man did what was right in his own eyes"

in Ruth we find people doing what is good toward others, especially Ruth and Boaz.

4. For Whom Was the Book Written?

The book was written for an Israelite audience that understood the importance of land, family, offspring, and kinship.

The Israelite reader knew that the continuation of the family, the name, and the inheritance were matters of great importance within Hebrew society.

But the book broadens the vision in a very beautiful way, because it presents a Moabite woman as part of God's people.

Thus the book declares that belonging to God's people is understood not only through national origin, but is also shown through faith, loyalty, and attachment to the God of Israel and His people.

5. Why was the Book of Ruth written?

The book has more than one purpose.

First, it explains an important origin of the family of King David.

Second, it presents a profound picture of redemption and the near kinsman-redeemer.

Third, it declares that God works even when He seems silent.

Fourth, it reveals that God can use a foreign woman at the heart of the history that would lead to King David.

Therefore, Ruth is not merely a love story between a man and a woman, but a story of divine providence, redemption, offspring, and kingship.

6. The General Division of the Book

The book consists of four chapters, and can be divided according to its main scenes:

Ruth 1

The famine, the deaths, and the return of Naomi and Ruth to Bethlehem.

Ruth 2

Ruth in the field of Boaz.

Ruth 3

The request for redemption at the threshing floor.

Ruth 4

Redemption, marriage, offspring, and the birth of Obed.

The literary structure of the book moves from:

Famine → death → emptiness → encounter → redemption → marriage → birth → David.

This transition is the heart of the message.

7. The Beginning of the Story: The Famine

The book begins, in the days of the judges, with a famine in the land.

This has theological significance within the ancient context, because the land that God had promised to Israel was suffering from famine.

So a family goes out from Bethlehem to the country of Moab in search of food.

The name Bethlehem itself means in Hebrew approximately "house of bread", and the irony is that the place bearing the name "house of bread" is suffering from famine.

8. Elimelech and Naomi

The man's name is Elimelech, meaning "my God is king".

His wife is Naomi, whose name is connected to the meaning of "pleasantness" or "sweetness".

But Naomi's life is completely overturned.

Elimelech dies.

Then her two sons, Mahlon and Chilion, die.

Naomi is left with her two sons' wives, Ruth and Orpah.

Here the book begins from the utmost depths of human emptiness.

9. Ruth and Orpah

Naomi decides to return to Bethlehem.

She asks Ruth and Orpah to return to their people.

Orpah returns.

But Ruth refuses.

She says to Naomi:

"Wherever you go, I will go, and wherever you lodge, I will lodge. Your people shall be my people, and your God, my God".

This is one of the greatest statements of loyalty in the Bible.

Ruth does not choose Naomi alone.

She chooses Naomi's people and Naomi's God.

10. Why is Ruth's decision important?

Ruth was a Moabite.

Moab was a nation connected to Israel by many historical conflicts.

But Ruth transcends the boundaries of national origin and enters into a relationship of covenant and loyalty.

Therefore her story becomes an example that God can bring a person from outside Israel into the history of His people.

This is a theme that will later appear more broadly in the history of salvation.

11. The Return to Bethlehem

Naomi and Ruth arrive at Bethlehem.

Naomi says to the women:

"Do not call me Naomi, but call me Mara".

She no longer sees herself as the sweet, full "Naomi," but as a woman who feels that God has allowed her life to become bitter again.

But the reader knows that the story is not over.

And this is one of the beauties of the journey:

Naomi sees the emptiness, while God works behind the scenes to create fullness.

12. The Beginning of the Harvest

Ruth and Naomi reach Bethlehem at the beginning of the barley harvest.

And this is no passing agricultural fact.

The harvest means that the famine has begun to recede.

And from here the transformation begins:

From hunger to food.

From death to life.

From emptiness to hope.

13. Boaz's Field

Ruth goes to glean the grain behind the harvesters.

This was connected to the law that gave the poor, the sojourner, and the widow the right to benefit from what was left in the fields.

And here an important name appears:

Boaz — בֹּעַז — Bo‘az.

The name is usually understood in connection with strength or swiftness, though there is linguistic debate about its precise derivation.

But more important than the meaning of the name is its role in the story.

14. Boaz

Boaz is a wealthy and prominent man, but he also appears as one who knows the law and applies it with mercy.

When he sees Ruth, he asks about her.

Then he permits her to remain in his field.

And he orders the workers not to harass her.

Rather, he commands them to leave some grain for her deliberately.

And here the practical picture of the Torah appears:

The law is not merely a text; it becomes mercy when a person applies it with a righteous heart.

15. Why did Boaz care about Ruth?

Boaz knows what Ruth did for Naomi.

She left her father and mother and the land of her birth and came to a people she did not know.

And he says to her that the Lord will reward her labor.

And here there is a deep theological point:

Ruth entered the land of Israel as a foreigner.

But she finds in the people of God a place of mercy and protection.

16. Ruth and the Foreigners

The story of Ruth prominently illustrates a repeated biblical principle:

God cares for the sojourner, the widow, and the poor.

Ruth almost gathers these cases in one person:

She is a widow.

She is poor.

She is a foreigner.

Yet she becomes the object of God's care.

Thus the book offers a practical model of social mercy in the law.

17. The Threshing Floor

After the harvest season ends, Naomi tells Ruth to go to Boaz at the threshing floor.

This scene needs careful reading, because some superficial readings turn it into a romantic story, while the text speaks in a much deeper way about kinship, redemption, marriage, and the continuation of the family line.

Ruth asks Boaz to take on the role of the near kinsman.

18. The Kinsman-Redeemer

The important Hebrew word here is:

גֹּאֵל — Go'el — the kinsman-redeemer.

The go'el is the close relative who is able to intervene within the framework of the law to recover certain rights related to land and family.

Here the word redemption is transformed from a general religious concept into a tangible legal-social act.

Boaz does not stop at sympathy.

Rather, he accepts responsibility.

19. The Existence of a Closer Relative

But Boaz discovers that there is a nearer relative than he.

And this is a very important point in the narrative structure.

For Boaz wants to redeem, but he does not go beyond the law.

He waits for the one with the first right.

Then the process is carried out before the elders.

Thus the book presents Boaz as a picture of the man who combines:

mercy and justice.

20. The Process of Redemption

Boaz and the elders gather at the city gate.

And the city gate in the ancient world was the place of judgment and the completion of formal transactions.

There Boaz presents the matter of the land and the woman.

And the nearer kinsman refuses to redeem, then declares Boaz ready to bear the responsibility.

And the transaction is completed before witnesses.

Thus the story moves from the personal sphere to the broader legal framework.

21. The Marriage of Ruth and Boaz

Boaz marries Ruth.

The woman who began the journey as a poor Moabite widow has now become the wife of a man of importance in Bethlehem.

But the greater surprise is not the marriage.

The greater surprise is the offspring who will come from this marriage.

22. The Birth of Obed

Obed is born.

And Obed becomes the father of Jesse.

And Jesse becomes the father of David.

And here the book reveals its full historical purpose:

Ruth → Obed → Jesse → David.

Thus, the story of a poor Moabite woman ends within the genealogy of the greatest kings of Israel.

23. Ruth and David

The book ends with a brief genealogy that leads to David.

This means that the book wants the reader to understand that events that seem very small were, in fact, part of a larger plan.

Ruth did not know that she would become the grandmother of King David.

And Naomi did not know when she said:

“Mara”

that her story would end with the birth of a child who would restore hope to her.

And Boaz did not know that his work as a kinsman-redeemer would enter the royal line.

And here the very important concept emerges:

Divine Providence

God works through ordinary events without making His hand conspicuous.

24. The Absence of Visible Miracles

It is very noticeable that the Book of Ruth does not contain a supernatural miracle such as the parting of the sea or the collapse of walls.

But this does not mean that God is absent.

Rather, the strength of the book lies in the fact that it presents God's work in the details of daily life:

Hunger.

the journey.

Work.

the harvest.

The marriage.

kinship.

birth.

Then, suddenly, the reader discovers that these small details were part of the history of salvation.

25. The Most Important Message the Book Presents

The message can be summarized as:

God works faithfully in the lives of ordinary people, turning bitterness into hope, emptiness into fullness, and using faithfulness, love, and redemption to shape His purposes in history.

This is a very important message in the study of divine providence.

Not all of God's work comes in the form of a miracle.

Sometimes it comes in the form of:

a door opened.

one person helping another.

a faithful decision.

a harvest at its proper time.

a marriage.

a child is born.

Then, after years, we discover that God's hand was present all along.

26. Ruth in the Line of Salvation

Ruth is one of the women mentioned in the genealogy of Christ in the Gospel of Matthew.

And Matthew records:

“Boaz fathered Obed by Rahab. Obed fathered Jesse. And Jesse fathered King David.”

Here the story becomes even deeper.

For both Rahab and Ruth were women from foreign backgrounds, and yet both entered the line that leads to David.

This reveals early on that God's saving plan is not confined to national boundaries in the way human beings might imagine.

27. Ruth and Christ

The book does not offer a direct prophecy saying that Ruth points to Christ, but the concept of the kinsman-redeemer has become one of the central elements that some Christian interpretation connects to the work of Christ.

Boaz was a close relative who was able to redeem.

But Christ took on our human nature and became close to us in order to fulfill the work of redemption in a deeper and fuller way.

Boaz paid a price to recover land, a name, and a family.

But Christ redeemed humanity from the dominion of sin and death.

Therefore, Boaz may be studied as a limited type of the redeemer, while preserving the essential difference between the type and the fully developed Christian doctrine of the New Testament.

28. Naomi: From Bitterness to Fullness

Naomi's journey is one of the deepest inward journeys in the book.

It begins:

with a husband.

then becomes:

a widow.

Then:

a mother without sons.

Then:

a woman who sees her life as empty.

but she ends:

with a child in her arms.

And thus the word “Mara” becomes a story of restoration.

And the book does not deny her pain, but it declares that pain is not the final word in the story of the believer.

29. Ruth: A Model of Faithfulness

Ruth was not in authority.

She was not wealthy.

She was not a military leader.

She was not a prophetess.

But she was faithful.

She left her homeland.

She served Naomi.

She worked in the fields.

She honored Boaz.

She embraced the path of redemption.

And in the end she became part of the line of David.

And this reveals an important principle:

God can use faithfulness in small matters to produce great historical results.

30. Boaz: A Model of Redemption

Boaz combines several qualities:

a close relative.

able to redeem.

willing to redeem.

committed to the law.

he pays the price.

He protects the weak woman.

He preserves the family name.

And for this reason, Boaz became one of the most important figures in Christian typological studies related to redemption.

But we must always remember that Boaz is a limited man, whereas Christ is the complete redeemer.

31. Bethlehem

Bethlehem is not merely a small village in the story of Ruth.

It is the place where several biblical threads converge:

Ruth and Boaz.

Then:

Obed.

Then:

Jesse.

Then:

David.

Then in the New Testament:

The birth of Jesus Christ in Bethlehem.

And thus the same geographical location becomes part of the story of salvation.

32. The time of writing of the book

The book specifies the time of the events as the days of the judges.

As for the date of the final compilation of the book, it is a matter of discussion.

The presence of David's genealogy at the end indicates that the reader knows David, and therefore the text in its current form presumes knowledge of King David.

Some scholars argue that the book was compiled or composed in a later period, and perhaps it had a purpose in explaining the legitimacy of the house of David and displaying its family roots.

But one cannot determine a precise year for the writing of the book without exceeding what the textual evidence allows.

33. The most important places
Moab

The place where Ruth lived before her migration to Israel.

Bethlehem

The main location of the events, where Boaz, Naomi, and Ruth lived, and from which the line of David came.

Boaz's fields

The place where the story of provision and meeting began.

The threshing floor

The place where the encounter between Ruth and Boaz concerning redemption took place.

The gate of the city

The place where the legal transaction was conducted before the elders.

34. The most important characters
Ruth

The foreigner who became part of God's people, and represents faithfulness and loyalty.

Naomi

The widow who passed from bitterness to restoration.

Boaz

The kinsman-redeemer who performed the work of redemption.

Elimelech

The husband of Naomi, and with his death the first crisis begins.

Orpah

The daughter-in-law who returned to her people.

Obed

The son of Ruth and Boaz, and the grandfather of David.

Jesse

The father of David, and appears at the end of the genealogical line.

David

The king who connects the story of Ruth to the history of Israelite monarchy.

35. The key words in the book

There is a group of concepts that the scholar must retain when studying Ruth:

Loyalty.

Mercy.

Redemption.

The kinsman-redeemer.

The land.

The offspring.

The family.

Divine providence.

The transition from bitterness to hope.

And these words reveal that the book is far greater than an emotional story.

36. Why did the book of Ruth come after Judges?

This arrangement is remarkably wise.

Judges ends with moral and political collapse.

Then Ruth comes and presents to us a small household in Bethlehem in which faithful people live.

Judges says:

«Each one does what seems right in his own eyes».

Ruth presents people who practically say:

«I will do what is good for the other».

Judges ends with chaos.

Ruth ends with the birth of David.

And thus the narrative moves from:

Chaos → to hope.

37. The theological line from Genesis to Ruth
Genesis

Promise and offspring and land.

↓

Exodus

Redemption and salvation.

↓

Leviticus

Holiness and worship.

↓

Numbers

The wilderness and the test of faith.

↓

Deuteronomy

The covenant and preparation for the land.

↓

Joshua

Entering the land.

↓

Judges

Repeated falling and the need for a savior.

↓

Ruth

Providence and redemption and the offspring that leads to David.

And here appears a beautiful biblical movement:

Joshua gave the land.

Judges exposed the corruption of humanity.

Ruth showed that God works in the midst of chaos.

Then David appears at the end of the line.

And from David begins the royal line that would later become one of the most important messianic lines in the Bible.

🔑 Summary of the Book of Ruth

The Hebrew name: רוּת — Ruth.
Meaning of the name: not linguistically settled with certainty.
Author: not explicitly mentioned, and Jewish tradition attributes it to Samuel.
Time of the events: the days of the Judges.
Place: Moab and Bethlehem.
Theme: loyalty, redemption, divine providence, and lineage.
Most important events: the death of Elimelech and his sons, the return of Naomi and Ruth, the meeting with Boaz, the harvest, the redemption, the marriage, the birth of Obed.
Most important characters: Ruth, Naomi, Boaz, Obed, Jesse, David.
Most important idea: God works in the small details to accomplish His great purposes.
The messianic axis: Ruth enters into the lineage of David, and from David's line comes the Messiah according to the New Testament.
