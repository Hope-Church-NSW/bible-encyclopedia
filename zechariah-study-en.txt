Introduction to the Book of Zechariah

The central idea of the book: The Book of Zechariah is one of the richest of the prophetic books in visions, symbols, and messianic hope. The prophet came to a people who had returned from exile, weak and discouraged, while the temple was still under construction, and the political reality did not resemble the glories of David and Solomon. So his message came to declare that God had not forsaken His people, that the work He had begun would be completed, and that the future is in God's hand, not in the hand of empires. The book begins with a clear call: "Return to Me, and I will return to you," then it opens before the prophet a series of night visions that reveal God's work in Jerusalem, and it ends with great declarations about the coming King, the Shepherd, the pierced One, the Day of the LORD, and the sanctification of Jerusalem. Therefore the movement of the book can be summarized as: return → visions → cleansing → building → a coming king → rejection of the Shepherd → tribulation → the coming of the LORD → the kingdom and holiness.

1. Name of the book and its meaning

The name of the book is Zechariah — זְכַרְיָה — Zekaryah / Zechariah, a Hebrew name composed of an element related to the root ז-כ-ר — z-k-r meaning "to remember" or "to be remembered," together with the name of God "Yahweh." Therefore the name generally means "Yahweh has remembered" or "the LORD has remembered."

This meaning is very fitting for the message of the book, because the people of God might have felt that the exile had made God forget them, but Zechariah declares that God had not forgotten Jerusalem, nor His covenant, nor His purpose.

2. Who is Zechariah?

The book presents Zechariah as:

"Zechariah son of Berechiah, son of Iddo the prophet."

The name Zechariah son of Iddo also appears in the Book of Ezra among the prophets who served with Haggai after the return from exile. It is possible that "Iddo" was the name of the grandfather by which Zechariah was known in the priestly record, while the book mentions the name of the father and the grandfather.

It appears from the background of the book that Zechariah was connected to the priestly circle, which is consistent with the frequent references in his book to Joshua the high priest, the temple, holiness, and the priesthood.

3. The year of Zechariah's birth and death

The Bible does not mention the year of Zechariah's birth or the year of his death. Direct historical information about his personal life is also very limited. However, the period of his ministry can be determined with relative accuracy from the dates mentioned in the book, since he began his prophetic ministry in the second year of Darius the king, which is approximately the year 520 BC.

4. Date of writing the book

Zechariah's ministry began in the second year of Darius, in the eighth month, according to Zechariah 1:1. Then the series of visions comes in the eleventh month of the same year, while some prophecies extend to later years.

Therefore a distinction must be made between chapters 1–8, which are clearly connected to the phase of rebuilding the temple, and chapters 9–14, which differ in style and historical background, and scholars have long debated the question of their date and literary unity.

5. The Historical Background of the Book

The Jews returned from the Babylonian exile to Jerusalem, but the return did not mean the return of the kingdom to its former glory. There was no king from David's line ruling as David or Solomon had, Judah was under Persian authority, and the temple was in the process of being rebuilt.

In this environment Haggai and Zechariah appeared. Haggai focused strongly on the necessity of rebuilding the temple, while Zechariah took the matter to a broader horizon: God does not want merely a building, but wants to restore His people, purify their leadership, and fulfill His royal purpose in Jerusalem.

6. To whom did Zechariah write?

Zechariah wrote to the community of Judah returning from exile, especially to the generation that was trying to rebuild its religious and national life in Jerusalem. Among the key figures in the background of the book are Zerubbabel the governor and Joshua the high priest.

But the message goes beyond that generation itself, because the prophecies extend to themes concerning the future, the coming King, Jerusalem, the nations, and the Day of the LORD.

7. The Reason for Writing the Book

The people needed more than a command to build. They needed hope.

The temple could be built, but what about the future of the nation? What about the Davidic king? What about the sin that led to the exile? What about the surrounding nations? What about God's ancient promise?

Zechariah came to declare that God was still working in history, that the exile had not annulled the covenant, and that Jerusalem had a future in God's purpose.

8. Division of the Book of Zechariah

The book can be divided into two main sections:

Chapters 1–8

Prophetic visions and messages directly connected to the period after the exile and the rebuilding of the temple.

Chapters 9–14

A collection of future prophecies dealing with the King, the nations, the Shepherd, the tribulation, the Day of the LORD, and the reign of the LORD.

Between the two sections there is a single thread: God restores His people and leads history toward His kingdom.

9. The opening message — "Return to Me"

Zechariah begins with a clear message:

"Return to Me, says the LORD of hosts, and I will return to you, says the LORD of hosts."

This phrase sums up the spiritual introduction of the book. Before speaking of visions, the future, and the king, there is a foundational issue: the relationship between God and His people.

10. The danger of repeating the history of the fathers

Zechariah mentions that the fathers of the people did not listen to the prophets. Therefore he warns the new generation against repeating history.

The idea is clear: the return from geographical exile is not enough if there is no spiritual return to God.

11. The first vision — the horses among the myrtle trees

Zechariah sees men on horses among myrtle trees. Then he hears that the whole earth is resting and quiet.

The vision reveals that God is watching over the nations and history, even when it seems to His people that God is far away. From here begins the series of visions that affirm that what happens in the world is not outside God's control.

12. Jerusalem at the heart of the vision

The LORD says that He is jealous for Jerusalem and Zion with great jealousy. This phrase is very important, because the people coming out of exile might ask: does God still care about Jerusalem?

The answer is yes. God has not abandoned Zion.

13. The second vision — four horns

Zechariah sees four horns, then asks about their meaning. The answer comes that they are the powers that scattered Judah and Jerusalem.

The horn in the Bible often symbolizes power and authority. Here the horns become an image of the empires and powers that history used against God's people.

14. Four craftsmen

After the horns, Zechariah sees four craftsmen who came to terrify the horns and cast them down.

The image is very important: the powers that terrified God's people do not have the final word. God is able to raise up other instruments that break the power of the powers that broke His people.

15. The third vision — a man with a measuring line in his hand

Zechariah sees a man going out to measure Jerusalem.

The message here is astonishing:

Jerusalem will not remain a small, limited city.

The LORD declares that Jerusalem will dwell as villages without walls because of the multitude of people and livestock in it.

16. "I will be a wall of fire around her"

The Lord says:

"And I will be to her a wall of fire all around, and I will be the glory in her midst."

This is one of the greatest images in the book. The true security of Jerusalem will not be in a stone wall alone, but in the presence of God Himself.

17. "Glory in her midst"

The phrase links security with glory. God is not only the guardian of the city from without, but He is its glory from within.

This is a very important idea in the study of the temple and Jerusalem: the value of the city does not come from stone alone, but from the presence of God.

18. The fourth vision — Joshua the high priest

The book arrives at an extremely important scene:

Joshua the high priest standing before the angel of the LORD, with Satan standing at his right hand to oppose him.

Joshua appears in filthy garments.

This image reveals a problem deeper than urban destruction: impurity and sin need to be dealt with before restoration can be complete.

19. "The filthy garment"

The LORD commands that the filthy garments be removed from Joshua. Then He says:

"See, I have taken your iniquity away from you, and I will clothe you with rich robes."

This is one of the clearest images of justification and cleansing in the book.

20. Joshua as a model for the people

Joshua is not merely an individual. The scene represents the priesthood, the people, and the return itself. Israel came out of exile, but the return needs spiritual cleansing.

Thus Zechariah reveals that rebuilding the temple without dealing with sin is not the complete solution.

21. "My servant the Branch"

In the same vision the promise appears:

"Behold, I am bringing forth My servant the Branch."

"The Branch" becomes one of the important titles in the prophecies concerning the coming king. It is linked to the idea of new growth from a root that appears cut off, a fitting image for Davidic hope after the fall of the kingdom.

22. The single stone

The LORD says that He will set before Joshua a single stone, and on the stone are seven eyes.

This image is one of the most ambiguous and variously interpreted in the book. But in the context of the book it is linked to God's complete work, divine watchfulness, cleansing, and the removal of the iniquity of the land in one day.

In later Christian reading, the images of "the Branch" and "the Stone" were linked to broad messianic lines in the Bible.

23. The fifth vision — the lampstand and the two olive trees

Zechariah sees a lampstand of gold with two olive trees beside it.

Then comes the great phrase:

"Not by might nor by power, but by My Spirit, says the LORD of hosts."

This is perhaps the most famous phrase in the book of Zechariah.

24. "Not by might nor by power"

The people faced a task that seemed greater than their capabilities. But God declares that the accomplishment of His purpose does not depend on human strength alone.

This phrase does not mean the abolition of work or planning, but that the source of the ultimate success of the divine work is the Spirit of God.

25. "Who are you, O great mountain?"

The Lord says:

"Who are you, O great mountain? Before Zerubbabel you shall become a plain."

The mountain here represents the great obstacle to the completion of the work.

The message is that no matter how great the obstacle, it cannot stand against God's purpose.

26. Zerubbabel and the final stone

The LORD declares that Zerubbabel will bring forth the capstone amid shouts of acclaim.

That is, the work Zerubbabel began will reach its completion. This encourages the people not to despise the day of small things.

27. "The day of small things"

The book says:

"For who has despised the day of small things?"

This phrase is directly linked to the state of the community after the exile. There was no great political glory, nor resources like the days of Solomon, but God was at work.

The lesson is clear: the divine measure is not always the size of the beginning, but the faithfulness of the work and God's purpose behind it.

28. The sixth vision — the flying scroll

Zechariah sees a flying scroll, and the vision declares that the curse going out over the face of the whole earth will enter the houses of the thief and the one who swears falsely.

Here the theme of moral sin returns. God is not concerned only with the temple and worship, but also with theft, lying, and corruption within society.

29. The seventh vision — the ephah

The prophet sees an ephah, a measure used in trade, and in it a woman associated with wickedness.

The image points to the removal of wickedness from the midst of the land. It is as if God does not want only the restoration of worship, but also wants to cleanse society of the practices that had corrupted it.

30. The eighth vision — the four chariots

Zechariah sees four chariots coming out from between two mountains of bronze.

This vision is linked to the spirits of heaven that go out to patrol the earth. Thus the book returns to its great theme: God works on a global scale, not only within the borders of Jerusalem.

31. Chapters 7–8 — Fasting

The people ask about fasting, and whether they should continue it as they did during the exile.

God answers with a deeper question: was your fasting really for the LORD?

Here Zechariah corrects the concept of worship: ritual without the heart does not fulfill God's purpose.

32. “Administer justice.”

The Lord requires mercy and justice, and no oppression of the widow, the orphan, the foreigner, and the poor.

This is a very important point in the book of Zechariah: rebuilding the temple cannot be separated from the moral rebuilding of the human being.

33. Great promises to Jerusalem

In chapter eight, the tone clearly turns toward hope.

The Lord says:

“I have returned to Zion and will dwell in the midst of Jerusalem.”

Then Jerusalem becomes the city of righteousness, and the elders and children dwell in her streets.

The picture is the transition from a ruined city to a living, vibrant community.

34. “The fast of the fourth and fifth month...”

The Lord declares that the fasts of grief will be turned into joy, gladness, and good feasts.

This is a very beautiful principle in the book: God is able to turn the memory of disaster into the memory of salvation.

35. “Many peoples are coming.”

The promise expands further:

“And many peoples and the inhabitants of many cities will come.”

And the peoples will come to Jerusalem to seek the Lord.

Here the universal dimension of the book becomes clear: God does not work for Israel in isolation from the nations, but desires that the knowledge of Him may reach the peoples.

36. Zechariah 9 — The Coming King

The second section of the book begins with a magnificent royal tone:

“Rejoice greatly, O daughter of Zion; cry aloud, O daughter of Jerusalem. Behold, your King comes to you; He is righteous and victorious, humble, and riding on a donkey, and on a colt, the foal of a donkey.”

This is among the most famous messianic prophecies in the Bible.

37. The Messiah's Entry into Jerusalem

The Gospel of Matthew uses this prophecy directly in describing the Messiah's entry into Jerusalem. John also mentions the prophecy itself in the context of the kingly entry of the Messiah.

Thus the image of the humble king riding on the donkey becomes one of the clearest points of contact between Zechariah and the New Testament.

38. The Kind of King Declared by Zechariah

The surprise is that the King is not riding a massive war chariot. Rather, He comes humbly, riding on a donkey.

This reveals that His kingdom differs from the kingdoms of the world. He is a true King, but He does not present the image of a king who relies on military display.

39. “He will speak peace to the nations”

It says concerning the King:

“And He will speak peace to the nations, and His dominion will be from sea to sea.”

Then the King does not come only to restore Israel, but He bears a message of peace to the nations.

This expands greatly in the New Testament toward the kingdom of Christ.

40. Zechariah 11 — The Shepherd

The book moves to the image of the shepherd and the flock.

It becomes clear that the people rejected proper care, and even the shepherd himself was rejected and given thirty pieces of silver.

This is one of the most important prophecies of Zechariah in reading the New Testament.

41. The Thirty Pieces of Silver

The Gospel of Matthew links the thirty pieces of silver to Judas's betrayal of the Messiah. Matthew cites material associated with Zechariah 11, though there is a well-known interpretive complication because of the mention of Jeremiah in the text.

But from the Christian perspective, the image of the thirty pieces of silver remains one of the strongest links between Zechariah and the story of the Messiah's Passion.

42. Zechariah 12 — “They will look on Me, whom they have pierced.”

A highly significant phrase comes:

“And I will pour out on the house of David and on the inhabitants of Jerusalem a spirit of grace and supplication, and they will look on Me, whom they have pierced.”

Then the text speaks of mourning for the one who was pierced.

The Gospel of John also used this prophecy in the context of the piercing of the side of Christ on the cross.

43. The Pierced Messiah

In John 19:37 we find a clear reference to this prophecy.

Here the image of “the pierced one” becomes part of the Christian reading of the Passion of the Messiah, where the cross does not appear as an isolated event apart from God's plan, but as part of the prophetic line that connects the King, rejection, the piercing, and mourning.

44. Zechariah 13 — “Strike the Shepherd”

He says:

“Strike the shepherd, and the sheep will be scattered.”

The Messiah uses this verse in the Gospels when He speaks of the scattering of the disciples during the Passion.

Thus the book connects once again directly with the Passion week.

45. “An Open Fountain”

He says:

“In that day there shall be an open fountain for the house of David and for the inhabitants of Jerusalem, for sin and for impurity.”

The image here combines purification and salvation. The sin that was the cause of judgment is not the last word; God opens a fountain for cleansing.

46. Zechariah 14 — The Day of the Lord

The book reaches one of its greatest eschatological visions.

It speaks of a day when the nations come against Jerusalem, and then the Lord Himself intervenes. It says:

“And His feet shall stand in that day upon the Mount of Olives, which is before Jerusalem on the east.”

This verse has become of great importance in Christian studies concerning the Second Coming and the Day of the Lord.

47. The Mount of Olives

The Mount of Olives is not merely a geographical location. It is a place associated in the New Testament with the crucifixion of the Messiah, His prayers, and His ascension, and then in the book of Zechariah it becomes part of the scene of the Lord's intervention in the last day.

This makes prophetic geography especially important when reading the end of the book in light of the New Testament.

48. “The Lord will be King over all the earth”

The book reaches a comprehensive royal declaration:

“The Lord will be King over all the earth. In that day the Lord will be one and His name one.”

This is the great goal of the book: not merely the political victory of Israel, but the proclamation of the kingdom of the Lord over all the earth.

49. Holiness in the Bells and Vessels

The book concludes in a striking manner:

“In that day there will be upon the bells of the horses: Holy to the Lord.”

Then holiness extends even to the vessels.

That is, the holiness that was especially associated with the temple and the priesthood will fill all of life.

50. Zechariah and Comprehensive Holiness

This ending is very important. The book begins with the problem of sin and impurity, passes through the cleansing of Joshua, and ends with a world in which “Holy to the Lord” is written on things.

As if the entire movement is:

Impurity → cleansing → presence → kingdom → holiness.

51. Zechariah and the Temple

The temple in Zechariah is not merely a building. It is a sign of the return of God's presence among His people.

But the book goes beyond stone to the human being. The priest must be purified, the people must practice justice, Jerusalem must become the city of righteousness, and the coming King must arrive in peace.

52. Zechariah and the Davidic King

One of the most important lines of the book is the continued royal hope.

After the exile, there was no independent Davidic kingdom, but prophecy did not abandon the promise of the King. The Branch appears, the humble King, the Shepherd, and the King who will reign over all the earth.

These images converge in Christian interpretation in the person of Jesus Christ.

53. Zechariah and the Messiah

It is difficult to study the book of Zechariah without pausing at length on its relationship to the New Testament.

The book presents a series of images that the New Testament uses in speaking of Christ:

the humble King, the Branch, the Shepherd, the thirty pieces of silver, the pierced one, the struck Shepherd, the spring of cleansing, and the Day of the Lord.

This makes Zechariah one of the most frequently used prophetic books for interpreting the events of Christ's suffering and his kingdom.

54. Zechariah and the Gospel

One can see the line of the Gospel within the book in a gradual way:

God calls for repentance.

Then:

God purifies the sinner.

Then:

God restores his presence to Jerusalem.

Then:

The humble King comes.

Then:

The Shepherd is rejected and pierced.

Then:

A spring is opened for cleansing.

Then:

The Lord intervenes and reigns over all the earth.

This is a profound literary structure that links salvation, the Cross, and the kingdom.

55. Zechariah and the Cross

If we read the book in the light of the New Testament, the Cross appears in a series of overlapping images: the rejected Shepherd, the thirty pieces of silver, the struck Shepherd, the pierced one, and then the spring of cleansing.

This makes Zechariah one of the most important books for understanding how the New Testament connects Christ's suffering with the fulfillment of the ancient prophecies.

56. Zechariah and the Resurrection and the Kingdom

The book does not provide a detailed account of the resurrection as the Gospels do, but it portrays a future in which the Lord is king over all the earth, and Jerusalem becomes the center of worship and holiness.

Therefore, the hope of the book goes beyond a mere return from exile to a kingdom-centered and eschatological horizon.

57. Zechariah and the Church

Zechariah's message to the church is very strong: do not despise small work, do not rely on human strength, do not separate worship from holiness, and do not lose hope when reality seems weak.

For the God who began the work in Jerusalem is the same one who promised to complete it; the God who purified the high priest is able to purify his people; and the God who announced the coming King is the same one who will proclaim his kingship in the end.

58. The most important people in the book
Zechariah

The prophet who bore the visions of hope, purification, and the kingdom.

Zerubbabel

The governor of Judah and leader of the rebuilding of the temple, who appears in the book as an instrument for completing the work.

Joshua son of Jozadak

The high priest who appears in the vision of the filthy garments and purification, and who is associated with the promise of the 'Branch'.

Darius

The Persian king in whose reign Zechariah's ministry began.

The coming King

The royal figure who appears in Zechariah 9 as a humble king and who, in the New Testament, receives a direct messianic interpretation.

The Shepherd

The figure who is rejected and struck, and whose image becomes associated in the Gospels with Christ's sufferings.

The nations

The powers and peoples that appear in the visions as part of history that ultimately submits to the Lord's reign.

59. The most important events in the book

The call to the people to return to the Lord, the vision of the horses among the myrtle trees, the vision of the four horns, the vision of the craftsmen, the vision of the measuring line, the promise of a wall of fire around Jerusalem, the cleansing of Joshua the high priest, the promise of the 'Branch', the vision of the lampstand and the olive trees, the declaration, 'Not by might nor by power, but by my Spirit,' the fall of the great mountain, the completion of the temple, the vision of the flying scroll, the vision of the ephah, the vision of the four chariots, the discussion of fasting, the promises of the restoration of Jerusalem, the coming of the humble King, the thirty pieces of silver, the pierced one, the striking of the Shepherd, the spring of cleansing, the Mount of Olives, and then the Lord's reign over all the earth.

60. The main message the book presents

God had not forgotten his people after the exile, and Jerusalem's weakness was not evidence of the failure of his promise; rather, he was working quietly to rebuild the temple, purify the priesthood, and restore his people. That is why he said, 'Return to me, and I will return to you,' then announced that the work would not be accomplished 'by might nor by power, but by my Spirit.' He also gave the people a promise that the day of small things would not remain without end. Then he revealed that the history of salvation was moving toward the 'Branch,' the humble King, the Shepherd, and the pierced one; and that the rejection of the Shepherd and his sufferings would not be the end of the story, because there was an open spring for cleansing. In the end, the Lord stands as king over all the earth, and Jerusalem becomes a city of holiness. Therefore, the book of Zechariah uniquely gathers within itself the return from exile, the rebuilding of the temple, the purification of humanity, the Davidic hope, the prophecies of Christ, the Cross, the kingdom, and final holiness.

61. The great movement of the book of Zechariah

The whole book can be summarized in the following sequence:

The exile.

Then:

Return.

Then:

“Return to Me.”

Then:

Night visions.

Then:

Jerusalem.

Then:

The purification.

Then:

The 'Branch'.

Then:

'Not by might nor by power.'

Then:

Completion of the temple.

Then:

Restoration of Jerusalem.

Then:

The humble King.

Then:

The rejected Shepherd.

Then:

The thirty pieces of silver.

Then:

The pierced one.

Then:

The spring of cleansing.

Then:

The day of the Lord.

Then:

'And the Lord shall be king over all the earth.'

Then:

'Holy to the Lord.'

Thus the book moves from a broken Jerusalem to a holy Jerusalem, and from a people who returned from exile to a king who reigns over all the earth.

🔑 The sentence that summarizes the book of Zechariah

'The book of Zechariah is the book of hope that begins with a people who came out of exile and found themselves weak before a reality that did not resemble the glories of the past. Then the voice of God announces that Jerusalem had not been forgotten, that the temple would be rebuilt, and that the work would not be accomplished by human power but by the Spirit of God. Then the prophet reveals that behind the earthly rebuilding there is a greater divine purpose concerning the purification of the people and the appearance of the 'Branch,' the coming of the humble King, and the Shepherd who is rejected, struck, and pierced. Then God opens a spring for cleansing, and in the end the day of the Lord comes, when the Lord reigns over all the earth and holiness becomes the distinguishing mark of all life. Therefore, Zechariah gathers in one book the return from exile, the rebuilding of the temple, the purification of humanity, the Davidic hope, the prophecies of Christ, the Cross, the kingdom, and the final holiness. It announces that what seems small and broken in the present can be the beginning of a divine work that ends in a greater glory, because God does not leave what he has begun until he brings it to its complete purpose.'
