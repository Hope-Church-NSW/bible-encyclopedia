Certainly. We now move to the second book: the Book of Exodus, adhering to the same model used in the Book of Genesis: a coherent encyclopedic study, extended paragraphs, and historical, geographical, linguistic, and theological depth, without inventing any uncertain information.

The Second Book: The Book of Exodus
Exodus — שְׁמוֹת — Shemot
5
1. The Name of the Book and Its Meaning

The Hebrew name for the Book of Exodus is שְׁמוֹת — Shemot, meaning “Names,” and it is the word with which the book begins: “These are the names of the sons of Israel who came to Egypt with Jacob.” This designation follows the customary practice of ancient Hebrew books of naming a book after its opening words, and it reveals from the outset that the book continues directly from the end of Genesis, where Joseph died and Jacob's descendants came to be in Egypt.

As for the Greek name Ἔξοδος — Exodus, it means “Exodus” or “departure,” and it is the name that became the basis of the name used in most modern languages. The Greek name captures the central axis of the book, because the events move from slavery to freedom, from Egypt to Sinai, from a people without order to a people under covenant with God, and from God’s presence outside the scene to the presence of his glory in the midst of the community.

Therefore, Exodus is not merely a geographical departure from Egypt; the biblical exodus is an act of redemption, covenant, and dwelling. God does not bring Israel out of the land of slavery merely so that they become an independent people, but so that they become his people, worship him, live in his presence, and have God dwelling in their midst.

2. The author and the meaning of his name

Jewish and Christian tradition attributes the book of Exodus to Moses, as part of the Torah or the Pentateuch. The name Moses in Hebrew is associated with מֹשֶׁה — Mosheh, and the book of Exodus connects the name to Pharaoh’s daughter’s words: “I drew him out of the water” (Exodus 2:10). Therefore, the name in the text is tied to the story of his rescue from the waters of the Nile.

From a scholarly standpoint, there is a linguistic discussion regarding the origin of the name; some scholars see the possibility of an Egyptian origin for the name, while the Hebrew text offers an interpretation linked to rescuing the child from the water. Therefore, in an encyclopedic study, it is better to say that the biblical meaning of the name is associated with being drawn out of the water, while the origin of the name itself remains a matter of discussion, rather than presenting one possibility as though it were a settled fact.

3. The birth and death of Moses

The Bible does not provide an exact chronological date for Moses’ birth, so there is no year that can be proven conclusively. His life can be placed within the second millennium BCE, but determining the century and the absolute date is tied to the question of the dating of Exodus itself, which is one of the issues over which scholars have disagreed in antiquity and in modern times.

As for his death, the Bible gives us clear information in Deuteronomy 34:7, where it says that Moses was one hundred and twenty years old when he died. Therefore, we can establish his age at death, while remaining cautious about giving a specific calendar year for his birth or death.

The importance of this observation appears in the study of the Bible; the goal is not to fill in gaps with numbers that appear scholarly, but to distinguish between what the text says, what can be inferred, and what remains unknown.

4. The context from which the book of Exodus begins

Genesis ends with Jacob’s family in Egypt, after God used Joseph to save his family from famine. But the book of Exodus begins after a period of time had passed in which the older generation had become part of the past, and it says that a new king arose over Egypt who “did not know Joseph.”

Here the great crisis begins: the people who had come to Egypt as a small family had become a very numerous people, so Pharaoh began to see their presence as a political and demographic threat. The relationship changed from welcoming a foreign family to collective enslavement and an attempt to control the growth of the people.

From this point, the story of salvation begins.

5. Who wrote the book of Exodus?

The book was addressed to the people of Israel, but it was not merely a historical record of what happened to their ancestors. It sought to explain to Israel where they had come from, who their God was, why they were a covenant people, and how they should live before God.

The generation that came out of Egypt did not merely need to know that their ancestors had been slaves; it needed to understand that their present identity was not founded on Pharaoh, or Egypt, or military power, but on God’s covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.

Therefore, the book of Exodus links the past to the present: the promise to the fathers → Egypt’s slavery → God’s redemption → the covenant at Sinai → God’s people → the dwelling and presence of God.

6. Reason for writing the book

The primary purpose of the book of Exodus can be summarized in three words:

Redemption — covenant — presence

God sees the humiliation of his people, hears their cry, remembers his covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and then intervenes in history to bring Israel out of Egypt. But the process of salvation does not end at the Red Sea; after the exodus comes Mount Sinai, and there the people enter a structured covenant relationship with God.

Then the final part of the book presents the construction of the Tent of Meeting and the glory of the Lord dwelling there. This makes the end of the book especially significant, because the ultimate goal was not merely to change the people’s geography, but for God to dwell in the midst of his people.

7. The general division of the book

The book of Exodus can be divided into seven main sections:

First: Israel in Egypt and slavery

Exodus 1–6

Second: the ten plagues and the confrontation with Pharaoh

Exodus 7–12

Third: the exodus, the sea, and the journey to Sinai

Exodus 12–18

Fourth: the covenant and the commandments on Mount Sinai

Exodus 19–24

Fifth: instructions for the Tent of Meeting and the priesthood

Exodus 25–31

Sixth: the golden calf and the renewal of the covenant

Exodus 32–34

Seventh: the building of the dwelling and the glory of the Lord dwelling there

Exodus 35–40

This division clearly reveals the movement of the book: slavery → redemption → journey → covenant → dwelling → habitation.

8. Israel in Egypt — Exodus 1–6

The book begins with a census of Jacob’s sons who entered Egypt, but it quickly moves from family memory to the new political reality. Joseph and his brothers and that entire generation had died, but the sons of Israel were fruitful and multiplied and became very numerous, so much so that Pharaoh began to treat them as a danger to be contained.

Pharaoh begins with a policy of forced labor, and then the policy develops into an attempt to kill the male children. Here Moses appears, the child whom God preserved in a miraculous way from death, then who was raised in Pharaoh’s house before fleeing to Midian after killing the Egyptian.

But fleeing was not the end of Moses’ story; it was preparation for his calling.

9. The burning bush and the call of Moses

God appears to Moses in the wilderness through the bush that burns with fire but is not consumed, and there a new phase begins in biblical history. God does not merely tell Moses that Israel is suffering; he declares that he has seen the humiliation of his people, heard their cry, known their afflictions, and come down to save them.

Here we come to one of the most important revelations of the name of God in the Bible, when God says to Moses:

“I AM who I AM”
(Exodus 3:14).

Then the name associated with Yahweh, the God of the fathers, appears as the God who enters history to fulfill his promises.

The important theological point here is that salvation is not a human initiative on the part of Moses or Israel, but a divine initiative.

10. Moses and Aaron before Pharaoh

Moses returns to Egypt and stands before the most powerful political figure in the world known to the text, carrying the message of God:

“Let my people go, that they may worship me.”

This statement reveals the nature of the conflict; it is not merely a struggle over labor or political independence, but a struggle over who owns the people and who has the right to worship.

Pharaoh effectively says that Israel belongs to him, while God declares that Israel is his people. That is why the plagues come as divine judgments against Egypt, its gods, and its system, revealing the supremacy of Yahweh over the Nile, the land, the heavens, life, and death.

11. The ten plagues

The narrative includes ten principal plagues:

The turning of water into blood.
The frogs.
Gnats or lice, depending on the translation.
Flies.
A plague on the livestock.
Boils.
Hail.
Locusts.
Darkness.
The death of the firstborn.

The plagues are not merely a series of natural disasters, but part of the theological struggle between Yahweh and Pharaoh. The clear aim repeated throughout the narrative is that Pharaoh, Egypt, and Israel should know that Yahweh is the Lord.

Thus the plagues become a proclamation of God’s sovereignty over nature, power, the gods, life, and death.

12. Passover

Passover is one of the greatest keys to the book of Exodus, because it represents the moment of transition from slavery to the exodus. Each household takes a lamb, slaughters it, and places its blood on the two doorposts and the lintel; when the Lord sees the blood, he passes over the house and no plague of destruction is there.

Here appear a set of concepts that will later become central in Christian theology: the lamb, blood, death, passing over, salvation, and redemption. Therefore, the New Testament reads Christ in the light of the background of Passover, and Paul describes Christ as “our Passover,” who was sacrificed for us.

From here Passover becomes a theologically crucial bridge between the exodus and the New Testament.

13. Leaving Egypt and crossing the sea

After the final plague, Pharaoh permits Israel to leave, but the Egyptian army pursues them, and the people find themselves between the sea and the army. Here the exodus reaches one of its greatest moments, when God opens a path through the sea and the people pass over.

The event is not merely a miracle of rescue for a group of people, but a historical transformation: the people who were under the dominion of Pharaoh are now a people on the way to the covenant of God.

Then the sea closes over the Egyptian army, and it becomes clear that the power of Pharaoh, which seemed absolute, was nothing before the dominion of God.

14. The wilderness: the testing after deliverance

After crossing the sea, a very different stage begins. The people have left Egypt, but now they must learn to trust God in the wilderness.

The lack of water appears, then food, then manna and quail, then water from the rock, and problems of grumbling, fear, and unbelief also appear.

Here the book presents a very important spiritual principle: deliverance from slavery does not mean that the journey of faith has ended; rather, after deliverance begins the testing of trust and obedience.

15. Mount Sinai and the covenant

The people come to Mount Sinai, and here the book moves from deliverance to covenant. God says to Israel that they have seen what he did to the Egyptians, that he carried them on eagles’ wings and brought them to this place, and then he presents to them their calling as a kingdom of priests and a holy nation.

The Ten Commandments come at the heart of this covenant, but they are not merely a set of separate ethical laws; they are part of a covenant relationship between God and his people.

The order is important:

God first delivered them, and then gave them the commandment.

That is, obedience is not the price of salvation, but the response of a people whom God has saved and brought into his covenant.

16. The Ten Commandments

The Ten Commandments appear in Exodus 20 and include:

Having no other gods.
Making no idols.
Not taking the name of the Lord in vain.
Keeping the Sabbath.
Honoring father and mother.
Not murdering.
Not committing adultery.
Not stealing.
Not bearing false witness.
Not coveting what belongs to another.

The order reveals a profound moral structure, because the relationship with God comes first, followed by the relationship with other people. Scripture does not separate worship from ethics; rather, it sees that corruption in one's relationship with God will be reflected in one's relationship with others.

17. The Tabernacle

Beginning with Exodus 25, detailed instructions are given concerning the tabernacle, its furnishings, its building materials, the priests, and the service. At first glance, these chapters may seem like a sudden shift from the story of the exodus to construction details, but they actually fulfill the book's ultimate purpose.

For Exodus began with the question:

Where is God among his people?

And it ends with the answer:

God dwells in the midst of his people.

The ark, the Most Holy Place, the lampstand, the altar of incense, the altar, the priesthood, the garments, the veil, and the holy oil are all elements that work together within a system of worship that makes God's presence among the community a central reality.

18. The Golden Calf

Before the tabernacle is completed, one of the book's most serious events takes place: the making of the golden calf. The people who saw the plagues, the crossing of the sea, and the covenant at Sinai quickly turn aside to make an image and worship it.

The story reveals the depth of the human problem: people can see God's works and then make for themselves a tangible substitute for God.

Moses intervenes as a mediator and intercedes for the people, and here an important aspect of his leadership is displayed: he is not merely a military leader or a lawgiver, but a mediator who stands between God and the people.

19. Renewal of the Covenant

After the sin of the golden calf, God's relationship with Israel does not end; rather, the covenant is renewed. This is one of the book's most powerful scenes of grace, because the people quickly proved their unfaithfulness, yet God continues to deal with them on the basis of his covenant and mercy.

Here God's attributes appear in the proclamation of his name before Moses, including mercy, compassion, patience, abundant lovingkindness, and faithfulness.

Thus, the book reveals that the covenant rests not on human righteousness alone, but also on God's faithfulness and mercy.

20. Construction of the Tabernacle and the Coming of Glory

The events of the book conclude with the construction of the tabernacle according to the divine command. When the work is completed, the theological moment for which the entire book has awaited arrives: the tabernacle is filled with the glory of the Lord.

Here we see that the story of the exodus has reached its goal.

The story began with a people crying out under forced labor.

Then:

God hears.

Then:

God comes down to save.

Then:

God brings the people out.

Then:

God brings the people into the covenant.

And finally:

God dwells among them.

This is the deep structure of the entire book.

21. Main Characters
Moses

He is the central human figure, the prophet, leader, and mediator whom God uses to bring Israel out of Egypt and into the covenant.

Aaron

Moses' brother and the first high priest, he has a fundamental role in priestly ministry, but he also appears in the incident of the golden calf, showing that human leadership itself is not infallible.

Miriam

The sister of Moses and Aaron, she appears as a leader and singer after the crossing of the sea.

Pharaoh

He represents the Egyptian authority that resists the release of the people, and his confrontation with Moses becomes part of the revelation of God's sovereignty.

Joshua

He begins to appear more clearly as a military leader and will later become Moses' successor.

22. The Book's Most Important Theological Message

The central message of the Book of Exodus may be summarized as follows:

God sees his people, hears their cry, redeems them, enters into a covenant with them, and dwells among them.

This makes Exodus more than a story of political liberation.

It is a story of redemption.

But redemption itself leads to covenant.

And covenant leads to worship.

And worship leads to the divine presence.

Therefore, the book's theological trajectory may be outlined as follows:

Slavery → Redemption → Covenant → Holiness → Dwelling
23. Christ in the Book of Exodus

The Book of Exodus presents a set of images that become highly important for understanding Christ.

Passover opens the way to understanding Christ as the sacrifice with which salvation is associated.

Moses provides a model of the mediator who stands between God and the people.

The manna becomes the background for Christ's teaching about the bread of life.

The water from the rock is associated with Christ in Paul's interpretation.

The presence in the tabernacle opens the way to understanding the idea of God's dwelling among humanity, which reaches its climax in John's declaration concerning the Word who became flesh and dwelt among us.

Thus, “Christ in Exodus” does not mean that every detail must be turned into a symbol of Christ; rather, it means that the theological structure of Exodus becomes part of the background that the New Testament uses to understand Christ's work.

24. The Time of the Book's Events

The Book of Exodus is associated with the period of the Israelites' enslavement in Egypt, followed by their departure from it, their journey in the wilderness, and their arrival at Sinai. However, determining the absolute date of the exodus is disputed among scholars. The most prominent historical models are approximately the fifteenth century BC and the thirteenth century BC, with other details and models in modern studies.

Therefore, the encyclopedia will not adopt a single year as an indisputable fact; rather, it will mention the traditional or probable chronological model and then note the existence of disagreement when it affects the understanding of the book.

25. The Time of the Book's Composition

According to Jewish and Christian tradition, the Book of Exodus is associated with Moses and with the period in which Israel was formed as a covenant community in the wilderness. Modern critical studies, however, discuss the composition of the Torah as a process that extended through different stages, and schools differ in identifying the sources, editorial stages, and final date of the text.

The best approach in an encyclopedic study is not to turn this disagreement into a battle, but to present the reader with the traditional position, the academic position, and the evidence associated with each. In this way, the researcher can know where the text itself stands and where historical conclusions and theories concerning literary composition begin.

26. Why Is the Book of Exodus Central to the Bible?

Many books mention the exodus because the event became Israel's great memory of salvation. The prophets and the Psalms return to it, and Scripture uses the image of the exodus to interpret God's subsequent acts. In the New Testament, the language of Passover, the wilderness, manna, dwelling, and covenant becomes part of understanding Christ's work.

The reason is clear: Exodus presents the first complete model of the idea that God redeems an enslaved people, brings them from death to life, brings them into a covenant, and makes them his own people.

This structure appears again in the New Testament in a deeper form through Christ's death and resurrection and the formation of the new covenant community.
