Introduction to the Book of Psalms

The central idea of the book: The Book of Psalms is not merely a collection of hymns and prayers, but the book of worship and prayer that places before humans every condition of life in God's presence: joy and sorrow, fear and hope, falling and repentance, war and peace, the feeling of abandonment and confidence in divine presence. And at the heart of the Psalms, humans meet God, the king meets the people, worship meets covenant, and hope meets the Messiah; therefore, the Book of Psalms has remained the primary book of prayer and praise for God's people throughout the ages.

5
1. Name of the book and its meaning

The Arabic name Psalms is derived from the word psalm, which is connected to the Arabic verb "zamara," meaning to sing or play the reed pipe, and it is close to the Hebrew concept used in the title of the book.

As for the Hebrew name, it is תְּהִלִּים — Tehillim, and its meaning is "praises" or "hymns."

And this Hebrew name is very important; because the book at its core is not merely a collection of poems, but a book of praise directed to God.

And in the Greek translation, the book was called Psalmoi — ψαλμοί, a word connected to songs accompanied by playing a stringed instrument, from which the English term Psalms is derived.

So we have three dimensions to the name:

Psalms → singing and playing.

Tehillim → praises.

Psalms → hymns accompanied by music.

And these three dimensions reflect the nature of the book as a book of prayer, praise, music, and worship.

2. The Author

There is not one author of the Book of Psalms.

And this is a very fundamental point in understanding it.

The book is a collection of psalms written by a number of individuals over different historical periods, which were then gathered into one collection.

And among the most prominent names associated with the Psalms:

David.

Asaph.

The sons of Korah.

Moses.

Heman.

Ethan.

There are also psalms that do not have the name of a specific author.

And the greater number of psalms with titles bearing his name are attributed to David, and therefore the book became strongly associated with him, so much so that it is sometimes called the Psalms of David, but this does not mean that David wrote the entire book.

3. David and the meaning of his name

The Hebrew name of David is:

דָּוִד — Dāwîḏ

And it is connected in Hebrew to the meaning "the beloved" or "the beloved one."

And David is a central figure in the Book of Psalms.

For he is:

A shepherd.

A warrior.

King.

Psalmist.

Poet.

A man who experienced pain, fall, repentance, and hope.

This background explains why the Psalms became so strongly associated with his spiritual voice.

4. The Year of David's Birth and Death

The Bible does not give a direct date for David's birth.

But the sequence of kings and events allows his reign to be dated approximately to the tenth century BC.

Many scholars estimate that David's reign was approximately between 1010 and 970 BC, with some differences in the dating details.

It is mentioned that David died at approximately seventy years of age according to the chronology given in 2 Samuel 5:4–5, as he was thirty years old when he became king and reigned forty years.

5. The Time of Writing the Book of Psalms

Here a distinction must be made between:

The time of writing the individual psalms.

and

The time of compiling the book into its final form.

The Psalms were not all written in a single era.

Some go back to the era of David, that is, the tenth century BC.

Some are linked to the period of the temple and worship in Jerusalem.

Some reflect post-exilic experiences.

It is clear that the final collection underwent a process of compilation and organization spanning centuries.

Therefore, it can be said that the Psalms represent a long history of Israelite worship extending from the time of Moses to the post-exilic period.

6. For Whom Was the Book Written?

The Book of Psalms was written primarily for the community of God's people in worship.

It is not an academic book directed only at scholars.

Rather, it was used in:

Public worship.

Prayers.

Feasts.

The temple.

Pilgrimage processions to Jerusalem.

Individual and communal praise.

Therefore, a psalm could be the prayer of a single person, then afterward become the prayer of an entire community.

7. Reasons for Writing the Psalms

The purposes of the book can be summarized in a set of themes:

Praising God.

Prayer before God.

Expressing pain.

Confessing sin.

Seeking protection.

Remembering God's works.

Glorifying the divine kingdom.

Declaring hope in the Messiah.

Teaching the people wisdom.

Using music in worship.

Thus the Book of Psalms becomes a book in which a person can find language to speak to God in every situation they go through.

8. The Division of the Book of Psalms

The book consists of 150 psalms.

Its traditional division is five books:

Book One

Psalm 1–41

Book Two

Psalm 42–72

Book Three

Psalm 73–89

Book Four

Psalm 90–106

Book Five

Psalm 107–150

This fivefold division is not arbitrary, but resembles, in a literary and traditional way, the division of the five Books of Moses.

9. Why Five Books?

At the end of each section there is a closing formula of praise or blessing.

This gives the book an intentional structure.

Some scholars believe that this arrangement links praise to the covenant and the law, so that the Psalms become a kind of worshipful response to God's work and word.

Thus the book begins with the first psalm:

"Blessed is the man who has not walked in the counsel of the wicked..."

And the book ends in Psalm 150 with an ascending series of:

"Praise Him."

The book begins with man before two paths.

And ends with all of creation in a scene of praise.

10. The First Psalm — The Gateway of the Book

The first psalm is not merely a beautiful psalm.

It resembles the gateway into the entire Book of Psalms.

It places before man two paths:

The way of the righteous.

and

The way of the wicked.

Then it likens the righteous one to a tree planted by streams of water.

Here a fundamental idea appears that will be repeated throughout the book:

True life is connected to its source, and the source of the believer's life is God and His word.

11. The Second Psalm — The King and the Messiah

After the first psalm comes the second psalm.

Here the focus shifts from man to:

The kingdom.

And the phrase appears:

"You are My Son, today I have begotten You."

The New Testament read this psalm with a clear messianic reading.

It reveals therein the conflict of the nations against the Lord's Messiah, and then the proclamation of the dominion of the King whom God raised up.

For this reason, the Second Psalm represents one of the most important entrances into the theology of the King and the Messiah in the Book of Psalms.

12. The Psalms as a mirror of humanity

One of the most important characteristics of the Psalms is that they do not hide human emotions.

In them:

Fear.

Anger.

Tears.

Joy.

Jealousy.

Remorse.

Confusion.

Trust.

Hope.

For this reason, the reader is able to find himself in the Psalms.

Thus, the Book does not say that the believer does not experience pain.

Rather, it teaches the believer how to bring his pain to God.

13. Lament Psalms

A great many Psalms begin with lament.

Such as:

"How long, O Lord?"

This question is repeated in various forms.

But the Psalm often does not end at the question.

It moves from:

lament

To:

remembrance

Then:

trust

Then:

Praise.

This is one of the most important spiritual patterns in the Book.

14. Psalms of praise

There are entire Psalms devoted to praise.

And in them appear actions such as:

Praise.

Give thanks.

Shout.

Sing joyfully.

Bless.

And in Psalm 150, praise reaches its climax.

Praise is no longer tied to a single instrument or a particular group.

But rather:

"Let every breath praise the Lord."

Thus praise becomes a call that includes every living creature.

15. Psalms of repentance

Among the best known are:

Psalm 51.

It is connected with David's fall into sin involving Bathsheba and Uriah.

Here we see genuine repentance.

David does not try to justify himself.

Rather, he asks for:

"Create in me a pure heart, O God."

This is very important.

For repentance in the Psalms is not merely the feeling of guilt.

Rather, it is a request for a change of heart.

16. Psalm 23

Among the best known Psalms of Scripture:

"The Lord is my shepherd; I shall not want."

And David uses the image of the shepherd to explain God's relationship to His people.

The Lord:

leads.

waters.

restores the soul.

guides.

protects.

And is present with the human being in the valley of the shadow of death.

In Christian reading, this Psalm becomes especially deep when Christ declares Himself in John 10 to be the Good Shepherd.

17. Psalm 22

The Psalm begins with a loud cry:

"My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?"

And it is one of the most important Christian Psalms.

And the New Testament uses it directly in the context of the sufferings of Christ.

And in it appear images associated with pain and contempt and the division of garments, which made it one of the most important texts read in the light of the cross.

Thus the pain of David in the Psalm becomes a text carrying profound prophetic and messianic dimensions.

18. Psalm 16

The Psalm offers a strong hope in not leaving the soul in Sheol.

And Peter uses it in the Book of Acts in interpreting the resurrection of Christ.

Thus some of David's Psalms become an essential part of the way the apostles understood the resurrection of Christ from the dead.

19. Psalm 110

He says:

"The Lord said to my Lord: Sit at my right hand until I place your enemies as a footstool for your feet."

It is the most frequently quoted Psalm in the New Testament, or one of the most frequently used in interpreting the identity of Christ.

Christ Himself used it in the dialogue concerning the identity of the Messiah.

And the New Testament links it to the kingdom, glory, authority, and priesthood of Christ.

20. Psalm 118

This Psalm is one of the most important Psalms of thanksgiving.

And among its best-known expressions are:

"The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone."

And the New Testament uses this image in speaking about Christ.

And some expressions of the Psalm are also associated with the events of Christ's entry into Jerusalem.

Thus the Psalm passes from Israelite worship to a clear messianic reading in the New Testament.

21. Psalms of the King

There is a group of psalms associated with the king.

In them we find themes such as:

The throne.

The kingdom.

Victory.

Justice.

God's choosing of the king.

These psalms are very important for understanding the development of the concept of:

The Davidic king.

This line then becomes a foundation for understanding the messianic hope.

22. The Psalms Called "Songs of Ascents"

From Psalm 120 to 134 there is a group known as:

Psalms of Ascents / Songs of Degrees.

This title is often associated with the journey of ascent to Jerusalem during the seasons of worship.

These psalms thus become the voice of the pilgrims as they head toward the city of God.

This gives them a geographical and spiritual dimension:

The road to Jerusalem becomes a road of worship.

23. Wisdom Psalms

There are psalms that bear a wisdom character.

They speak of:

The righteous man.

The wicked.

Wisdom.

The fear of God.

The Word of God.

Among the clearest examples:

Psalm 1.

Psalm 37.

Psalm 73.

Here the psalms meet the wisdom literature found in Proverbs, Job, and Ecclesiastes.

24. Psalm 73

This psalm addresses a very profound question:

Why do the wicked succeed while the righteous suffer?

This question is very close to the problem of the Book of Job.

But the turning point comes when the psalmist enters God's sanctuary.

There his perspective changes.

Thus he does not find the solution merely by analyzing circumstances.

But rather in restoring the correct vision of God and eternity.

25. Psalm 90

Psalm 90 is very distinctive because it is attributed to Moses.

It links the psalms to an era much older than David.

It meditates on:

The eternity of God.

The brevity of human life.

Sin.

Time.

Wisdom.

Thus it places the voice of Moses within a book that later became Israel's book of praise.

26. Asaph and the Sons of Korah

David was not the only one who wrote the psalms.

Asaph was one of the important figures associated with music and worship in David's time.

A group of psalms is also associated with:

The Sons of Korah.

This reveals that the Book of Psalms is not the product of a single poet alone.

But rather the fruit of a communal life of worship.

27. The Temple and the Psalms

The temple was an important center for Israelite worship.

For this reason we find the psalms full of references to:

The sanctuary.

The temple.

The altar.

The gates.

Jerusalem.

Zion.

This makes sacred geography part of the language of the psalms.

28. Zion

Zion is not merely a mountain.

In the psalms it became a symbol of the place of God's presence, kingship, and worship.

From here appear:

The city of God.

The mountain of the Lord.

The dwelling place of God.

The kingdom of God.

Jerusalem in the psalms is transformed into a spiritual center of experience and hope.

29. The Psalms and the Kingdom

One of the great themes of the book:

The reign of the LORD.

This appears especially in psalms such as:

93.

96.

97.

98.

99.

The idea is that God is not merely the local God of Israel.

But rather:

The Lord is king over all the earth.

This gives the psalms a cosmic vision.

30. The Psalms and Creation

The book is not limited to man.

Rather it speaks of:

The heavens.

The land.

The sea.

The mountains.

The trees.

Animals.

The sun.

The moon.

The stars.

And creation itself is called to proclaim the glory of God.

And this reaches its pinnacle in Psalm 148.

31. Psalms and War

Because many of the psalms emerged from the context of kingship and wars, we find in them military language:

Enemies.

The sword.

Fortresses.

The army.

Pursuit.

But the Christian reading does not use these texts to justify personal vengeance.

Rather, it understands them within the context of the Old Testament, and then reads them in light of Christ's teaching on loving enemies.

32. Psalms and Humanity

Psalm 8 asks:

For who is man that you remember him?

Then it places humanity in the context of creation.

And the New Testament uses this psalm in speaking about Christ, humanity, and dominion over creation.

And here we see that the psalms bring together:

The majesty of God.

and

The dignity of humanity.

and

Humanity's need for God.

33. Psalms and Christ

One cannot study the Book of Psalms with a deep Christian reading without studying the messianic psalms.

For the New Testament uses the psalms extensively in interpreting Christ.

Among the most prominent are:

Psalm 2 — The King and the Messiah.

Psalm 16 — The Resurrection.

Psalm 22 — The Suffering.

Psalm 45 — The King.

Psalm 69 — Pain and Rejection.

Psalm 72 — The King and the Kingdom.

Psalm 89 — The Covenant with David.

Psalm 110 — The King and the Priest.

Psalm 118 — The Rejected Stone.

34. Christ in Psalm 110

Psalm 110 is very important because it brings together two functions:

The kingdom.

and

The Priest.

And it appears in it:

You are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek.

And the New Testament, especially the Book of Hebrews, builds on this text a deep understanding of the priesthood of Christ.

And thus the psalm connects kingship and priesthood in the person of Christ.

35. Psalms and the Cross

Psalm 22 is one of the strongest examples.

It begins with the voice of the one who suffers.

Then it gradually transforms toward a declaration of confidence.

And when we read it alongside the accounts of the cross in the Gospels, we find that the New Testament uses some of its images and expressions in interpreting the suffering of Christ.

And thus the cross in the Christian reading becomes not merely a tragic event.

Rather, it is part of a prior scriptural line that announced the suffering of the Messiah, his hope, and his victory.

36. Psalms and the Resurrection

Psalm 16 played a foundational role in the preaching of the early church.

For in the Book of Acts, Peter uses the psalm in announcing that David was not speaking about himself alone in the end, because David's tomb is known, whereas Christ rose from the dead.

And thus the psalms became part of the apostles' proof of the resurrection of Christ.

37. Psalms and Christian Worship

The Book of Psalms continued in the early church.

And the New Testament exhorts believers to use:

Psalms.

Hymns.

Spiritual songs.

And thus the Book of Psalms transitioned from the Jewish temple to the life of the church, with its texts read in light of the work of Christ.

38. What Does the Book of Psalms Offer to Humanity?

The Psalms teach a person that:

He prays when he fears.

He gives thanks when he rejoices.

He repents when he falls.

He waits when the answer is delayed.

He praises when he sees the work of God.

He cries out when he suffers.

He trusts when he does not understand.

And this is one of the reasons for the continuation of this book in the lives of believers until today.

39. The Most Important Message in the Book of Psalms

The message can be summarized as:

Every condition of humanity can be transformed into prayer when placed before God, and every true path begins with trust and ends with praise.

For the Psalms do not deny reality.

Rather, they take reality with all its complexities and place it before God.

40. What Do We Learn from the Book of Psalms?

That worship is not connected only to joy.

That a person can weep before God.

That acknowledging weakness is not spiritual weakness.

That repentance begins by asking for a new heart.

That the word of God is the source of steadfastness.

That God is King even when evil appears victorious.

That God is shepherd to humanity in the valley as in the green pastures.

That messianic hope advances through the line of the Davidic king.

Christ is the key to the final reading of the Psalms in the New Testament.

🔑 The sentence that summarizes the Book of Psalms

The Book of Psalms is the voice of humanity before God in every circumstance of life; there the fearful weep, the sinner repents, the afflicted cries out, the redeemed rejoices, the King sings praise, and the people await the coming of the Messiah; therefore the book begins with humanity before two ways, and it ends with every breath praising the Lord.
